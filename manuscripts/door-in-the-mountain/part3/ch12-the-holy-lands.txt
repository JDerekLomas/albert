CHAPTER 12
The Holy Lands

I had reached the holy lands. Things had gone well in Guatemala, China, and Mongolia, and now National Geographic wanted me as their explorer of the ancient world at large. I was to host my very own series, Lost Cities.

My whole life had been a single big bet, from the time I had told my mom I was going to buy a horse to find the nomads of Mongolia till now. I had been testing audacity time and time again and it had worked. And everything had been pointing to this. There was happy shock and a heavy dose of gratitude that it had actually turned into something real and lasting. The dream had come true.

I checked with the kids and Kara, and we made a complex jigsaw of a calendar for the year to still be able to co-parent equally. Then I packed my bags and the journey began.

I was connected to an executive producer named Lucy Van Beak who had been assembling a team of researchers to find the mysteries where new technologies could contribute real discovery. The idea was that by leveraging film budgets we could bring the cutting edge to revealing the past, and the sky was the limit, beyond resources common to archaeology.

I had come to the ancient port city of Acre on the coast of Israel to meet the wider crew for the first time.

We gathered in a small hotel lobby. The director made introductions. Joe Steel was an engineer, a clean-cut, soft-spoken Englishman in his early thirties who had built his own company mapping the world for Hollywood. He’d come with laptops and espresso instead of trowels, and he wore the same easy smile no matter how impossible the timeline or wild the idea. We brainstormed late into the night.

The next morning Joe handed me an iPad. He had coded a prototype of our imaginations as I slept, an augmented reality window into the tunnels beneath the city using some tunnel scans he had done the day before. I held it up against the modern street and watched a thousand-year-old hidden Templar passageway appear on the screen, running underneath the cobblestones in front of me.

The cold sea air of the Mediterranean lifted between the stones. A call to prayer rose from a minaret somewhere to the east. From a few blocks west a church bell answered.

There had once been a Crusader stronghold here, the last stronghold to fall in the Holy Land. Beneath the streets ran tunnels where the Knights Templar had prepared for sieges, where alliances with Mongols had been whispered against the advance of the Mamluks. Above those vaults were Ottoman stone houses. Above those, present-day cafés and markets. History was strata underfoot.

Hours later, with cameras up, we were crawling into the tunnels. It was exciting, the dynamics of the team, the access we had into the layers of the city.

Between scenes I caught myself watching the streets the way I had watched the ghats of Varanasi, they were both places of pilgrimage.

---

Over the years the expeditions in this region would pull me through the eastern Mediterranean and beyond.

In Jordan I stood in front of four thousand year old petroglyphs with Bedouin archaeologists who were crowdsourcing a catalogue of the many petroglyphs that covered the Martian-like red desert walls of Wadi Rum with the smart phones in everyones pocket. There in the light of the most beautiful desert sunset I have ever seen I was shown images of ancient caravans, camels carved into the rock had ben waypoints for travelers long ago. When I asked which was their favorite glyph I was shown the etched graphite from a man expressing his pain in the wake of a broken heart.

“See, we are the same! Nothing has changed, same things that mattered then matter now, hahahah” the Bedouin laughed. “These walls are our giant library.”

Further along I was shown the engineering marvel of Petra’s catchments by Jordan’s Princess Dana Firas. The entire city carved into the cliffs of red rock was as enigmatic as they had been when I first saw them as a kid watching Indiana Jones. But now my team mapped the city with our heavy lifting lidar drones to reveal intricate carved pools on top of every roof, irrigation channels tracing their way down the facades into huge cisterns that still contained water.

“Petra had once been covered in stucco, painted with brilliant colors,” Her Highness told me. “And lush with plants and running fountains.” I had her record a message to millie, one princess to another.

I traveled the Nile from Egypt into Sudan. From Karnak at sunset, to the flooded tombs of the Black Pharohs of Kush. Pyramids all the way down the nile.

“I swam into mummy water,” I told my kids when I returned home.

“Go shower dad!” was their unified response.

On the seafloor off the Israeli coast we used side-scan sonar to search for Canaanite shipwrecks. Their beads traced a network of people who had survived the megadrought four thousand two hundred years ago by throwing huge gatherings with vats of wine and inviting the whole sea to trade with them.  Hundreds of hurried ceramic containers of wine told of one hell of a party that had been thrown in the face of catastrophe.

Up on the mountain-top forts Jewish rebels held against the Romans, the stones still holding the silhouette of the siege.

And under Acre, the Crusader tunnels. These hidden escape routes had been dug in secrecy by knights like the ones my mother had taken me to make wax rubbings in the churches of Cambridge with as a kid.

It was a place of consequences. Conflicts and war.  An epic landscape of rugged wonder.

People had been fighting over this stretch of ground for five thousand years. Different names. Rising and falling. Yet they kept coming back.

Maybe it’s because the ground had its own story.

---

Another series, investigating the events described in the old testament that we might find against the landscape. Collective memories that sparked the three major monotheistic religions of the world.

I stood at the edge of the Dead Sea, the lowest point on the surface of the earth, a place where the mantle itself is splitting open. Beneath the brine ran a vast salt cap, and from somewhere deeper still, black asphalt would bubble up through it and rise to the surface in lumps the size of cattle. Ancient fishermen pulled them from the water and traded them in cities like Petra, where the Nabataeans used the sticky tar substance for waterproofing and embalming. The Romans called this place Lacus Asphaltites. The Sea of Asphalt.

I walked the shoreline above what some archaeologists now call the most likely site of Sodom and Gomorrah, Tall el-Hammam in the Jordan Valley. The aerial scans we made there had picked up something that had only been theorized, the radial signature of a massive impact. The archaeologists had observed stone melted into glass and concluded it was destroyed around 1650 BC by a cosmic airburst, a meteor that exploded in the air with a thousand times the energy of the Hiroshima bomb. Not everyone accepts it. But I had walked that ground and seen the stones that had been melted to glass with my own eyes. The area around the Dead Sea stayed uninhabited for six hundred years after. The Bible tells of Sodom consumed by fire from above. Perhaps the story was a memory of something like this.

A few thousand kilometers to the west, the volcanic Greek island of Santorini blew its top around the same century. Standing on the rim of the caldera, amongst the throngs of tourists and the white buildings with their blue doors, it seemed impossible to imagine an eruption that some scholars think had released the energy of hundreds of thousands of atomic bombs. Pyroclastic flows down the cliffs. Tsunamis reaching the coast of Egypt within hours, their waves drawing the water from the coastline before crashing back in. The bloody Nile, the darkness over the land described in the Old Testament, could it have been the ash and red bloom of Santorini drifting across the Mediterranean?

And further north, at the end of the last ice age, around seventy-five hundred years ago, as sea levels rose in uneven pockets, the Mediterranean had likely broken through the Bosphorus and poured into what had been a freshwater lake. The Black Sea likely filled by tens of meters in the span of just a few years, swallowing an area the size of Florida and every potential human settlement on its shore. Was this the seed of the flood story that ran from Gilgamesh through Genesis through the Qur’an? A planet-scale event that would have been encoded in the memory of every culture that took in the survivors.

These events were not allegory. They had happened to real people in real places. Whatever the stories became across the millennia, they began as humans trying to find words large enough for what the earth and the sky had done to them.

But knowing the mechanism didn’t dispel the magic.

My father had spent his life mapping the physics of the universe and still couldn’t say what had happened before the first instant. My mother played a Chopin nocturne that would draw out emotions within me without being able to tell me why.

We could name the moving parts. But that couldn’t explain away the cosmic mystery of what each one of those experiences was. The fact that we are alive at all, on this bubbling, breathing, constantly changing rock in a universe of matter that survived by the tiniest of margins, is a miracle.

I was walking through geologies of wonder. The Holy Lands were one. Religions had been born from places where humans grappled with the violence and awe of nature. Where the cosmos and the source of it had been speaking loudly for longer than anyone could remember.

I would see the pattern again many years later, in Iceland. I stood over an active volcano with a man named Thor whose childhood village of Grindavík had been destroyed. We picked up volcanic stones and tossed them like feathers, pumice so light it practically floated on the wind.

“Can you imagine it?” he said. “It destroyed my village. But it is Mother Nature, and it is beautiful too.”

The glaciers moaned in the distance. The sky danced with auroras. The Icelandic manuscripts I’d been shown held a world tree binding nine realms, three Norns spinning fate at its roots, a Ragnarok that returned everything to fire and ice in every age. From those texts came the world of elves and trolls and giants that would later seed Tolkien and Marvel.

And one my mother took me to as a kid. Stonehenge, the five-thousand-year-old site everyone has seen and no one fully understands. Less than a mile and a half away lies a forgotten spring far older than the stones, and we went to find it. Its water maintaining the same mild temperature all year, a lifeline through the frost of winter.

That would have drawn mesolithic travelers for thousands of years before the first stone went up.  But it also had a rare algae that stains the flint that lies in the pool, turning a bright pink when oxidized in the air in front of your eyes.  Imagine a spring of magic color changing rocks that gives you water when everything else has frozen over. If I was a traveler eight thousand years ago I could see myself wanting to build a giant stone monument to honor the changing of the seasons around it.

Landscapes of wonder were the birthplace of origin stories, places where nature revealed its magic and left us to explain it.

The Holy Lands were everywhere.

---

After each journey I came home and found the same forces at work in my own house. Watching two kids become themselves was as astonishing as anything I had stood in front of.

Charlie was nine now and Millie seven, and they seemed to be growing faster each day.

The plan we had worked out ment I would be gone for a few weeks at a time and then come back and immediately drop into dad mode for an equal amount of time.

From the road I had sent them messages from wherever I was: a helicopter over Greek islands, a tunnel in Acre.

Charlie would reply with questions about specifics. What were color on the ancient walls, millie would ask if I had seen the new notes she had left me in my luggage. I would point to the yarn spun neon pink protection bracelet that had been on my wrist throughout the shot for the world to see and give her a long hung. Then I’d put a trinket in each of their hands, a bracelet from a real princess or bead from some far-off land and tell them the stories that I had brought back.

Now it was time to seize the days.

“Carpe Diem,” I’d say with the same gusto every morning, until they rolled their eyes. School breaks we’d head into the desert, the coast, the mountains.

One summer we found a stretch of river deep in the Inyo National Forest, where the ridges of the high Sierra fold into one another for a hundred miles. Freezing snow-fed water ran alongside natural hot springs that had cut shallow muddy jacuzzis into the bank.

We took turns plunging into the snowmelt, then crawling into the hot water. Millie made face sculptures from the steaming mud while Charlie and I challenged each other to see who could stay in the cold longer. He usually won.

“Why is it the water hot if there’s snow everywhere?” He’d ask.

I told them there was magma below us heating the spring before it surfaced. Millie watched the steam come off the mud. Then they wandered through the meadow above ten thousand feet, playing in the grassy tundra while chasing tiny frogs with yeps of joy each time they caught one.

When I was a boy myself and an earthquake had shaken us till we were forced to retreat into the canyon behind our home, my father did his best to explain the geophysics of tectonic plates by pointing out the sinusoidal pattern that were left in the shelf of National Geographic magazines in our house. I was still certain we were lying on the belly of a giant.

Every culture has a giant.

As I watched in awe of my growing children, playing along the banks of a steaming river pouring down from the shoulders of giants made from granite that pushed it way up through the earth’s crust. The magic of the moment was bigger than the science that could describe it.

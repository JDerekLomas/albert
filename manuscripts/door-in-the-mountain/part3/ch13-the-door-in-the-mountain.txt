CHAPTER 13
The Door in the Mountain

From jungles, to rivers, to deserts, it was now time for the high mountains. Peru.

Peru had haunted me for years. It’s where Gil had died.  There was also Johan Reinhard’s photograph of an Inca girl preserved in ice, her face still composed after five centuries on the mountain.  And I had learned that when my great grandfather immigrated to North America he had had a brother who got on another boat and headed for Peru. No idea what had happened to him.

Lost Cities was in full swing and my team was now moving about the world like a motley band, only instead of instruments we wielded cameras, sound recording gear, LiDAR drones and whatever ropes or rafts we needed to get to the unknown. Each expedition seemed to be moving me further into the search for the source of the world views that I was seeing everywhere.  We started looking deeper into the past of the ancients themselves.

This time the trail was leading towards the forgotten story of the Wari, the people who came before the Inca.

The Inca were iconic of course. As a boy I had been seen images of Machu Picchu in the National Geographic magazines that lined my parents shelf, the mountain top ceremonial site with it’s magically intricate stonework seemed built as a symbolic connection to the land, placed geographically in the ecological nexus where the Andes break into cloud forest and fall away toward the Amazon. The bend of the Urubamba river wraps around it on three sides and a stone “hitching post” at the top of the site seemed carved to catch the sun. I had often tried to imagined the rituals that had taken place there. What did this way of seeing percolate up from?

Across the Andes people held that they had not come from the land but were *of* it. Every lineage had its own place of emergence, a spring or a cave or a mountain that had given them up the way a river gives up a fish, and in the oldest telling is that first people were carved out of stone and sent underground to come out at their appointed places.

And the more I dug the more I realized I had only seen the surface, there was a long continuum of cultural experiments that had preceded even the Wari, far before the Inca. Archaeologists had identified seven periods before the Inca ever arrived, each one its own people with its own way of life. The oldest of them was building pyramids on the coast five thousand years ago, at the same time as Egypt and with no knowledge of it. The builders of Machu Pichu were only the last ones standing when the Spanish arrived, but Peru had been a cradle of cultural evolution for thousands of years.

And there was one plant that seemed to run through all of it. San Pedro pollen lies in the deepest layer of a cave to the north, used ten thousand years ago, alongside the first beans anyone in South America ever planted. By the time the first temples went up it was already on their walls. At Chavin, three thousand years ago, a human figure holding a stalk of plant is set at the heart of the temple. The galleries behind him were cut to carry sound, and twenty conch trumpets were found inside, hauled up from the sea.  These rooms were built with acoustic intentionally to be played as instruments of resonance for the people sitting in the dark with this cactus running through them. The cultures that rose out of this ground had done so with a ritualized connection to the same plant.

With our porters, we loaded the mules and began the climb into the Andes. Andaas was the name of our Peruvian archaeologist, a soft-spoken yet passionate guardian of his heritage. One of the porters was an unassuming man maybe twenty-five years older than me. His face was lined the way the mountains were lined. The others called him Elder.

One morning we passed a circular stone dwelling with an opening facing the tallest peak. Human bones poked up through the cold dirt, maybe a femur. “They are not really dead,” Andaas said. “The dwellings are where the living come to speak with them, and to eat with them.”

Elder pulled a pouch of coca leaves from inside his coat. He held three leaves up to the sky, blew on them, and scattered them into the dwelling. The other porters quieted as he did, then repeated his actions at a distance. Now I understood he was not only a porter but also a *paqo,* a shaman of these mountains.

The expedition went on. We pushed higher. Sixteen thousand feet, then seventeen. The thin air could be felt by everyone on the team. Then we sent up the LiDAR drones, which had been equipped with high-altitude propeller blades to account for the lack of lift in those conditions. It passed back and forth over a the tall grass that covered a ridge of a Wari archaeological site, attempting to reveal what time had hidden with vegetation.

By the middle of the week we had come back down to a small hacienda near the Sacred Valley. A rest day to charge ourselves and all the equipment. Llamas hung around the lanes outside, smelling of sheep and horse and wet dog when the afternoon rain came through. While the team unpacked I quietly asked Elder if he would show me how he understood the deeper spirit of the land. Not for the cameras.

He agreed.

The next morning we slipped out of town with an apprentice he had asked to join.

He drove me through the sacred valley till we came to a side of a mountain with an obscure Inca site rising along its shoulders. A cave about half way up.
At the foot of the trail he handed each of us something to carry, a small circular hand drum, a guitar, a long white horn that appeared to be made of wood or paper mache wound into the shape of a figure eight.  Elder wore a felt conical hat with bright tasseled scarves looped around his neck. Against his quiet way of moving, and nearly absent use of words, the colors called out loudly. He and his assistant both shouldered bright woven blankets which seemed to make the mountains happier.

The path opened into a grand staircase rising like an amphitheater hidden in a fold of the valley. The steps had been worn smooth by centuries of feet, but now it was just us. The altitude made the light feel crisper. Elder climbed ahead of us playing his flute.

At the top, a vast opening looking over crumbled ruins descending below and the green of a mountain rising on the opposing valley wall. Elder led us to the back of the cave. There, carved into the heart of the rock, was a large stone doorway. It did not open into anything, just stone against stone.

“A doorway to the ancestors,” Elder said, half in Spanish and half in Quechua, and I caught enough of it. Out of his bag emerged a small water bottle filled with the San Pedro that he had distilled that morning. I had watched his elderly mother reduce the cactus skins in a simmering pot at his family home before we had left town.  He placed it on a small stone alter that sat at the center of the cave entrance.

Years earlier, the ethnobotanist Wade Davis had placed a stalk of this cactus into my hands following our meeting at a National Geographic event. He called it a *Grandfather plant* to the *Grandmother plant* Ayahuasca of the jungle. Of all the keys to the mind he had encountered as an scientist, he said, “this was a special one.” But at that time it had only been a plant with a story.

Now I was sitting in the back of an ancient ceremonial cave in front of an Inca-built stone door into the mountain with a small bottle of it collected from its native soil sitting on an altar.

Elder handed me a cup . I thought of the last time I had seen through the lens of plants, in the dunes of Death Valley to treat my phantom pain. And I thought of my great grandfather’s brother, stepping off a boat into this country a hundred years ago. Had he walked under these mountains?

Succumb. I drank.

The flute sang. The same melody Elder had been playing as he walked on the way up. Repeating it in a never ending stream.

He tried to explain. What I understood was that the door reached back through to the ancestors, seven layers of cultures that had emerged through land, and the song had emerged through them, this was a portal between past, present and future.  He said the plant was a way to be with *Pachamama.*

I learned later that in Quechua, *pacha *means world and space and time and era, all of it in one word, a language that never split when from where. Pachamama Not Earth Mother. Earth-and-Time Mother.

A stinging acrid taste, the San Pedro spreading downward, like I was being drawn into the earth rather than lifted out of it. The world did not melt into visions the way the psilocybin had in Death Valley. It clarified. The stone grew more present. I could feel the weight of the mountain above me and the roots of everything below, and the boundary between my body and the ground beneath seemed to fade. The earth felt present and alive, with something close to compassion. My heart opened to it. Nothing appeared that had not been there before. What changed was how much of it I could see. Every place I had been, people had found some way to do this, a plant or a drum or a fire or days without food, and each one had been shaped by whatever the land around them offered. Different lenses, ground from whatever was to hand. This one had been ground from a cactus that grew out of these mountains, and what it showed me was the mountains.

I picked up the hand drum and began a slow steady pulse. The apprentice took the guitar from his shoulder and started a quiet chord pattern beneath Elder’s flute. The three of us sat in front of the door and held the song between us. The melody kept circling. Then I knew it. Filtered through time, echoing in local folk songs, eventually emerging as *El Cóndor Pasa* on my parents’ old vinyl records. It was the song of the Andes, a sound that had literally resonated around the globe with many having felt its pull unknowing of its source.

We came down from the cave as the sun dropped. The apprentice walked along the path using a small hand saw to collect eucalyptus branches, an armful that grew until it was a small forest tied to the roof of their tiny hatchback. We drove back to Elder’s family home. The trees in the garden were hung with small things in random places. CDs on fishing string turning in the air to act as spinning mirrors, wooden carvings his grandchildren had made, old wooden horns curled around the branches.

In the courtyard he wove the eucalyptus branches into a low dome over a flattened patch of earth, a depression scooped in the middle for hot rocks. A fire heated them while we sat. The dark night emerged and the stars filled the sky.

The stones were glowing red when Elder set them in the depression. I stripped to my briefs and crawled into the small space leaving my prosthetic at the door, a thatched thicket of eucalyptus. He poured water gently over the stones and sent steam erupting into the dome, humming his ancient song. The smell of eucalyptus filled everything. Sweat came before I knew it had started.

As the heat rose my body began to dissolve into it with a sense that I was in the heart of a jungle melting into the green. I felt a deep connection to a part of my humanity that was not separate from the earth but a part of it. The feeling transcended any cultural divisions of the world. A universal truth.

From somewhere down the valley a train began to call. Haunting, long, slow notes drawn through the canyon walls in a delayed reverb that did not fully end before it began again.

As the grandfather plant ran through me and the heat mounted the portal had been opened. I lay in the embrace of Mother Earth as the barriers of time seemed to collapse, inside a womb of steaming leaves while a train horn howled like the trumpet of a conch shell across a mountain range.

----

Not too long after that my father’s world called. Friends of his old stopping grounds in Cambridge, had asked me to speak on enigmas, the mysteries that still had no answer for what was called the Darwin Lectures, an intimidating stage.

I was filled with anxiety. I had always loved public speaking, but this was different. Cambridge held two of my deepest wounds in one place.

These was my father’s world, one where I had been a boy wondered if would ever be good enough. And it was the city where the first experience in prejudice seeded an insecurity of outsiderness through the words of my then best friend. Ever since, I had been searching for answers, to salve that feeling. I had searched for what lay beneath everything to try and find what was universally human that I belonged to?  I had crossed the world and gone back through time looking for it. Now I was returning to where the wounds began, to say what I had found in an audience that made me feel l like I was still a boy in my father’s shadow.

I spent months juggling school pickups with expeditions trying to think it through, bouncing ideas off of Derek about cultural evolution, imagination, the roots of diversity, rituals, plants. More than anything he was helping me feel like I wasn’t facing it alone.

The day before the talk my the university hosts had put me up in a tiny room that felt like a monk’s cell, a small old bed, a simple desk against the window. I sat at the desk preparing the talk and watched the long wooden boats glide past, punters pushing them along as they had for hundreds of years.

It was in that same city my mother had knelt me on the stone with a sheet of paper over a medieval knight, and shown me how to bring a face seven hundred years dead up through the paper with a stick of wax.

The morning of the talk I finally called my father. I had held resentment for over a decade after he had left my mother.  After Charlie was born I tried to put those resentments aside so my kids wouldn’t have to carry that weight, but there was still the scars. Yet in that moment I still needed him.

Despite his repeated attempts to connect, we had not spoke in a while. But I told him what I was going to say, and for the first time in my life the conversation felt different. He wasn’t advising from above. He was listening the way a colleague listens, leaning in, asking questions, making connections I hadn’t seen.

Sensing that I was struggling with insecurities he began to tell me of my grandfather. He too had stood in front audiences and proposed a new way to imagine the world with a connected East and West in a time of great political tension. A view of a more connected humanity, a shared existence in our one world. The prejudices he was fighting had resulted in the suspected murder of his oldest son, my father’s older brother. I had known the story, unfolding before my birth, but I had trouble truly understanding it. The suffering that must have left in them would have been immense. My father had turned to the stars to search of belonging in the cosmos, far away from the traumas of mankind. Now I had spent a life diving back into the same purpose my grandfather had opened, but through a lens of history rather than modern politics. What was at the bottom of everything, what connected us rather than pulled us apart.

Elder had walked me up to a door in the mountain and told me it reached back to the ancestors. I had drunk the “grandfather plant” in a ritual older than the door and sat in front of it and felt it. Now I was connecting with my grandfather through the words of my father.  How many generations before him had we been on the searching for the same thing.

Before my dad hung up he told me he was proud of me.

Then he said it. “Believe in yourself. You’ve been there. Trust in what you felt there.”

I finally saw him, were just humans looking for what pulled everything together from opposite ends. His search was galactic, mine cultural.  And for whatever reason it had been part of my own ancestral tradition to do so.

Sitting in that monk’s cell looking out at the river, I pushed aside the unsympathetic clock that was counting down the moments before I needed to walk to the podium.

My father’s world had asked me to come back with an enigma, my answer was not a single artifact or mystery, it was the human condition at large. The cactus in Peru had opened a door for seven layers of cultural evolution to see their place within the land.  Each culture around the world evolved from an ecology with other doors that provided other insights. The rituals. The Temples, and the art that we built as humans were how we tapped into the intuitions and primal feelings that helped us imagine world views.  But what was the source of our imagination that all these doors were pulling from?

Did they all open to the same place?

The day after I flew home, the world shut down. Covid.

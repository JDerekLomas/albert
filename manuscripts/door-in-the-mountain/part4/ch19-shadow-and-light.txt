CHAPTER 19
Shadow and Light

The latest season of filming was done, and narration work was scheduled for the week after I got back.

The dirt was barely off me as I headed out the door to the recording studio, that’s when the text came in.

---

For years, in the windows between expeditions I would come home still carrying whatever the last land had felt, tasted, or sounded like, and pour it into my life. The two things I would pick up on the road to bring home were spices or instruments that I had found along the way. Each land seemed to have a specific tuning, or rhythm. And I would call Jamie and Leo over and let it seep into our jam sessions.

A drum had come the year after Varanasi. National Geographic had arranged a short video profile on my work, and a film crew from Bulgaria had shot a recreation of the night on the sacred mountain, the seven shamans, the fire, the drums. They arrived at the house with a gift from the shoot, a large hand drum made by a woman in the forests above the Black Sea. We unwrapped it on the living room floor. It was octagonal, the same shape those that had arrived on the Burkhan Khaldun years before. The hairs on my neck rose as I held it in my hand, so much had happened since the crescendo from that mountain.

When I lifted it, the light of a lamp came through from behind and threw the shadow of the leather ties across the face of the drum in triangular struts. Jamie went still. “That’s the drum I dreamed!” she said, referring to the lucid dream she had just described to me earlier that day. She never let anyone but the two of us hold that drum after that. Her stage name was Shadowlight, and it was the shadow and light that brought her to the drum.

People had started asking whether something was developing between us, but it wasn’t really like that and impossible to explain at the same time. The truth was our connection felt bigger than that, an ancient cosmic bond that could be felt when our eyes were closed and the sounds of our instruments would dance with each other through the air, saying so much without a word. Spirit twins, we called it. A friendship from a life or version of us that couldn’t really be placed and shouldn’t be treated recklessly with the messiness of normal human love.

But the kids had grown up with her as their magical wizard auntie. One night the whole city lost power. Jamie and Leo were over when it happened, and we continued to make music with our battery powered amps by candlelight as the kids ran down from their rooms to gather around the remaining light in the house. She told them they could move fire with their minds by watching the flame, accompanying their attempts with the mystical sounds of her violin. Every time the orange would flicker, they screamed with delight, convinced she had revealed a magic within them.

On a weekend where the kids were with their mother Jamie, Leo, and I loaded the gear into my truck and drove east into the desert.

Leo would set up a bunch of LEDs, we brought our battery powered amps and pedal boards that blinked with their array of lights. But here was no one out here to perform for. That was the whole point. We would have our own concert in the desert, playing as loudly and as freely as we liked for the rocks and the open sky above. Jamie named us the Infinite Vortices, because when we found our flow we would “opened a the portal,” she would say. “It is our responsibility to do so.”

We set up in a dry wash on a pile of blankets microphones, my electric guitar, the drum in my lap. I struck it into the mic and it reverberated across a long delay so each strike came back a half-beat behind itself and stacked into a looped pattern that seemed to hold in the air. I accelerated, further into an explosive release that shook the earth beneath me. Then silence.

Slowly, I picked up my guitar and began to strum. For a year I had been loosening the strings together, dropping one a quarter turn and striking it and waiting, then dropping it further, dowsing for the place where the notes stopped needing to be held down in cords and rang into each other on their own. That night my hands found it.

I slipped off it once and stopped. “Just do it again,” Jamie said, the way she always did. “Then it’s part of the song.” So I did it again. Her five-stringed electric violin had no frets, nothing fixed on it anywhere, freer than the doctrine of my guitar, and she slid up into the tuning and flowed through it. Leo felt the rhythm pulsing through the drones of our harmonics and laid beats underneath. The three lines locked into one that none of us was steering.

We had played this way a hundred times, but that night a tiny bit more of me let go that hadn’t before. I stopped trying, and the music started playing itself. We fell all the way into it, further than we’d ever been, running for hours. The moon crossed the wash. Coyotes answered off to the east. At one point Jamie lifted her electrified violin into the air and let the wind take the string, and it sang a tone none of us was playing, the sound of whales crossing the invisible sand, so big it put tears on our faces.

“We’re cosmic pirates!” She would laugh while taking a sip of whisky.

To her, music was a way to put love into the universe.  Even if no one was listening but us, the resonant vibrations that we channeled had a deep purpose.  It was the ritual, for us to connect to something bigger than ourselves.  To Jamie, it was the source.

---

The last time I saw her play in public had been a few months earlier, a sold-out performance in an IMAX dome theater in San Diego. She had built a cymatics rig that fed the resonance of her violin into a shallow plate of water at the front of the room which would create truly magical shapes in the water that were projected in extraordinary detail onto the curved space above us.

The lights went down. I sat near the back with Charlie and Millie.

She started low. A single note held for a long time. Rings opened. The rings became hexagons. The hexagons broke and reformed into dancing shapes. I could feel the violin in my sternum. The whole theater vibrated.

She moved her bow with deep intentionality and the water kept moving casting brilliant images all around us, transporting our minds into a space where magic and matter seemed to form in the harmonies of waves. She Called the show *Resonance*.

When it was over the kids ran up to their pirate wizard auntie through the crowd. She was wearing earrings painted as monarch wings that she later gifted Millie.

---

“They found a tumor and said I have six months to live.”

It felt like the wind had been pulled from my chest as I read the words on my phone. I dropped to my knees in the doorway and screamed “No!” into the screen.

Thirty minutes later I walked through the doors of the hospital where my children had been born and my leg had been removed. Dozens of Jamie’s friends overflowed her room into the hallway. When she saw me she called me over.

“They said I’m dying.”

She looked scared and yet fierce.

Everything in my life had said we manifest what reality comes through our imagination, and Jamie was the master of that.  “We can stop this,” I told her.

---

As a gigging musician she never had much money to start with, but she was beloved and that made her rich in community. Michelle offered her the guest apartment on her property on the cliffs overlooking the ocean as the staging ground for the fight.

Musicians and friends from all parts of her life came with offerings of care, it was like watching a deity forming right in front of me. But while a genuinely massive outpouring of love surrounded her, she turned to me to help her navigate the many string of consequential decisions ahead.

There were no expeditions on the horizon and I began juggling parenting and caregiving like I had done with exploration, doing my best to partition my life and bring everything I could to her fight.

She was determined, and we followed her lead. If she had one word for what she wanted, it was sovereignty. Sovereignty over every decision about her own body, her treatment, her death if it came to that.

So even as her caregiver, I much of that was to sit as an observer, doing as she instructed, playing music beside her and learning from her experience as she moved through her own transformation. Foods that might help her body resist. Tinctures. And eventually, singing bowls that I carried into the infusion centers, awkwardly explaining to the doctors that we needed to resonate the medicine first, per her command. No one would dare question her.

She was mischievous with her power, commanding Leo not to cut his hair until she was healed, fulfilling her long desire to see him with a fro. His usually buttoned-up tech entrepreneur transformed over the months to her joyous delight.

Yet all the while, beneath the lighter moments, she was facing the impossible.

She would stand stoic on the cliffs staring into the storms, holding her father’s walking stick like a staff. In the gaps between torrents of wind and rain the sun would emerge, casting rainbows in rapid succession.

Six months had passed, and we had proven the drs wrong, but still slowly her body seemed to be going in the wrong direction.  The steady stream of complications began a season of hospital stays.

Santiago, a Colombian guitarist who had been in a band with her and loved Jamie like a sister, moved to San Diego to became my right hand in supporting her. He had a massive heart that seemed to almost burst out of him in every direction and would stay at her side on the nights I had the kids. And still, exhausted beyond reason in the ICU, he’d find the energy to hit on a doctor as she walked through, then, when she gently turned him down, ask her for a warm blanket instead. We called ourselves the guardians of the temple.

As the winter rolled in the storms picked up further, torrents of rain that filled the dried desert lake beds where we had played for the first time in tens of thousands of years.

Santiago turned to me one afternoon.

“Hay Belleza en la tormenta.”

There is Beauty in the storm.

It was true, but the intensity of the journey picked up with the storms, and with it the complications. Soon I found myself balancing life or death crisis between school pick ups.

Sometimes I would return after a long stretch at the hospital and find Millie, now eleven, had made dinner. Charlie, at thirteen, had set the table. They had grown through transformation time and time again. I was proud of them for how they were responding. But it broke my heart.

That fall Charlie had begun waking me at five-thirty each morning to drive him to the gym before school, building his body for his dreams to become a pro skier. On the pre-dawn drives he would ask me about how she was doing. “Remember when she taught me to move the fire of a candle with my mind?” he asked. “Maybe she can fix this with hers.”

Millie as a tween was emerging into her own world, now wanting to try acting. But when she could feel I was struggling, she would quietly come sit beside me, and warp her arm around me in long hugs. She too had grown up to the sounds of Jamie’s violin ringing through our home.  They needed me also.

---

The one year mark passed.

Why wasn’t it working, imagination was supposed to be our super power.  There was no one I knew in the world who could harness that better than Jamie.  I went through everything I could think of. From finding the experimental immunotherapies to strict diets. From deep visualizations to sound baths.  But still her body declined.

Yet everywhere we went magic would flow to her.  Even now there were so many moments of syncronicities, whatever was needed arriving at the exact time it was needed.   There was a surprising amount of joy because of it.

One spring afternoon Jamie’s strength rebounded. She picked up the violin and played as she had long before. The brain fog that had been breaking the connection between her mind and her fingers vanished, and suddenly you could see the layers of awareness return. Her vibrations sang through the small apartment while a few friends and I watched in awe.

She set the violin down to go rest.

“There is so much love,” she said, looking over her thinned shoulders. “It all turns into love.”

That night I finally opened *The Tibetan Book of Living and Dying*. Handed to me on the origin lake of the Maya, under a full moon months before. I had not been ready for its preparatory prose and neither had she, but it was time.

I started reading as she slept, to interpret it for her gently. The hospice nurses had left a pamphlet, to my surprise it mirrored so many of the observations within the Tibetan book. The body slowing. Breath shifting. The senses turning inward. The same progression, described one way by palliative science and another by fourteenth-century Tibetan monks.

I tried it sitting next to her. Thoughts entered and I invited them away, but every so often I could find that stillness. It felt like the vibrations of my mind would sync into a resonance for a moment, then a new thought would knock it away.

---

Friends came and played, and her creative output intensified the more her body failed, terminal lucidity, the hospice nurses called it, the unexpected clarity that sometimes precedes death. Hers seemed to last not days but weeks.

She started living only by her stage name. Shadowlight.

One afternoon, exhaustion in every line of her face, she summoned the strength to hold our octagonal drum in the air and sing. Sunlight filtered through a prism on the window and cast a rainbow over her.  The rhythm of her beat was pure as she danced.

She looked at me and smiled.

“It’s fun, you know? All of it.”

I laughed. “It’s true, but how can this be so fun?”

“Because when there are no options left, all you have is your imagination,” she said “And if you have a good imagination, everything can be magic,” she finished with a mischievous smile.

She told me about the place she was visiting. Regular day stuff, she said. Normal activities. “Just better lighting.” She was making friends there.

I felt like an guest sometimes, sitting quietly as she communed with the unseen. Cultures around the world have described this. Christianity saw saints and angels gathering near death. Hindu scripture describes Yama’s messengers, the Maya had their dogs at the threshold.

Many tradition described what Jamie was experiencing.

One night, to catch my breath, I took a long walk through Michelle’s kitchen and into the atrium where ceremonial masks from around the world hung on every wall. At one end was a large painting I had passed countless times. I stopped in front of it now.

A small family on the tundra, two children being sent off on a reindeer-drawn sled in front of a teepee-like yurt. Years before, Amaraa had taken me north to visit the reindeer herders who lived in tents like those, at the far edge of Mongolia. I looked at the faces of the people on the faded canvas, their high cheekbones the same ones that gave away Jamie’s Korean blood. It felt like a version of us was up there.

I couldn’t help but wonder, had we done this before, in some earlier turn of the cycle? I stood there a long time.

---

On June 6, 2024, in the soft morning light, on the mesa above the Pacific less than a mile from where one of the most ancient burials in North America had been found a decade earlier, Shadowlight crossed.

She wore the Mongol fur hat I had gotten long ago on the steppe and given her, she called it her magic hat. In her other hand was the bone of a buffalo wrapped in white fur with a lone feather that had been given to her years before.

I had watched parts of her fade and turn off one by one, slowly, painfully, but also beautifully, with all the magic she knew. Now the last of her earthly being came down to a single green line blinking on the monitor. When even that grew too faint for the machine to read, I took a rose from the many flowers surrounding her and laid it on her chest. The tiny vibrations of its petals became the last physical flutters of life I could see in her body. But even there you could feel the invisible strength of that magic. The same thing her music had always felt like.

We began to chant softly beside her.

Om. Om. Om.

I swear the light in the room became thicker. The veil between the realms collapsed. She took one last long breath and exhaled the wind from her body. She passed through.

Then there was silence.

The words of her last song looped in my head, “together we’ll dance across the universe.”

We stayed with her. For more than a day I kept vigil with her amongst her flowers, and let the invisible continue to play out around us.

In the dark hours before the next dawn, Santiago and I put on a recording of hers, *Cosmic Butterfly*. We played next to her earthly vessel, in the opening tuning we had found in the desert years before. I danced with her one last time. At 2:22 AM the song ended.  It was her favorite number.

The oldest ritual of all had been completed.

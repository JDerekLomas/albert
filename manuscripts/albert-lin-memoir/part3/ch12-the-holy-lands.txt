CHAPTER 12
The Holy Lands

I had reached the holy lands. Things had gone well in Guatemala, China, and Mongolia, and now National Geographic wanted me as their explorer of the ancient world at large. I was to host my very own series, "Lost Cities." 

My whole life had been a single big bet, from the time I had told my mom I was going buy a horse and met the nomads of Mongolia till now. The audacity along the way had all been pointing to this, and the gratitude and shock that it had actually turned into something real and lasting was overwhelming.  My childhood dream of being an explorer had come true.

I checked with the kids, and Kara, we made a plan for the year to make it work while still co-parenting half the time, and I packed my bags for the journey began.

A producer named Lucy Van Beek had been assembling a team of researchers to find the projects where our technology could contribute real work. I had come to Acre [where?] to meet the wider crew for the first time.

That night the team gathered in a small hotel room. The director made introductions. Joe Steel was an engineer, a clean-cut, soft-spoken Englishman in his early thirties who had built his own company mapping the world for Hollywood. He'd come with laptops and espresso instead of trowels, and he wore the same easy smile no matter how impossible the timeline. We brainstormed late.

The next morning Joe handed me an iPad. He had coded a prototype overnight, an augmented reality window into the tunnels beneath the city. I held it up against the modern street and watched a thousand-year-old hidden passageway appear on the screen, running underneath the cobblestones in front of me.

Acre had been a Crusader stronghold once, the last one in the Holy Land. Beneath its streets ran tunnels where warrior-monks had prepared for sieges, where alliances with Mongols had been whispered against the advance of the Mamluks. Above those vaults were Ottoman stone houses. Above those, present-day cafés and markets. History was strata underfoot.


I put the screen down and caught myself watching the streets the way I had watched the ghats, both places of pilgrimage. A call to prayer rose from a minaret somewhere to the east. From a few blocks west a church bell answered. [What created the spiritual power of these sacred lands -- and how did it relate to all the others? We had the potential to use new technologies to rediscover our past ]


---

Over the years our work would pull me through the eastern Mediterranean and beyond.

Four-thousand-year-old figures on the petroglyph walls of Wadi Rum. Petra's catchments, engineered into the desert to teach stone how to hold water.

The Nile from Egypt into Sudan, Karnak at sunset, the flooded tombs of Kushite pharaohs I swam down into.

On the seafloor off the Israeli coast, Canaanite shipwrecks. Their beads traced a network of people who had survived the megadrought four thousand two hundred years ago by throwing parties with vats of wine and inviting the whole sea to trade.

Up on the mountain-top forts Jewish rebels held against the Romans, the stones still holding the silhouette of the siege.

And under Acre, the Crusader tunnels.

It was a place of consequences. Conflicts and war and an epic landscape of rugged wonder.

People had been fighting over this stretch of ground for five thousand years. Different names. Rising and falling. Yet they kept coming back.

Maybe it's because the ground had its own story.

---

I stood at the edge of the Dead Sea, the lowest point on the surface of the earth, a place where the mantle itself is splitting open. Beneath the brine ran a vast salt cap, and from somewhere deeper still, black asphalt would bubble up through it and rise to the surface in lumps the size of cattle. Ancient fishermen pulled them from the water and traded them in cities like Petra, where the Nabataeans used the sticky tar substance for waterproofing and embalming. The Romans called this place Lacus Asphaltites. The Sea of Asphalt.

I walked the shoreline above what some archaeologists now call the most likely site of Sodom and Gomorrah, Tall el-Hammam in the Jordan Valley. The aerial scans we made there had picked up something nobody had seen, the radial signature of a massive impact, with stone melted into glass in a ring around it. The researchers there had concluded it was destroyed around 1650 BC by a cosmic airburst, a meteor that exploded in the air with a thousand times the energy of the Hiroshima bomb. Not everyone accepts it. But I had walked that ground and seen the stones that had been melted to glass. The area around the Dead Sea stayed uninhabited for six hundred years after. The Bible tells of Sodom consumed by fire from above. Perhaps the story was a memory of something like this.

A few thousand kilometers to the west, the volcanic Greek island of Santorini blew its top around the same century. Standing on the rim of the caldera, amongst the throngs of tourists and the white buildings with their blue doors, it seemed impossible to imagine an eruption that some scholars think had released the energy of hundreds of thousands of atomic bombs. Pyroclastic flows down the cliffs. Tsunamis reaching the coast of Egypt within hours, their waves drawing the water from the coastline before crashing back in. The bloody Nile, the darkness over the land described in the Old Testament, could it have been the ash and red bloom of Santorini drifting across the Mediterranean?

And further north, at the end of the last ice age, around seventy-five hundred years ago, as sea levels rose in uneven pockets, the Mediterranean had likely broken through the Bosphorus and poured into what had been a freshwater lake. The Black Sea filled by tens of meters in the span of just a few years, swallowing an area the size of Florida and every potential human settlement on its shore. Was this the seed of the flood story that ran from Gilgamesh through Genesis through the Qur'an? A planet-scale event that would have been encoded in the memory of every culture that took in the survivors.

These events were not allegory. They had happened to real people in real places. Whatever the stories became across the millennia, they began as humans trying to find words large enough for what the earth and the sky had done.

But knowing the mechanism didn't dispel the magic.

My father had spent his life mapping the physics of the universe and still couldn't say what had happened before the first instant. My mother played a Chopin nocturne that would draw out emotions within me without being able to tell me why.

We could name the moving parts. But that couldn't explain away the cosmic mystery of what each one of those experiences was. The fact that we are alive at all, on this bubbling, breathing, constantly changing rock in a universe of matter that survived by the tiniest of margins, is a miracle.

What I was beginning to see was that I was walking through geologies of wonder. The Holy Lands were one. Religions had been born from places where humans grappled with the violence and awe of nature at a geological and planetary scale. Where the cosmos and the deep had been speaking loudly for longer than anyone could remember.

I would see the pattern again many years later, in Iceland. I stood over an active volcano with a man named Thor whose childhood village of Grindavík had been destroyed. We picked up volcanic stones and tossed them like feathers, pumice so light it practically floated on the wind.

"Can you imagine it?" he said. "It destroyed my village. But it is Mother Nature, and it is beautiful too."

The glaciers moaned in the distance. The sky danced with auroras. The Icelandic manuscripts I'd been shown held a world tree binding nine realms, three Norns spinning fate at its roots, a Ragnarok that returned everything to fire and ice every age. From those texts came the world of elves and trolls and giants that would later seed Tolkien and Marvel.

And one my mother took me to as a kid. Stonehenge, the five-thousand-year-old ring everyone has seen and no one fully understands. Less than a mile and a half away lies a forgotten spring far older than the stones, and I went to find it. Its geothermal water holds the same mild temperature all year, drinkable even as the land freezes over in winter. A rare algae stains the flint pink where it lies in the pool. That spring would have drawn people for thousands of years before the first stone went up, which is why some archaeologists now suspect it, not the circle, is what the whole place was built around.

Landscapes of wonder were the birthplace of origin stories, places where nature revealed its magic and left us to explain it.

The Holy Lands were everywhere. Over time I started to see that all lands were holy.

---

After each journey I came home and found the same forces at work in my own house. Watching two kids become themselves was as astonishing as anything I had stood in front of.

Charlie was nine now and Millie seven, and they seemed to be growing faster each day.

We had worked out a plan where I would be gone for a few weeks at a time and then come back and drop into dad mode for an equal amount of time, which meant flying back and forth around the world constantly. From the road I sent them messages from wherever I was: a satellite phone in the jungle, a hotel in India, a tunnel in Acre. Small notes of where I had been and what I had seen.

Charlie would reply with questions about specifics. What color the paint on the ancient walls was. Millie would send new surprise notes and gifts in my luggage, and I would show her I had found them. Sometimes she would make a protection bracelet for me, and make sure I wore it on the shoots, even if it was made from bright pink yarn. When I came home I'd put a trinket in each of their hands, a bracelet or bead from some far-off land and tell them the stories that I had brought back.

Then we would seize the days. "Carpe Diem," I'd say with the same gusto every morning, until they rolled their eyes. School breaks we'd head into the desert, the coast, the mountains.

One summer we found a stretch of river deep in the Inyo National Forest, where the ridges of the high Sierra fold into one another for hundreds of miles. Cold snow-fed water ran alongside natural hot springs that had cut shallow muddy jacuzzis into the bank. We took turns plunging into the snowmelt, then crawling into the warm mud. Millie made face sculptures in it. Charlie and I challenged each other to see who could stay in the cold longer. He usually won.

"Why is it hot if there's still snow?" Charlie asked.

I told him there was magma below us, rock heating the springs before they surfaced. Millie watched the steam come off the water. Then they wandered through the meadow above ten thousand feet, playing in the grassy tundra while chasing tiny frogs with yelps of joy each time they caught one.

When I was eight the Loma Prieta earthquake had shaken us in the canyon behind our house. Before my father did his best to explain the geophysics of tectonic plates by pointing out the sinusoidal pattern that was left in the wall of Nat Geo magazines on the shelf, I had been certain we were lying on the belly of a giant.

Every culture has a giant.

As I watched the kids play along the banks of a steaming river pouring down from the shoulders of our magnificent giants of granite that pushed their way up through the earth's crust, the magic of the moment felt more real than the science that could explain it.

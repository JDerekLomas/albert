CHAPTER 17
The Cloud Warriors

Once a year National Geographic brought everyone home. Every grantee it had ever funded, from every continent, converged on Washington DC for a few days.

The first time I walked into that building I had been a graduate student in my only suit with a binder under my arm, trying to talk my way into a grant. Now I came back every year and knew where the coffee was.

The hotels around headquarters filled up with people who spent most of the year somewhere with no cell service. Non-lethal bear traps and GPS collars stood in the hallways, crated and waiting to be shipped. Moonshine appeared out of somebody's field kit. Everyone in that building had been alone in a hard place for months and had four days to say so out loud, so nobody slept much and the conversations ran long and got strange.

That was where I met Johan Reinhard, who had found the ice maiden high on a volcano in the Andes. And Wasfia Nazreen, who had climbed the highest peak on every continent in the name of the women of her country. There were so many incredible explorers everywhere you looked. And that was where I met Wade Davis. I had read his books before I ever met him, before he had placed the *father plant* into my hands.

He described to me a way to see human culture, using the word *ethnosphere*. "The sum total of all thoughts, dreams, ideas, intuitions, myths and memories brought into being by the human imagination."

And he said we were losing it faster than we were losing the *biosphere*. "Half of the seven thousand languages still spoken are no longer being taught to a single child. Every two weeks an elder dies and takes the last syllables of one into the ground."

He talked with conviction and it left an impression.

---

A month after the camel was born I was back in Peru, after the Chachapoya, the Cloud Warriors, the people the Inca had fought and the Spanish had all but erased. Eoin O'Shea was my partner developing shows now, and he had a thing for sticking it to the man. Together with the Lost Cities family we went after the underdogs, Joe and Gary and the whole crew working together to tell the story of the ones left out of the history books. This was something that had been driving me since before even that first trip to Mongolia.

Their citadel of Kuélap had surfaced to archaeology a century before, a vast city in the clouds of a scale to challenge Machu Picchu, but too vast and intricate to be explained by the few home sites around it. War had swallowed the world of those who built it, and then a cloud forest dense enough to hide a civilization.

We drove up into the cloud forest to find Mama Rosa. Jairo Valqui, a linguist out of San Marcos in Lima, had spent years in these valleys chasing what was left of the Chacha tongue, and he was the one who took us to her. "She is among the last who still carry fragments of it," he told me as our truck chugged up the steep dirt roads. "She might actually be the last."

Mama Rosa lived in a mud-brick village high above the valley. When we arrived she and her husband were stoking a bread oven in the courtyard, a sea of guinea pigs in one corner. A bright red skirt, a white top, a bandana over her gray hair. She pulled a bench over for us and went back and forth between us and the oven, feeding the fire and moving the loaves around, while Jairo translated.

Most of what she spoke was Quechua, but every so often a word surfaced from her mother's tongue, like a ghost coming through the fog. I could tell when because Jairo would stop and write it down.

She remembered her grandmother speaking Chachapoya, the two of them walking the long mountain trails, learning names for each curve of the river and each pass as they went. That was how she learned the way.

Which is why what survived is mostly that, place names and a few hundred family surnames, that's it. Kuélap is one. Nobody is certain what the first part means, but the ending, *lap*, is Chacha, *a stronghold*, and was a clue left without context on modern maps.

But this was about more than names on a map.

My friend Lera Boroditsky, a celebrated linguist, once told me about a language in northern Australia where "there are no words for left or right." She said, "Everything is done in cardinal directions, north and south and east and west, at every scale."

"Left and right can depend on your point of view," she explained. "When you turn around, what used to be on your left is now on your right. North and south and east and west are absolute. They are locked on the landscape."

She had helped me understand that the words any culture creates are its frame of reference, and that frame is a way of being a person. "Does the world turn around us, or do we turn around it?"

I sat in that courtyard with Mama Rosa, listening to the last resonant vibrations of the Chachapoya worldview in her voice as she maintained the embers of her wood oven.

"I hope my children will remember these words," she said.

Then she laughed at us and our equipment. "I never dreamed anyone would come with cameras this big to listen to me."

She and Jairo had been writing down the last fragments of that memory for years, searching for meaning and perspectives embedded in between the names and in the hints of a grammar, holding a little more of the ethnosphere intact.

When it was time to go she broke off a piece of the warm bread and put it in my hand, and sang us a farewell that wove in fragments of her lost language. "Come back to visit, remember me, before I am gone," it flowed with its haunting melody.

---

Days earlier I had been up on the funeral cliffs above Juan Santillán's village. He was a farmer who also kept the local heritage office. Every hold I reached for had been used before, by the Chachapoya themselves, hauling their own dead up to rest.

Near the top the crew stayed below to belay me, and I went the last stretch alone, past a pile of wavy reddish-brown hair, a single human tooth, fragments of rope. The smell was earthy. Limestone without the water. I shimmied around the last corner with a thousand feet of air beneath me.

There they stood. A line of clay sarcophagi, the purunmachus, their painted faces angled at the horizon. Hollow eyes and open mouths. One had broken open, and I could see the embalmed skeleton inside. They had held that pose for six hundred years, through the painting of the Sistine Chapel and the first step onto the moon.

I sat down amongst them and ate a sandwich from my pocket, probably the first person to have lunch with the Cloud Warriors in six centuries. Many Andean cultures had eaten with their dead too, believing what's gone was never really gone.

I looked at the faces painted onto the sarcophagi and wondered if their expressions were the same that I had seen around the bread oven a few days before.

The next morning the team had gathered around the data as Joe and Duncan brought it to life on their screens. The room filled, the crew and local townspeople, some perched in the rafters, everyone waiting to see what the machines had found.

More sarcophagi resolved out of the rock. Then, high in the cliff and beyond any reach, a hidden cave, and inside it the LiDAR revealed a vast scatter of bones. A cliffside graveyard in the clouds. I looked over and Juan was crying. It was more than old bones and clay to him. They were his own story.

---

And we were following old trade paths now, the ones that once bound the funeral cliffs to the desert and the Amazon. The Peruvian archaeologist Jeff Contreras brought us to their arteries from the mighty Marañón, the headwaters of the Amazon, to see if we could follow them to the settlements they once fed.

The descent from the cloud forest was like traveling through epochs. The green thinned with every switchback, the jungle giving way to a sunbaked canyon land that looked more like Arizona than the Amazon it fed. By the time we reached the river the water had thickened to the color of chocolate from rain coming off the high Andes.

Tradition said no journey on the Marañón should begin without an offering of coca leaves to Pachamama, a way of remembering that you travel through living spirits and not just landscapes. The name is Quechua, though the idea was older. What the Chachapoya called the earth, nobody can say.

The morning we were launching the rafts, things were chaotic, delays on the road making our start time later than we had hoped. In our rush, we somehow forgot the offering. Later that would feel like breaking a covenant.

The river was already swollen, and it grew angrier as we came to a critical turn, where a wall of stone forced the water sideways into a vicious undertow. A gust of wind caught the camera boat ahead of us and slammed it into the rock, out of position.

Our raft hit theirs. Theirs flipped.

Gary, our second cameraman, Adam, and Graeme, our burly Scottish safety officer, dropped straight into the current with all the camera gear.

I stood on the bow with Jeff, staring into the muddy water. Most of the team popped up in their life vests.

Not all.

Five seconds. Ten. Twenty.

I hadn't taken a breath since they went under. Had I just watched my friends drown?

At nearly a full minute they finally burst to the surface, forced up by their vests like popcorn. Adam's gasps came out as half-sobs.

Our director, Steve Sherman, had gone in too, and the first thing out of his mouth, soaked and absurdly heroic, was, "Get the drones up! Get the drones up!"

The cameras were gone and he was still filming the story in his head.

We made the bank. Gary's shin was a bloody bulge. He looked down at it, then up at me.

"Somebody get me a cigarette," he said. Then, "Who's got the fucking hard drives?"

Then Jeff pulled a handful of coca leaves from his pocket and dropped to his knees at the water's edge.

"We forgot the offering," he said. "We have to thank the river. No one died."

The debate began about what to do next. There were those of us who wanted to take the last of the gear and continue. But it seemed the accident had cracked something loose, and bad news kept coming. I learned my mother was found unconscious at home, a diabetic spike. Our ropes expert, one of the strongest people I knew, fell in the shower and broke his ribs. A few days later, as we sat in purgatory waiting to see what the production could pull off, word came from Nat Geo. We were standing down. Thankfully my mother recovered quickly, but the universe seemed to be making a point.

And on my first night back in San Diego a buffalo skull that had hung screwed to my wall for years fell and shattered on the floor, scattering bone and teeth in a way that eerily recalled the remains I'd found in the cloud forest.

Charlie and Millie performed their own small rituals, their séance of burning sage and burying the buffalo bones in the backyard, half playfully and half because their friends had made it clear they would not sleep over in a house where the skulls fell off the walls. I often wondered how my kids would remember their childhood. But whenever I checked in to see if they thought I should keep going, they would both always say yes.

---

Months passed and by September the rain in Peru had calmed and it was time to make peace with the river. We returned, but this time Jeff hired a paqo, a local shaman named Inti from the neighboring village to make the offering and ask the river's permission.

The night before we put in, I went down alone to sleep by the water under the open sky. Inti was already there, sitting on his heels at the edge of the river, hands cupped around a small fire. I sat down beside him and watched it for a while. Eventually I told him everything. Not just the flip, but my mother in the hospital, my friend's ribs, the skull on the floor, the offering we'd forgotten. "Is it a curse?" I asked through translation.

He chewed coca leaves for a long time, listening for something I couldn't hear. Then he began to speak, low and unhurried, as Luigi our head boatman translated for me.

"It's not a curse. You're lucky. The spirits are not so vain that they would break you over one forgotten offering. They see deeper. The river was swollen." Inti placed more and more coca leaves in between the cheek of his mouth as he spoke.

"A storm was already coming down out of the high Andes that would flood the valley behind it. Your raft flipped just one bend before the gorge where the Chachapoya trails meet the Inca trails of the high range. That section has no exit. You would have been inside it for days when the storm hit." Luigi continued to translate. "The signs were warnings. That you felt them at all means you are connected to the other side. Follow your intuitions, and always respect the old ways."

"Out here the rule is nothing probable, everything possible," Luigi added with a smile.

Years before, Elder had shown me the door in the mountain. Inti gave me something plainer. From down in the canyon, a curse had been the only reading available. But the storm that would have killed us had been out past the horizon the whole time, past anything we could see from between those walls. Through that perspective we hadn't been cursed, we'd been spared. Inti had read the river the way I read a LiDAR map.

I slept on the river that night. A light rain came and went. When I woke, an Amazonian giant yellow-leg centipede crawled out from under my sleeping bag. Its venom can kill. It had used me for warmth without biting.

In the morning light, Inti gathered us on the bank. We each took handfuls of coca leaves and poured them into the water, the way the ancient Chachapoya and Inca had done. I watched the leaves drift downriver until they were out of sight, our prayers floating along with them.

The first time I had come to these mountains I had taken something out of them. Cactus distilled in a pot at Elder's family home, drunk in a cave in front of a door I could not open. This time I had come to stand humbled and offer something back.

Then we loaded the rafts and pushed off down the same water that had thrown us only months before.

This time we found our flow, moving with the river instead of past it.

Downstream the old trade routes ran where Jeff had said they would, stone-lined trails from the banks up into the cloud forest. We pointed the LiDAR at places on the map that held the ancient names we had heard from Mama Rosa. The locations were now empty forests and mountains, but what came back in the data were vast terraces of Chachapoya home platforms climbing every hillside. The civilization that Kuélap had hinted at had been everywhere around us, just under the trees.

I thought again of Mama Rosa, the last to carry the words that once rose from these homes. In her voice, more than the data, was their world.

Years ago, when I had first arrived in Peru, Elder had told me his stone door reached back seven versions of us before the Inca. At first I thought that sounded like a lot, but then I learned there was archaeological knowledge of even more. They had lived and fought together through deserts, jungles, mountains, and cloud forests. Each emerged from the diverse ecologies of what the Inca had called Pachamama. The Spanish conquest had done its best to wipe the history of the land clean, and the result had been tragic. But in Mama Rosa's words, and in the sarcophagi faces that still looked to the sun, aspects of them were still living today. They were the cultural echoes that remained, each giving hints of what had been figured out. This was what Wade had called the ethnosphere. And it was worth fighting for at its frontiers.

CHAPTER 14
The Question

The Ganges had set me down. The Mediterranean had shown me that all lands were holy. Peru had shown me a door in the mountain. Then Cambridge called. The Darwin Lecture, a storied stage where members of academia gathered each year around its biggest open questions. I had been asked to speak on enigmas, the mysteries that still had no answer. I spent months juggling school pickups with expeditions trying to think it through. The truth is I was filled with anxiety and self-doubt, the imposter syndrome in me bursting at the seams.

Derek, my old best friend from college, had been my intellectual sounding board through the months of prep. By then he was a professor of AI and computer science in Europe. His real obsession was the esoteric stories of the philosophers of the past, Pythagoras being his favorite. We spent long hours on the phone across time zones, sending each other pages, each of us reading and answering the other. We had even begun writing something together, a chapter we called *The Enigma of Mind: A Theory of Evolution and Conscious Experience*. Our debates ran late and ranged wild, from the weird to the absurd and back, two old friends going at it like the captain and the surgeon trading arguments across the wardroom table in my old sailing novels. But the thing that mattered most was simpler than any of the intellectualism. I was standing at the edge of something that left me feeling exposed, and Derek jumped in so I wasn't facing it alone.

Cambridge held both of my oldest wounds in one place. My father's world, where I had always wondered whether I was good enough for him. And the same streets where, as a boy, I'd been made to feel I didn't belong. Ever since, I had been chasing one question underneath everything, why I felt the way I did as a human.

Not as an academic problem, a lived one.

I had crossed the world and gone back through time looking for it. Now I was returning to where the wounds began, to stand up in my father's world and say what I had found. It felt like a rite of passage, a chance to face the fear of not belonging and turn it into something else.

---

For years I had been an explorer searching for mysteries in the ancient world. But the strangest mystery I had stumbled into was in my own mind, learning to remap reality with tools that may have been older than any building.

The day before the talk my university hosts had put me up in a tiny room that felt like a monk's cell, a small old bed, a simple desk against the window. I sat at the desk and watched the long wooden boats glide past, punters pushing them along as they had for hundreds of years. Out there were people whose actual job in life was to think about thinking, and they were going to fill a hall and listen to me?

I had given lectures before, many of them. I had grown to love the feeling in my chest as the adrenaline surged before I took the stage. But this was different. These were my father's halls. Some part of me kept waiting for someone to walk in and tell me there had been a mistake. I felt like I had been transported into the boy version of me before a big test.

In that same city my mother had knelt me on the stone with a sheet of paper over a medieval knight, and shown me how to bring a face seven hundred years dead up through the paper with a stick of wax. I had been doing a version of that ever since.

I thought of Charlie and Millie, asleep on the other side of the world. The fossils we dug in the Anza Borrego sandstone. The cardboard sleds down the dunes of Death Valley. What would be their first memories? How much I would rather be with them now.

The morning of the talk I called my father. All the grudges aside for the years of suffering at the behest of his decisions, I still needed him. I told him what I was going to say, and for the first time in my life the conversation felt different. He wasn't advising from above. He was listening the way a colleague listens, leaning in, asking questions, making connections I hadn't seen.

Before he hung up he told me he was proud of me. Then he said it. "Believe what you've seen, don't be scared. Most people read about these things, but you've been there. Trust in that."

All those years I had carried his rejection of my mother like it was also a rejection of me. The grudge I had worked so hard to let go of finally went somewhere quieter.

We were just two humans searching for the same thing from opposite ends. His galactic, mine cultural.

Where does it all come from?

I had spent the years since the accident watching different cultures do remarkably similar things with their bodies and their minds.

Drums on a sacred mountain in Mongolia. Drums splitting my palms in a Shiva temple beneath the Ganges. A drum a friend dreamed before it arrived from a forest near the Black Sea.

A medallion pressed into my hand at Everest base camp. The same syllable chanted over me in the dirt of California. The same syllable sounded out to the turn of a string of beads on the banks of the Ganges.

A stone door carved into rock in the Andes that opened into nothing. A door beside the burning ghats where Baba Lutlo had grown up.

Psilocybin in the dunes of Death Valley. A father plant distilled in a pot in the Andes.

Each ecology had grown its own tools. Did they all reach for the same place?

Sitting in that monk's cell looking out at the river, I pushed aside the unsympathetic clock that was counting down the moments before I needed to walk to the podium.

My notes sprawled across the page, names and places. I still didn't have an answer. Only the question I had been circling.

I walked to the lectern with the notebook in my hand, set it down, and looked out at a hall full of my father's world. Then I spoke.

"Imagination is not an individual gift," I said. "It is the engine of cultural evolution itself. Each of the tools I've seen is a filter, a perceptive lens shaped by the ecology that grew it, and the filter is what gives us our diversity. We have built our cultures in the shape of doors." I looked up from the page. "What I can't tell you is where they open from."

*What is the source of imagination? What does it all draw from?*

I had been the boy in these halls. They had asked me to come back with an enigma, and this is what it had distilled down to.

The next day, the world shut down. Covid.

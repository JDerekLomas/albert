CHAPTER 13
The Door in the Mountain

We filmed in jungles rivers, deserts, and now time for the high mountains. Peru had haunted me for years. Machu Picchu rising from the clouds. Johan Reinhard's photograph of an Inca girl preserved in ice, her face still composed after five centuries on the mountain. It's also where Gil had died.

What we were after this time was about what came even before all of that. Not the people whose terraces rim Machu Picchu, but the people from whom they had emerged. Again, into the source. The Wari believed they had not come from the mountains but were *of* the mountains, that the rock had given them up the way a river gives up a fish. And the duality of all things had been written into the way they buried their dead, men and women in pairs, like the moon and the sun or the sky and earth.

With our porters, we loaded the mules and began the climb into the Andes. Andaas was the name of our Peruvian archaeologist, a soft-spoken yet passionate guardian of his people's story. One of the porters was an unassuming man maybe twenty-five years older than me. His face was lined the way the mountains were lined. The others called him Elder.

One morning we passed a circular stone dwelling with an opening facing the tallest peak. Human bones poked up through the cold dirt, maybe a femur. "They are not really dead," Andaas said. "The dwellings are where the living come to speak with them, and to eat with them." Pachamama, the Earth Mother, held us all. There were other mothers too. Mama Killa was the moon. Mama Cocha was the sea. Each her own domain. Elder pulled a pouch of coca leaves from inside his coat. He held three leaves up to the sky, blew on them, and scattered them into the dwelling. The other porters quieted as he did, then repeated his actions at a distance. Now I understood he was not only a porter but also a paqo, a shaman of these mountains.

The expedition went on. We pushed higher. Sixteen thousand feet, then seventeen. The American archaeologists we were with showed signs of altitude sickness. Then we sent up the LiDAR drones, which had been equipped with high-altitude propeller blades to account for the lack of lift in the thin air, and began scanning in search of what tall grass and time had hidden once again.

By the middle of the week we had come back down to a small hacienda near the Sacred Valley. A rest day to charge ourselves and all the equipment. Llamas hung around the lanes outside, smelling of sheep and horse and wet dog when the afternoon rain came through. While the team unpacked I asked Elder if he would show me how he understood the deeper spirit of the land. Not for the cameras.

He knew what I was asking.

The next morning he was waiting outside the hacienda with a younger man at his side. The apprentice. A quiet kid in his twenties, who watched Elder the way you watch someone you are learning everything from, and said almost nothing. We slipped out of town.

At the foot of the trail he handed each of us something to carry. He gave me a small circular hand drum. The apprentice slung a guitar over his shoulder, and to his pack he tied a long white horn that appeared to be made of wood or paper mache, miraculously wound into the shape of a figure eight. Elder wore a felt conical hat with bright tasseled scarves looped around his neck. Against his quiet way of moving, and nearly absent use of words, the colors called out loudly. He and his assistant both shouldered bright woven blankets which seemed to make the mountains feel happier. Then we started up.

The path opened into a grand staircase cut into the mountainside, rising like an amphitheater hidden in a fold of the valley. The steps had been worn smooth by centuries of feet. Now no one was on them but us. The altitude made the light feel crisper, the way it had when I first arrived in Lhasa years before. Elder climbed ahead of us playing his flute, the apprentice walking silently behind him, and I came up last with the drum under my arm. Up here there was nothing to be but a body climbing into the thin bright air.

At the top, a vast open space looked out over crumbled ruins descending below and the green of a mountain rising on the opposing valley wall. Elder led us to the back of the overhang where the stone of the cave cooled into shadow. There, carved into the heart of the rock, was a stone doorway. It did not open into anything, just stone against stone.

"A doorway to other realms," Elder said, half in Spanish and half in Quechua, and I caught enough of it.

In front of the cave sat a small altar carved into a boulder. Vandals or possibly enemies of the Inca had taken the top off years ago, and the break was uneven and old. He set down a small water bottle filled with the San Pedro that he had distilled that morning. I had watched his elderly mother reduce the cactus skins in a simmering pot at his family home.

Years earlier, Wade Davis had placed a stalk of this cactus into my hands on my way out of a National Geographic gathering. He called it a *father plant* to the *mother plant* of the jungle. Of all the keys to the mind he had encountered as an anthropologist, he said, "this was the one." But it had only been a plant with a story. Now I was sitting in the back of an ancient cave in front of an Inca-built stone door I could not open, and a small bottle of the father plant from its native soil was on the altar between us.

Elder handed me the bottle. I thought of the last time I had seen through the lens of plants, in the dunes of Death Valley, and the one word that had gotten me through it.

Succumb. I drank.

The flute sang. The same melody Elder had been playing as he walked on the way up. Repeating. It sounded oddly familiar. He tried to explain, half in Spanish. "It is a song seven civilizations older than the Inca."

A stinging acrid taste, the San Pedro spreading downward, like I was being drawn into the earth rather than lifted out of it. The world did not melt into visions the way the psilocybin had in Death Valley. It clarified. The stone grew more present. I could feel the weight of the mountain above me and the roots of everything below, and the boundary between my body and the ground beneath me became something I could no longer locate. The earth felt patient, alive. Something close to compassion.

I picked up the hand drum and began a slow steady pulse. The apprentice took the guitar from his shoulder and started a quiet chord pattern beneath Elder's flute. The three of us sat in front of the door and held the song between us. When Elder dropped out for a breath, the apprentice carried it. I played lightly, the beat living within me.

At some point I thought of my great-grandfather. My father had once told me that when my great-grandfather had boarded a boat from China to the new world his older brother had got on a different boat. That boat had gone to Peru. The two of them had said goodbye on a dock in the south of China likely knowing it was forever. That brother had stepped off in this country and lived a life I would never know. Had he walked under these mountains?

The melody kept circling. Then I knew it. Filtered through time, echoing in local folk songs, eventually emerging as Simon and Garfunkel's *El Condor Pasa* on my parents' old vinyl records. I learned later the band had traveled through Peru and picked it up. It was the song of the Andes, a sound that had literally resonated around the globe with many having felt its pull unknowing of its source.

Elder gestured at the door one more time before we left, explaining that it was a threshold where past, present, and future overlapped. Through my limited Spanish, I understood it was a door he said reached as far back as seven versions of us before the Inca.

We came down from the cave as the sun dropped. The apprentice walked along the path using a small hand saw to collect eucalyptus branches, an armful that grew until it was a small forest tied to the roof of their tiny hatchback. We drove back to Elder's family home. The trees in the garden were hung with small things in random places. CDs on fishing string turning in the air to act as spinning mirrors, wooden carvings his grandchildren had made, old wooden horns curled around the branches.

In the courtyard he wove the eucalyptus branches into a low dome over a flattened patch of earth, a depression scooped in the middle for hot rocks. A fire heated them while we sat. The dark night emerged and the stars filled the sky.

The stones were glowing red when Elder set them in the depression. I crawled into the small space in briefs and left my prosthetic at the door, a thatched thicket of eucalyptus. He poured water gently over the stones and sent steam erupting into the dome, humming his ancient song. The smell of eucalyptus filled everything. Sweat came before I knew it had started. As the heat rose my body began to dissolve into it.

From somewhere down the valley a train began to call. Haunting, long, slow notes drawn through the canyon walls in a delayed reverb that did not fully end before it began again.

As the father plant ran through and the heat absorbed me, the door had been opened. I lay in the embrace of Mother Earth and nothing more, inside a dome of eucalyptus branches while a train horn drifted across a mountain range.

The reverb of that horn and the howl of the train followed me out of the dome. It would surface in me for years. And over time, I could not say whether any of it had been a dream.

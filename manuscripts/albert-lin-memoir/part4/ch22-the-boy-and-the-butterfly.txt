CHAPTER 22
The Boy and the Butterfly

One afternoon Charlie walked past my mother's piano, stopped, and sat down at the bench.

He had not played much before his accident, just messing around here and there. Yet what came from the piano was a complete melody. Notes I had never heard him play. 

I stood in the doorway watching. He finished, looked over at me, and grinned.

"My brain feels like a phone that's been completely wiped of its apps," he said. "There's no noise. I can see the patterns easily."

The cracked guitar I had found him in Reno, whose fractures had given it its voice, had been his instrument in the early stages of his recover. It had opened something more universal within him.  Now that was coming through the keys.

The accident had cleared the slate, and from wherever he had been, he had brought music back with him.

The buds of his identity would grow from it.

It was spring in Southern California.

---

When we first arrived back home from the hospital, the door was covered in notes from our neighbors. As we helped Charlie in, I remembered how I had been carried out of a wheelchair through the same threshold. Now it was Charlie.

His balance off, the vestibule system in his brain was injured. His eyes had lost their left periphery, so his head stayed in a constant slight tilt to compensate. And he had no idea where he was one hour to the next, everything about how we lived needed to be reconsidered.

Rich installed a gate at the top of the stairs the day before we came home. I made Charlie sleep next to me so that if he rose in the night I could make sure he didn't fall. Like his shadow, whenever he moved I walked behind him with a hand hooked in the back of his belt for support.

While his face looked the same, something had changed. They called it an "invisible injury" because the scars were on the inside.

But you could hear the injury in his voice. At first he spoke in an endless mumbling stream of thought, observations, and processings pouring out of him as he worked to figure out the world again.

His neurologist called it *perseveration* and suggested we put him on a medication to quiet it, temper his impulses as his frontal lobe had been impacted. I refused. The drug would only have masked it, made him duller, and what he needed was the opposite, more brain activity, not less, mixed with periods of rest to regenerate. The rambling would sort itself out on its own once impulse control rewired, and that would come with time.

Besides, I liked his rambling, it was better than the silence we had experienced. And it was not mindless.

He was going over everything around him as if he were seeing it for the first time, and the things he stopped on were the ones I had conditioned myself with life, to walk past.

He was curious with an awe of everything. Butterflies. Tiny caterpillars eggs on the milkweed in the garden. To me they were just white dots. To him they held the potential of the magic of the universe. Something the size of a grain of sand that could grow wings capable of carrying it across a continent.

Johnny and I drove around San Diego buying every milkweed plant we could find.

This would be the staging ground.

The hospital had done the rebirth. This was the second part, the coming of age.

Six months to build a brain. Yet it was more than a brain, it was the person within it. How would the experience affect who he would become?

---

There was a deeper seed from his coma that seemed to bubble up.

One specific memory.

The first time he said anything about where he had been, he had only had a handful of words in his brain.

He had seen a ton of light. And remembered a feeling. He called the feeling *stoke.* Then he passed back out.

It wasn't until a month later, as the connections in his brain slowly re-emerged, that more came.

A white void.

A park bench to his front and right. Deep familiarity, it was very inviting, like looking at your own home.

But he didn't sit on it. Not by choice.

He said there was a bright light all around him. There was a brightness and a love, "not a love between things, but the love that things are made of."

I think that was the *stoke* he had described.

He told me this deadpan, innocent and unassuming. Just what he had seen and felt. It was clear in how he interacted with the world, the feeling had a presence in everything.

This is what he remembered from the very edge of death. A year before, Jamie told me about the places she was visiting before she died. "Regular day stuff," she said. "Normal activities. Just better lighting."

Many could argue it was just the brain doing what brains do during a Near Death Experience. The phenomenon is embedded into accounts of the dying across all cultures. I had even read about it in the hospice pamphlet. Scientists have observed a surge of gamma activity in the last minutes of a dying brain, along with a release of serotonin and dopamine, the chemicals of euphoria. 

That may be, but the threshold of death has a magic. You can't explain away the magic with science. A neural correlate is not a cause. 

In other words, does a beautiful sunset cause the brain to release chemicals or do chemicals in the brain create how we experience each sunset? Is the beauty in the sunset or the chemicals?

Who could ever know.

--- WHATS HAPPENING TO THE BRAIN

Kara and I split responsibilities. She would keep Millie's school routine going while I would stop all other work to focus on home therapy with Charlie. But they would all come to visit regularly.

Laura said she would fend off the emails. I could use help so Johnny moved into the guest room downstairs to help, brothers in arms for the journey ahead. This, my own boy, was now the expedition.

Qasim came to visit that first week, an unexpected messenger. Jamie had introduced him to the crew in the epic desert jam for my fortieth birthday, and in the years I had known him we had become close. He had always seemed like someone who sought joy. Maybe that's why we called him Aladdin. But beneath his happy character he had never shared that he had traveled through his own brain injury journey, alone.

"Post-concussion syndrome years before, not like Charlie's but still serious," he told me as if admitting something. "Light hurting my eyes. Loud sounds debilitating. Balance and coordination."

Before we had met, he had gone through it in isolation, and it had almost crushed him.

"It was so fucking hard to talk about," he said. "It took years... maybe I didn't know how to talk about it. It took so much from me"

Then he turned to Charlie and spoke almost as if he was speaking to himself.

"No one believed me, no one could see the scars to prove it was really happening but I could feel them in the way my mind wasn't working."

After an exhaustive search Qasim had found a small clinic in Utah called Cognitive FX, where Dr. Alina Fong used fMRI scans to guide intensive therapy sprints. Finally, someone who offered to help him visualize the invisible.

His scans came back with some regions glowing too bright and others barely lighting at all.

"The axons are stretched or severed," he recalled her saying. "But the signal underneath is still strong enough to carve new routes around the damage."

They told him that his balance might now be processed in regions that had never done it before, detours that cost more energy and worked worse. A brain running its traffic the long way around.

"That's why you're having these energy crashes, your brain is on overdrive because it needs to be reorganized. It's a weird thing, by breaking my brain revieled that it was there," Qasim told Charlie.

It was hot, I opened the back doors and let the air move through. The cats milled, choosing whoever sat down.

"Our heart. Metabolism. Balance. Vision. Hearing. The way you laugh before you decide to. These *subconscious* functions of our brain are all happening beneath anything we can hear or think in our *conscious minds.*" he went on.

The accident had broken the wiring between those two layers.

The trick, Qasim had learned from Dr. Fong, was to flood the system. Force as many parts of the brain to work at once. With every region firing at the same time, the brain would be forced to rebuild the direct pathways it had lost, out of sheer need for efficiency.

Then repetition. Repetition. Repetition.

In the backyard, we set to work.

HOW WE DID IT
--- Mind

My travels had shown me that our human evolution had been much more tied to the cadence of the natural day. And if I was building a human, I would tap into its most natural state to do it. So life revolved around setting the clock.

So every night, at eight PM sharp we lay down side by side and ran a meditation that we made up as we went.

Our minds would focus first from the top of the head, feeling the mental sweep flow down through our neck then down through the body and out the arms and legs, till finally each finger and each toe, pushing the tensions of the day out through them.

Then we would try to find the space in our minds between thoughts.

When I would find it, the blank infinite illusive sensation was both fleeting and profound like standing at the edge of a great abyss. The phosphenes danced across my unthinking mind, visual geometries that some archaeologist argue explain the oldest rock art, the patterns a mind makes in the dark. Something faint from the source of imagination.

Finally, we listed what we were grateful for.

He listed everyone who had saved him, in order, in gratitude. It took some time.

We did the same thing at eight AM, but this time it would be his intentions for the day.

Head, arm tingling, fingers, infinite space in our minds, then.

"My intentions today are to progress in my healing and to work on my helmet design and help pay this love forward."

My original philosophy behind this was so that he would ingrain a sense of agency through the repeated framework of intention and gratitude on either end of the day.

Earlier I had placed commercial wearable biosensors on him and watched his body temperature fluctuate wildly. His heart rate variability was locked in fight mode.

That was his autonomic nervous system, the deep regulatory rhythm under heart rate, temperature, breath. It turned out to be part of the map he was rebuilding.

But simply lying together and breathing calmly, morning and night, was retraining the metronome under everything else. 

The timing was important, consistency was key.

After our morning routines we would play a game of chess for attention and spatial memory before I would build him a ridiculously giant omega3 heavy breakfast. This became harder as he grew tired of the piles of smoked salmon I pushed on him every day. To make up for it, my boxes of supplements filled in the gaps. The body needed fuel.

I would watch him take each bite, then observe him visualize the molecules working his damaged axons like tiny construction workers fixing his body as he had been instructed to by Deena and Andrew. Charlie had the knowledge of Olympic excellence teaching him the tools of maximizing his human potential through nothing more than thought.

And Deena Kastor, of all people, had been there in the darkest moments to help me realize it was going to be a mindset game for everyone involved. She had literally written the book, *Let Your Mind Run*.

--- Body

One morning before the heat came up we strung the slackline low between two trees in the backyard. San Diego summer temperatures had arrived two weeks after we got home.

Johnny took one side. I took the other. Qasim was at the fence. All of us had our hands out ready to catch him.

He stepped on. Down. Stepped on again. Down.

Between tries, breath work. Rapid inhales. Deep exhale. Huge inhale. Hold. Oxygen explosions into his brain, the way the hyperbaric chambers had pushed it into mine. It was the same conceptually, only now in the back yard not in a medical submarine.
A Halloween pirate patch arrived over his left eye to try and retrain his vision. His left periphery was not working, the reason his head sat at a permanent tilt.

"We're both pirates now," I said one day while he was focusing on his balance.

"Concentrating," he said, without laughter.

He held his thumb up and walked it out to the edge of where his vision gave up, then brought it back, reciting a math equation the whole way.

Then he juggled while doing the same problems again.

He got five steps on the slackline that day, and six the next.

By the end of the summer it would be fifteen, a moment where we danced around him like he had won the Olympics of life.

the petite yoga instructor we called Big Pearl so as not to confuse her with Rich and Katrina's daughter, ame every week now. Then the yogic flow routines Pearl would lead us through. 

We mostly failed at. Charlie had an excuse. Johnny and I were just embarrassingly bad. "This is of my culture," Qasim said, putting us all to shame.

There were other sports too. Archery was about finding the same anchor point with each draw. We went to the gym each day to strengthen his neck and body back after the atrophy of the hospital.

It would take dedication, repetition and time.

I called it a *everything-athalon*.

But then he started saying the questions that hurt.

"I cant wait to do a back flip again, ok maybe not backflips at first. But we can still hit rock jumps right?" 

I had no idea how to respond.

STATUS UPDATE 

Timescales continued normally for the rest of us while he seemed to accelerate.

"I feel like my third-grade self," he told me one morning, frustrated by how small things made him angry. A few weeks later: "I think I've graduated to fifth grade."

He was watching himself grow up from the inside, and reporting back.

You could hear it at the piano too. A music therapist came twice a week, and to both of our surprise he began learning entire compositions in minutes. He progressed fast, unburdened by habits and absorbing new things at speeds that startled everyone.

His mind held onto sounds and feelings, turning them into what came off the keys. His fingers and his neurons dancing as one, without a conscious thought between them.

It was a time of rapid growth, each day bringing another function back online.

I had traversed the realms, standing with Shadowlight as her human body had faded into the void, then reached back in with my son and was seeing him emerge from the same void step by step.

But now seemed like he was flying. 

Still, I had no idea Charlie would be giving his own TEDx talk in a few months.

--- Butterflies: coming of age

When school was out Millie began staying with me half of the time again, it was wonderful.

Monarchs migrated through our yard that summer landing on the piles of milkweed plants Johnny and bought, clearing out the nurseries around San Diego. Every time one flew into the yard they would yell "Jamie!"

Charlie and Millie would sit beside each other for long hours watching them in their cycles, keeping count. Millie knew which milkweed had which caterpillar on which day, and pointed them out to Charlie when he forgot. He would stare at their tiny wiggles, chomping away and growing with each day until they would attach themselves onto leaves, shed their skins and morph into beautiful chrysalises.

I watched from the kitchen and saw my own two butterflies forming.  Had I been focused too much on Charlie? I remember feeling the challenges of being the youngest like she was. But on top of that, this year everyone was focused Charlie.

My own sibling dynamic had been different. Jane made a point of humbling me physically and Ed built the Little Dipper and launched me off a cliff in it. But they did love me. 

It had just taken Millie and Charlie almost losing each other to pull them together this way. At night they cuddled in a pile of appreciation most siblings never arrive at. 

It must have been hard on her, but she wore it with grace.

Every once in a while Millie and I went out alone for ice cream, kind of like those long drives with my father thorough the forest.

"Have you ever wondered where gravity comes from, like not what it is but actually where it comes from?" she asked one night.

"I have no idea," I said. "We should ask grandpa."

While my world had focused in this rebirth, my daughter was becoming more than the youngest healer in the room, but an explorer of her own.

She always asked to bring a cone home for her brother.

WHAT WORKED

--- Community

One of the biggest medicines, like always, was community.

Charlie's friends arrived in groups of fourteen-and-fifteen-year-olds. I warned them he probably wouldn't stop talking once he got going. They sat and listened patiently through his lifetime of wonderings. Then one by one they told their own experiences with how they had each found out about the accident.

Each had received the terrible call, and had lived through not knowing if their friend was going to make it. They sat in the living room and cried in each other's embrace, then had a gentle slow nerf battle while Charlie awkwardly wore a ski helmet. At that stage I feared that any little bump to his head could be catastrophic.

A few times a week Kara and Diana came over and we played pantomime games in the living room, all five of us howling as Charlie acted out a fox or a refrigerator or a volcano. Millie always won, sending us into hysteria with her impressions. She was a natural actor, happy claiming "it came from my grandma!" Sometimes friends would join, Johnny would flop around on the floor like an idiot till someone yelled "FISH!"

There was community surrounding all of us. I needed it too, caretaking is hard.

When Charlie's persistence merged his memory loss, and perseveration to a crescendo and I had nothing left to meet it with, Johnny would step in and take the room. Rich would swing through and bbq up some chicken, and drop a sage thought here and there just when the moment was right.

Charlie had experienced joy and community all summer. But other families, friends of friends, had started reaching out for advice when someone they loved had fallen to a TBI. Qasim had told us how most people go through this depressed and alone, and the thought had imprinted on him.

"Dad you have a bunch of cameras, we could make something like our old home movies and share it so others can see hope." It came from an innocence as pure as the describing the white void, just a kid full of empathy.

I looked at Johnny, he turned toward me. "Let's do it brother, I am in it." Laura started raising funds and Hunter, a neighbored friend who had been our connection to the mountain, jumped in on production and we started filming his recovery like an expedition.

My whole life I had made films of adventure, searching for stories of what it means to be human with crews that had become like family all over the world. Now I was in the middle of the one that mattered most to me, and we had our team.

That was when I saw what had been around us. Pearl on the mat. Qasim at the slackline. Rich at the gate. Ed at the archery field. Deena calling from the mountains. Charlie's friends in the backyard. Kara, Diana, Johnny, Laura, Hunter, and most of all Millie — they were the handprints on Charlie's cave.

It had been the same for me, with everyone who had cared for me ten years earlier. And the image of Mike Coots riding in the heart of a wave with the bright yellow prosthetic had given me hope, and permission to believe my life could still be mine. He had unknowingly been one of the many handprints on the wall for me in my darkest moment of self doubt.

Now my boy wanted to pay that forward.

Just a few months before, I had been standing in an exhibit looking for what humanity was like thirty-one thousand years ago when I had received the call that began this all, trying to imagine the genesis of compassion and the story of Tebo1. Now I was living inside the very thing I had been looking for.

*Ecstasis per communitas.* The technology had not changed.


--- Ocean (Connection to nature, something bigger than your self)

But the oldest technology of all was not ours, it was Nature. This is what all my travels had shown me.

His doctors had advised to maximizing growth in the window of neuroplasticity, and to do that I went back through my own understanding of the doors that opened the mind.

The Maya overlook at dawn, the monkeys starting up somewhere below and the mother tree standing over all of it. Surfing each morning as the sun rose. The dunes with my kids. Nature had been my biggest door.

That summer I walked my son through the forest, the ocean, the desert, and the mountain. Each one a different filter for his growing mind.

We would stand every day on the cliffs overlooking the Pacific, our eyes gazing out to the horizon as the waves lapped toward us. Terry Sejnowski at the Salk had once told me the frequency of approaching waves could entrain brain waves the way binaural tones do. It was why I had played those frequencies around his head while he lay in the coma. Now the waves were doing it through our eyes.

These were the same cliffs where Jamie had transcended.

I put my arm around him as the waves approached. "You only get to choose what to do with them," I told him, repeating what Pohaku had shown me years before.

The fish became more active with the warming waters, and Charlie was noticeably good luck. First trip out was to a lake and a giant catfish found his line. He let it go.

"I am part of the flow of life!" he shouted through joyous laughter as it swam away.

In the mornings we would stand on the beach with our feet in the sand, using sand crabs to target the fish that mingled in the surf. The waves lapped toward us in visual frequencies, the entrainment of our own brain waves falling into sync. Nature was healing us.

Then out on the water. Yellowtail. White seabass. Johnny, a pro-level spearfisherman, prepared what we caught, and we lived off the bounty of the sea.

"I think this is the best summer of our lives," Charlie said to us around a bowl of ceviche.

--- Desert (memories)

As fall approached in we drove to the desert with the cooler temperatures.

The last time we had been together in lands like this was in the deserts of Oman, where I had searched for a story about the power of imagination that could pull metal from rock and launch our earliest civilizations. I had watched Millie get her face licked by a camel days before pulling a baby camel into the world with my own hands. Now I pulled into wash of Anza Borrego, our local wilderness hoping the core memories of a childhood were still beneath the surface.

Charlie helped me unload the camp chairs. Millie was already off on a hill, chasing something.

I took our guitars from the back and tuned them both the way I had tuned them with Jamie years before, the open drone Santi had started calling the *love tuning* whenever I had played it for Jamie.

Charlie's fingers found their placements. I told him jokingly Jamie must have left him something when she pushed him back through the door of existence. But I wasn't really joking. Every time he strummed, it felt like the source she always talked about was coming through his fingers.

The vast expanse, the rolling horizon, the smell of the campfire drew from the well of his brain a flood of remembering, like water hidden just beneath the surface.

"I remember being here, I remember shooting arrows with Milo and Derek." Milo was Derek's son and they had ran through these hills as six year old boys long ago.

Millie returned with some rocks she had collected and examined their morphology under a microscope. "I found a tiny geode!" she exclaimed with happiness.

The stars came out and the three of us watched them traverse the sky in our open desert, just as we had years before in the wake of other thresholds. The divorce, the leg, now Charlie's injury.

I looked at his hands as he strummed his guitar and saw the bracelet Deena had placed on his wrist. "Connections." The memories were still there, somehow locked beneath the severed wires. They just needed to be drawn back to the surface. [Note - anything powerful about how memories exist from a scientific/metaphysical perspective? Short and powerful?]

WHAT DID IT MEAN
--- Mountains (the conflict)

Most of all he yearned for the mountains. They had been sacred to us. But now they daunted me.

He wanted to run, jump, fly and one day ski again.

"I don't get it, Dad. I was doing back flips on skis one second, and the next thing I know it's summer and everyone is telling me I can't walk up the stairs alone."

When his teammates drove down from Mammoth to visit him he told everyone he'd be back on the snow by winter.

Knots tied in my stomach every time he said it. I didnt know how to respond and stayed quiet until the visit was over

At first I wanted to tell him yes, the way I always had, that he could do anything. Then the terrifying image of another accident consumed me. 

I told him, "I cant loose you again."

"So your saying I cant follow my passion anymore?"

"I know son, but your brain is different now the drs said these injuries build upon each other, one bad hit and imagine loosing everything you have worked so hard to get back?!  I am scared to see you jump in our back deck let alone on a mountain again."

He looked me with eyes like I was breaking him.

"Dad, how can you say no, look at your life?" He retorted.

"Look, I know you don't have a memory of the accident, and those terrible days in the hospital where we didn't know if you were going to live or die. But I do! The rest of us were the ones to live with that. And it almost crushed us! How can you ask us to go back to that!?"

Then silence. The hypocrisy of my fatherly concerns filled the void. 

Isn't that what I had done to my family ten years before, to the very person I was talking to?

A deep sigh. 

He had seen me chase giant waves, pack for the jungles and climb cliffs in search of lost cities with a prosthetic. Yet he and his sister had also been in the very same vehicle as me when I had been crushed in front of their eyes.  

"That wasn't fair, I am sorry." I broke the silence. 

"I am just afraid... But living in fear is not something I want either. We'll just have to find a new way to approach everything.  Let's take it one day at a time."  Who was I to say what was possible now?

Not long after he started working on new designs for helmets, each morning sitting at the table researching biomimetic materials like the ones I had studied in college.

The helmet he had worn that day had saved his life, yet the rotation forces of acceleration from the impact had still sheared his brain apart to a hairs breath of death.

"Viscoelastic designs, that is what I need!" he would murmur as he worked the problem.

He wasn't in school so he had plenty of time to dive into the research. I even found him a contact with Giro a leading helmet manufacture where he met their head engineer, who kindly showed him stacks of designs and went through Charlie's notebook where he had been keeping his research. It had an adorable handwritten "do not touch" sketch on the cover. It reminded me of the notebook I had carried around to pitch my dreams of the Valley of the Khans Project decades before.

"Passion is a word for one's life," he had once told me in the hospital, before he knew what a cow was. I was seeing it in full now.

--- Love (internal Charlie)

One day we were driving home when the sunset took over the entire sky, horizon to horizon, turning the ocean into something molten. Charlie went quiet for a long time, the machinery of his brain receiving that beauty.

Then, almost to himself:

"That's the feeling, Dad. That's what it feels like on the other side."

He picked up his phone and called his mother, told her he loved her, and hung up with tears running down his face. Not from sadness, he said. From the feeling of the love.

How could a sunset feel like love?

Nine years before, coming up out of the anesthesia of my own amputation, I had said the same impossible thing. *It's just love, and it wins.*

--- TEDx

His recovery of cognition reached past what any of us could have imagined.

Five months and Charlie returned to school. His broader community embraced him like a hero.

Everything seemed to be back.

"Recovering is not about coming back, it's about becoming," he would say with a happy look of confidence across his face.

They gave him earplugs at first because he couldn't filter what was around him. "It's like being constantly distracted by someone talking in the distance," he told me. The simple act of listening was more cognitively interesting than I had realized, sorting foreground from background, voice from noise.

His school was project-based, and helmets became the project.

"I think I want to be a materials scientist, gamer, entrepreneur, pro skier," he told me one night.

His grades started arriving, A+ A+ A+... His teachers and doctor both used the word *miracle.*

Then Charlie came to me one day and said he was planning on do a TEDx talk. A friend's parent had suggested it and he had taken writing it on all by himself.

It would be a good brain exercise I thought. On the other hand, he was deeply vulnerable, did I want him to feel the anxiety of being on the stage?

Everything about neuroplasticity seemed to be able flow and calm.

But the other part of this is that his identity, his core identity wasn't just about calm and flow. It was passionate.

Millie was backstage with a couple of their school friends, helping him run through his cue cards and his nerves.

I sat nervously with our crew of his recovery as he took the stage.

He introduced himself.

"I was told to start with a joke about your topic, so I asked Ai for a joke. And it replied

*There is absolutely nothing funny about brain injury. Under no circumstances should you tell a joke...*" Deadpan. "So I got nothing."

Perfect delivery.

"There is something human about surviving though."

The tension was broken.

He had been working on the talk for months on his own, intent on it being his own voice. He told us what he had found.

There were nearly three million traumatic brain injury patients in the US each year. He talked about the power of community, how no patient should have to go through it alone, how we could change the culture around it and the invisibility that it hid in.

And he told them about the bench, and the white void, and the feeling of love he had experienced as he was fighting for his life in a coma. "Reality doesn't happen to you, it's made through living."

When he finished, the room cheered. I fell out of my seat clapping the way I had for Millie's first time pitching. We all sat and cried ourselves into a puddle of gratitude, it was so far from where we had seen him.

--- Grieving his loss

Six months. When we had left the hospital the Dr had told me we had six months in which the brain would be the most neural plastic and open to recovery. 

At six months from his rebirth we filmed an interview. 

Charlie was radiant, strong, funny, sharp, unrecognizable from the boy who had been on life support half a year before. It felt triumphant.

But when the conversation moved to skiing, his expression changed.

I watched in real time as my son began to grieve the future he had built in his mind of becoming a pro skiier, something he had shared with all his teammates. He had worn soda tabs from red bull and Yerba matte, two sponsors he had dreamed of. His walls were covered in the posters of the best mountains, and he was actually competing on a team from a Mountain that had made Olympians.  But while most kids could feel invincible, some part of him had begun to recognize that risk was now different for him.  

Until now I had been keeping him from the mountains .  

I had blamed it on the idea that we needed to stay at a stable altitude with maximum oxygen through his recovery, which was true. But there had been this growing tensions beneath it for me . My heart ached at the idea of crushing his dreams, no matter what they were.  Who would want to tell their kid they cant be an astronaut if they belived they could to.  What if someone had told me I couldn't be an Explorer after my own transformations.

I understood his pain. The very first question that I had asked my Drs years ago after being crushed was actually if I would ever surf again. 

These things we did for fun, the ways in which we had played in nature, they were more than an activity. They were sacred to us, our identities, our relationships to the earth.  They fulfilled that deep part of our human condition, to find ways to connect with something bigger than us.

Then mid-thought, he stumbled through words, and out of the layers of his memory he recalled the exact moment that had drawn him to the mountains in the first place.

He was ten. We were on his first black diamond run. My brother Ed was there and others were watching as we dropped down the rocky chute under Chair One. He had faced it with us watching over him and we were all giggling all the way down.

"I think this has always just been about having fun with my dad," he managed to get out through a choked voice while wiping his eyes. "I just need to get back to that. We'll never lose that."

I was a wreck in the corner, trying not to ruin his words with my sobs. I looked up at Johnny, doing his best to hold the camera. He was there too.

The foundation of our joy had never been the sport. It was the love between our family in those landscapes, and everything built on top of it was only a filter through which we got to feel that love.

My wounds filled in, the guilt I had carried subconsciously was that I was the one who had brought him to the thing that nearly took his life. But facing the mountain was no longer about skiing. It had always been about love. How could we let fear win over love.

The next morning we packed the car. The snow had yet to fall. But it was time to go.

Over the years she had made me necklaces or bracelets to wear on my many expeditions, saying that when they fell off they had done their job of protecting me. Now she made them for both of us. She had her bestfriend's birthday party that weekend and stayed in San Diego.

We drove north on our sacred road, the same one we had taken every Friday that winter, and so many times before. The summer was gone and the green of the hills was replaced by sage and gold. The cycles turning.

When Mammoth appeared on the horizon, Charlie sat forward. No hesitation. Only a pull toward the place that had made him.

For me it was harder. We passed the airport where I had heard the ventilator through the phone. The river I had spent the winter on after Jamie died. Then we turned left to our home mountain.

We found Deena, Piper, and Owen and his dad Scott first. Then the patrollers, the people who had saved his life. He had thanked them in his meditations each night for six months, having never met. Now standing in front of them.

They brought him into their headquarters and showed him how that day had unfolded from their side. Oxygen on the way down. The life flight plane already on the tarmac. Critical moves in the first moments where every second had counted. "When it's this serious, they often don't make it back," one of them said. They had done it all just right. Their training and execution may have been the difference in the margin.

We went to his team locker room, empty in the off-season, to retrieve the things that he had left there from the day of his accident. It was quiet and dark.  He stood in the doorway and looked at the space where his old life had happened. By this point he understood the re-injury of a brain injury would be worse, but hear the stories from the ones that were there he knew that this locker room had not changed but he had. 

"Recovering is not about coming back, it's about becoming," he said to himself, more quietly than he had in the past.

Then we took the gondola to the summit. The mountain in summer was a different country, granite, wildflowers in the cracks, the whole range visible. Charlie sat on a rock at the top. I sat next to him. At that altitude there were still a few small patches of snow. He reached down and brought the cold to his lips.

After six months of building from the status of a newborn, Deena's visualization from those first days in the hospital had come true.

That night, at a restaurant in town with the small crew who had driven up with us, Rich, Johnny, Hunter, we ate and laughed and recounted stories of the adventures of the last six months. 

But under it all you could tell Charlie was still processing the day, how close he really had come to death, those who had come together for him, and the change he had to accept. 

The small talk stoped, I couldn't fake some smooth answer that would make it all better and say it would all be fine. It was time to be real, with him and myself.

"This is a hard part of growing up," I said. "Grief. There are stages to it, shock, denial, anger and acceptance and you are in those now. It's uniquely hard when it's some part of yourself that dies. I know. And we are here to help you face that together, but it's up to you to choose what grows from this."

He listened for a while. We talked more as he worked through his experience. You could see a transformation in his eyes. 

Eventually he spoke.

"Maybe you have to accept loss to see what's on the other side," he smiled.

As I got the check, I noticed that the energy at the table had lighted. It was as if by saying it he 

"well if I cant join team, maybe we can become powder chasers."

The Guru of Thieves had said it once to me, on the banks of a river that had been carrying ash and prayer for three thousand years. "To be alive is to cycle through the changes. The only way to cycle through them is to accept when one phase dies so the next can be born."



We walked out into the valley after dinner. The sky was dark, and then it wasn't, a brightness building along the horizon so intense we mistook it for the dawn. Then the buck moon emerged, enormous and unhurried, and its light poured across the valley floor and turned the sagebrush silver. A herd of cows walked through our foreground, and somewhere in the distance, wolves started howling.

Many years before, in a ger, Amaraa had told me the Mongolian origin story. A blue wolf came down from the sky to a sacred mountain and met a white deer born from the earth, and from their union the first clans were born.

I thought of the duality of my kids, the winter's wolf moon that Millie had been born to, the late summer sun that Charlie had been born under. The blue birthmark that had been passed down had faded into them, waiting to return in the next generation to come. Now wolves were singing in the valley in front of us, somewhere in the dark, the same primal echo returning time and time again.

The sound of their calls rolled across the open land, one voice joining another until the whole basin bathed in it. We sat in our camp chairs and listened. Then spontaneously we all started howling back. The voices of our little crew climbed into the night and mixed with the wolves and the moonlight and the mountain air, and whatever any of us had been carrying poured out and dissolved.


Later that night Charlie journaled. The entry ended with a single line.

*I love you old Charlie, but it's time for me to keep moving and finally I can let go.*

--- The river

Before we left we drove down to the river. The water wound through the valley floor, rolling over rocks and bending into deep pools where trout had spawned through the cold months. Fall had left the grass thick on both banks, and bugs suspended above the current in bright clusters, catching the early light.

I handed Charlie the rod that I had earned after a summer long job I had had at his age. We pulled on our waders and stepped in.

I had been searching for a long time. 

Asking the same question everywhere I had been, though I didn't always recognize it.

At a one level it's clear was searching for whats lost.  Lost tomb, Lost leg, Lost Cities, lost love.  But whats on the other side of loss is not just something to get back.  The duality of loss and growth coexists as equal pairs of each other. Yin and yang. And to pass through the eye of that duality we are given a chance to discover who we are.  Each time we do, in the dissolution of what we had been we can touch source of whatever it is at the bottom of everything.  The source.

This is what had been revealed.

But standing in the river with my son, I understood it had never been hidden.

The Maya had called it *k'uh.* The Mongolians *hiimori.* Pohaku called it *mana.* Every culture had reached for the same space and found a different word. What its source was, I would never really know.

I suspected, as a kid in my father's car, listening to him explain the Big Bang as best as he could but told we had to accept no one could really see what happened before.

Yet it could be felt.

It was my mother who had first shown me how, at a piano bench, stopping my hands to tell me to feel the invisible. While my father searched the space between matter and antimatter. My mother had found it in the space between the notes. It took me most of my life to see they were describing the same thing.   

But it was not just about atoms and galaxies. It was in the consciousness that gives matter life. It was the capacity to care, to forgive, to choose creation in the face of destruction. 

It was love. 

I had felt it in the friendship of my life. In the gesture of a nomad passing a silver bowl of fermented milk to a stranger. In a boy who did twelve breaths when they asked for ten, and the girl who pulled her brother's shoes off and pressed his feet into the grass.  

It was ancient. Older than handprints in the cave in borneo.  And each time we came together, at some level it was because of the capacity to care that was within in us. Love. But so was the capacity for the opposite. And the results of that struggle of duality were the stories I had seen across the world.  The door in the mountain that I had stood in front of years go, the ancient portal to the versions of humanity, it *was* in us.

But it was more than the love between things or beings. Even in the moments of heart ache, of love lost I had felt the resonance. It was bigger. Like the sunset that made charlie cry. 

Shadowlight had revealed a deeper resonance, moving water with it. "We are mostly water" she would say.  

The resonance, om, always arriving at my thresholds. 

It had wrapped its hands around my head, I had passed through me as I chanted beside Shadowlight when she transcended into the beyond. And then it was there again, as Charlie was reborn out of the beyond.  Each time om had been the resonance that I could feel beneath everything.

The sound had passed through me, and now to my kids. The sound of the river, the sound of all of this, was pressing against our bodies as Charlie and I waded into the gentle currents.

He cast his line. The current took it past the rocks and through the morning light. I looked at the cold water, at my two feet, one flesh and one carbon, and at his legs that six months ago couldn't hold him at all. We watched the river move over us the way it moves over everything, with the patience it had always had. It had been there for me so many times before.

The seasons would come and go, the cycle would turn and return in an infinite spiral of vortices, carrying what is given, returning what is needed.

I had drawn many names through my own life: *Xiao poo. NaChing. El Robotico. Luhk Dolen Katau. Albert.* And I believe there will be many more. That was the lesson of impermanence. But every ground I stood on had given back a different name, and none of them were wrong. My parents had given me the first. *Yu-Min*, citizen of the universe. Belonging to everything. It took most of my life to find out what that meant. 

And now I knew that my children would begin to draw their own names of life, each one a version of the infinite possibilities within them.  

Standing there, I heard every voice, every version of the boy beside me and the one that lives within me. 

We watched.

The fly floated along the water as swirls rolled beneath it. Its tiny tuft of feather and fur lay just above the surface, in the plane between two realms.

The light of the sun cast through the shadows of the dark water.

Then a flash. His line went taut.

Charlie looked at me with excitement. For a moment I saw Millie at three, the same astonishment on her face when her first fish hit the line.

There was something on the other end.

CHAPTER 20
The Aya Huma

Months had passed. Time worked differently. I played with my kids in the warm embrace of a San Diego summer, wading into the Pacific each day and bathing in its salty wash. Millie and I would play catch in the evening sunset. Charlie and I went to the archery fields on Sundays, where his coach, a descendant of the Maya himself, told us, "Archery is what everyone's ancestors did, wherever you came from."

In the evenings after the kids had gone to bed I walked out onto my balcony and looked into the stars. I was still lingering in the long, intense meditation of what had just happened.

Beneath the daze in those first weeks, I lay shattered. For as long as I could remember I had homed in on one belief over everything, that the mind was our superpower, that we imagine our realities into being. I had rebuilt my own body on that idea. I had stood in front of ten thousand strangers at a commencement speech and called it the engine of everything. And then the person who had taught me that everything could be magic got sick, and all the love and all the imagination in the world could not change its course. On those dark morning drives, Charlie had asked me whether she might fix the cancer with her mindset. I had wanted so badly to tell him yes, but she had died anyway. So what was any of it worth? For the first time since I was a boy, I did not know what I believed. Had it all been a big facade?

Then Derek called. He had always been the one to remind me "the universe wants us to play," maybe especially in the fog of pain and grief. At our first Burn he had walked the playa in a lab coat and goggles, the same wide smile of curiosity and wonder on his face the whole time. Now he was devising a trip to think toward the future and celebrate Jamie, out to Roatán, the old pirate island off Honduras where Blackbeard himself had once hidden. "A trip of brotherhood," he suggested, to help us in mourning within a circle of close friends.

Leo with his fro. Qasim with the perfect playlist. Eli. Luke. Tim, and I with guitars. Everyone had their own gifts and archetypes, archetypes that could have lived in any age of our human story. They had each been a part of my life for a long time, and had been close to Jamie. We playfully called ourselves a *sacred society,* eight of us, with Shadowlight as the center. Jamie's old name for the three of us in the desert turned out to have been waiting for this, the eight turned sideways making the symbol of the Infinite. The Infinite Vortices, a band of cosmic pirates on a pirate island, the way she had liked to think of us.

---

Derek, whom I had nicknamed Pythagoras by that time, described a project he had begun calling the Source Library. "The Renaissance had been sparked by a small group of people rediscovering and translating one lost tradition of esoteric thought, a single corpus of Greek texts to spark an intellectual revolution that changed everything," he told us. "With the tools we have now we could do it at a scale that tapped into every civilization at once, and find the connections where the ancient thoughts converged. A second renaissance!"

I had spent my life pointing the newest tools at the oldest ground, satellites at a buried tomb, lasers down through the jungle canopy at cities a thousand years gone. This was the same hunt, aimed higher. Not lost cities anymore. Lost thoughts.

"Ask what it means to be human and alive, and the world answers in seven thousand voices," Wade Davis had once said, "and these collectively comprise our human repertoire for dealing with all the challenges that will confront us as a species."

We gathered around a long table on the patio in the afternoons, laptops and books open, the wind turning the pages, and it felt like the council of a ship sailing into the future.

From neuroscience to computer science to the arts, we all brought different perspectives, but everyone at their core was just an explorer of the human condition, and the deeper we went, the more the questions we were chasing turned out to be the ones we were already living. Not ancient or modern, but timeless, surfacing in every age because they belong to all of them.

Why had so many cultures used the same word for breath as for spirit, for the thing that arrives on the first inhale and leaves on the last? What did they believe waited on the other side of it? Why had nearly every culture pictured a life as something spun or woven in advance, the Greek Fates at their thread, the Norse Norns weaving at the roots of the world tree, kismet, ming, the shape of a life set before it was ever lived? And why had many drawn time as a spiral, a wheel that comes back around rather than a line. If that was true death and birth were always tied.

How about the question I had been asking for so long. Imagination. The Sufis had mapped a whole imaginal world and called imagination an organ of sight, as real as the eye. Tibetan monks named the first stage of their highest practice the phase of imagination, and spent years picturing a deity until they became it. Culture after culture had sworn that the mind makes what is real. But they were all just people too, who got sick and buried loved ones. Had they ever felt as betrayed by their own philosophies as I did in that moment?

We were charting three thousand years of people doing exactly what we were doing right then, grieving and searching for understanding together.

---

Then the wind would shift and someone would yell to swim and we would close the laptops, drink some tinctures, and dive in.

The ocean was warm and full of light. We swam amongst dolphins and drifted over coral of impossible colors. Then, near a sunken wreck, a statue of a goddess rose up at us out of the blue. Probably a prop dropped from a dive boat. Or maybe not. No one cared, because it was Jamie, appearing the way she often did now. "Loud and clear!" we cried, "We hear you loud and clear!" Then we bobbed on the surface laughing in gratitude.

As the days passed we cycled between charting the next renaissance and falling in with the island's own eclectic crew, musicians mermaids and fishermen who kept turning up and becoming our friends. It felt like more of Jamie's handiwork, the way people and moments kept arranging themselves around us. We fished with a dreadlocked Dutchman. We jammed at a house party where Luke let it all out. On the quieter nights we sat with our new island friends on a dock under the stars, lines in the water, playing music softly while fish gathered to dance and never bit. I went back to the guitar tuning Jamie and I had found, one I now played only in the most intimate settings. I wept, and the others wept with me.

As the week closed we raised a glass and called out as one a new slogan we had landed upon for our sacred society.

*Ecstasis per communitas.*

Ecstasy through community. The state, the ancients had said, you could not reach alone. Community had been our technology of transformation this time.

The grief did not leave, but it was wrapped in a blanket of love that would allow time to do its job.

---

I rushed home the next morning, barely making my flight in a flurry of island motorcycle taxis and pleas with baggage check tickets, just in time to take Charlie and Millie to the mountains. Charlie's ski team was now in full competition mode and Millie was landing her jumps at the rink.

We were making the eight hour drive to Mammoth every single weekend, but we had found a cadence with it that felt right. I needed the familiar constant ritual of that drive up the 395 during that time.

---

Then the invite to climb came.

The tenth annual fundraising campaign for the Range of Motion Project. ROMP provided prosthetics to those who don't have access across rural Ecuador and Guatemala. The mountain was Cayambe, the only snow-capped peak the line between north and south passes through.

It had been so many years since I had seen that man on crutches in the jungles and felt the shame of privilege, now I could do something about it. But also, at nearly nineteen thousand feet it was among the furthest points from Earth's axis of rotation. Thus at its peak it spins faster than nearly anywhere else on the planet. I decided I would scatter Shadowlight there. She could dance through the cosmos.

I had not gone through life alone, and I would not climb this alone either. While I was at Jamie's side, my small team that had solidified over the years had held the rest of my life together. Johnny had been with me on the North Shore, and he would come to film the climb, though by then he was more brother than director of photography.
Laura, an old friend from the Challenged Athletes Foundation, had signed on as my partner to develop projects not long before, the one who ran everything so that it worked. She had the gift of a bottomless interest in people. We had started calling her Coach. It was Coach who found the ROMP climb, and who understood before I did that it would be an important climb for me, for many reasons.

---

At the ROMP headquarters in Quito I finally met Carlos in person. He was twenty, indigenous Ecuadorian, soft-spoken, with an infectious smile. We had been paired as rope partners, and together with support, we would attempt a climb to the top of the equator to raise funds to help the organization's goals from a global community.

Carlos had spent three years on crutches before the organization had donated a prosthetic to him earlier that year. He owned no phone, so we had spoken only once.

"Hello - Welcome - to - Ecuador," he said, a word at a time.

Then we hugged, and it felt like we had known each other for years.

Inside the clinic, an assortment of used and donated prosthetic components lined the shelves, each one waiting to change a life. A running blade. A toddler's foot the size of my palm. Inanimate things that would one day take on lives of their own. On the wall across from them was a mural of a tree built from the painted thumbprints of everyone who had ever made the climb.

I pressed my thumb against the paint and then against the wall, and left it there with the others.

Over the days of acclimatizing Carlos and I became brothers. We bought matching alpaca jackets in a local market and wore them everywhere. At some point I realized his name was the Spanish version of my own son's, Charlie, and he had the same temperament too, positive and earnest and goofy all at once.

We met the other climbers, apart from our local guides, mostly amputees from around the globe. One had survived a mass shooting in Aurora. Several had lost their limbs in car accidents. There were war veterans and Paralympians. For most of my life I had been the only amputee in the room. Here I was surrounded.

One by one each of them told their story in front of others who carried their own, and in that room we could share an empathy that reached behind every mask.

ROMP itself had been built by two brothers, David and Greg Krupa, who moved to Quito to start it from nothing. David, an amputee himself, was rugged and steady, the anchor of the whole operation. But even he, the stoic leader, was feeling the moment.

"Ten years ago, ROMP was founded... to make grown men cry," he said, finishing his welcome speech through choked emotions of gratitude.


---

Ten days later we drove to the base of Cayambe. As our caravan approached the village at the foothills, we passed an amputee on the streets. He was panhandling to passing cars and was stunned as our crew of one-legged athletes jumped out to greet him, asking for his contact information then sending him with the clinic nurse directly to get a leg made. He had survived on crutches for eight years following an accident similar to mine, unable to work.

We drove into the clouds until the buses could go no further, then transferred into smaller trucks. As we piled out, Johnny stopped to send up a drone. Everything was going great until it tumbled from the sky. On recovering it we realized it had reached the equator, the 0.000 line of latitude, and something had scrambled its systems as it crossed the line. Marveling at the invisible yet real divide between North and South, we heard the cries of Andean condors circling above us. We were standing on the middle plane of the world, the invisible seam where its two halves met, belonging to neither and to both.

Carlos and I climbed into the back of a truck filled with our packs for the final bumpy stretch. As we drove to the mountain refuge I pulled out my guitar and we made up a song and sang with joy.

*Dos Hermanos Estás Muy Locos.* Two crazy brothers.

As we passed the treeline and rose above the clouds, the mood changed. We fell silent before the vastness, range after range undulating into the distance like the swells of a great sea.

The truck reached its stopping point at the base of the glacier. Carlos and I jumped out to gaze at the peak, unobscured by clouds. The glacier reflected the sun like a lighthouse beacon. In the presence of the mountain's enormity a great wave of vulnerability swelled through my soul. I looked over to see Carlos had started weeping, and then I did. He put his arm around me and I put mine around him, and we stood there with the flood gates of our emotions opened. We had no shared language, only a shared humanity and a shared journey, and all the walls came crumbling down at once.

I cried for all I had lost and all I had been given. For the former versions of myself that were now memories. The child me. The student me. The partnered me. The fathered me. I cried for all who had donated to help amputees they would never meet, for Jamie and the love in the room as she took her final breaths, for the spirit that had merged with all of this, and for everything still ahead. I cried like an idiot, no verbal explanation needed.

A year before, Carlos had lived on crutches and had never seen snow. Now he stood on two legs near the top of the world. We had come from different worlds, mine of privilege, his of the earth. He had been born and raised in the jungle, and his father had taught him to hunt with a spear as a boy. I couldn't know what he was feeling, but while my life washed over me I knew a lifetime was washing over him too.

Dave popped out to help us grab our bags and stopped to put his arms around us. "I told you we would break you."

We wiped our tears with laughter and made our way to the stone cabin with its bunk beds and battered windows.

---

The team huddled for a few days, letting our bodies acclimate to the sixteen thousand feet of elevation below us, venturing out from the mountain refuge to practice glacier climbing with crampons and ice axes, singing along to my guitar each night, reading notes of encouragement from those who had come before us on prior climbs.

On the day we were due to begin the push to the summit, Dave gave a speech in our tiny stone hut. "There will be a day," he said, "where we look at this time and think it was unthinkable that there wasn't care for everyone who needed it. I can imagine that day." He spoke about the very first ROMP climb he and his brother had created ten years before. That group had trained and waited just as we had. But the night of the climb, a huge storm had come through, stopping them in their tracks. What mattered, he said, was that "they were all in it together."

We were slated to begin at ten that night. The glacier becomes unstable under high equatorial sun, so the safety window is in the dark. After a restless nap we woke at nine, ate dinner, filled our water bottles. As we sent our last messages on the satellite phone, the weather turned. Wind began pounding the windows. Eighty-mile-per-hour gusts rattled the small stone building and howled through the stove pipe. We delayed.

The hours ticked by. I checked my bag to make sure Shadowlight's ashes were there.

The storm kept growing. Around midnight, two hours past our departure time, one of the Andean guides came in. "Guys, come on down. We're going to have a team meeting."

They had climbed partway up to check conditions. The winds were overwhelming and the snow was wet. The summit attempt was off. We would reassess at three in the morning.

Stunned silence. The room was full of bodies that had trained for a year for this moment, tapped their friends and family to support the fundraiser.

I thought about Dave's speech. "What matters is we're all in it together." I picked up my guitar.

The wind howled again like a wolf climbing through the chimney. The windows rattled. The lights blinked on and off. I started playing. The war veterans. Carlos. The Paralympians. The Ecuadorian prosthetist who was climbing for her patients. The brothers who had built ROMP from the ground up. We started singing.

*Lean on me, when you're not strong. I'll be your friend. I'll help you carry on.*

As cheesy as it sounds, that image sticks with me, our little group of amputees and friends in a battered stone hut at seventeen thousand feet on the equator, singing *Lean on Me* as a blizzard of eighty-mile-per-hour winds swirled around us. The wolf in the chimney couldn't get in.

---

Around three in the morning I woke to use the bathroom and stepped outside. Calm. The stars were out. The storm had passed entirely.

Thirty minutes later we loaded our packs and mounted our crampons by headlamp and began up the first set of rocky escarpments. The cold was bitter, the darkness absolute. Then dawn's first light peeked over the horizon, and we saw that the storm had dumped a mass of snow across the mountain. In the distance, the peaks of Cotopaxi and Chimborazo rose out of the clouds.

As we climbed straight up the side of the volcano, I heard Carlos whispering a mantra with every footstep.

*Si se puede, si se puede, si se puede.*

Yes, I can. Yes, I can. Yes, I can.

Nearly eighteen thousand feet now. I was near the front of the rope line, eager to reach the summit before the sun got too high. But our guide pulled up short, concerned by a vast wind drift. He started digging a test pit to see what lay beneath. Sheets of ice layered beneath heavy blankets of powder from the oscillation of storms and sun that had come before. Significant avalanche risk. We tried another direction. More of the same.

We all looked at each other. The golden rule of climbing is that you must respect the mountain. The mountains will be here long after we are gone, as Maya had said years before. They were there after Gil died. They would still be here if we all perished in an avalanche. We had climbed as high as we could.

Any disappointment disappeared when I looked at Carlos. He was sitting in the snow with his pack down. He pulled a few feathers from the top pouch, handed one to me and Dave, and held one up to the sky.

"Who needs legs when you can fly?" he said. Johnny, who had been filming, stopped and wrapped his arms around us.

I took Shadowlight's ashes from my pack. I opened the bag and held it into the wind. The wind took her. She lifted across the high snow into the air, scattering across the spine of the equator, distributing herself in the same motion across both hemispheres. Then she was gone.

We had danced across the universe one more time.

---

We made our way down to a small flat in the glacier, away from the avalanche risk. As the team pulled out food and water to snack on, a snowball from Dave smacked Carlos in the side of the head. He looked shocked for a second, then started laughing. He grabbed a fistful of snow and threw it back, missed and hit Johnny right in the face. I joined in. So did all the others. A massive, joyful snowball fight on the side of a glacier at over seventeen thousand feet, supported by thousands from around the globe in an effort to raise funds for those who needed help.

As we descended we looked back. Right where we had cut off our climb, a small avalanche had rumbled down from the peak as the glacier warmed in the sun.

I had come up the mountain doubting everything, and it answered by refusing to let me control a single thing. Not the storm. Not the summit. Not the snow that would have buried us if we had pushed on. I could no more will my way to the top than I could have willed the cancer out of Jamie's body. And that, I understood on the way down, had never been the point. Imagination had never been a way to bend what happened. It was the way I met it.

Eventually we reached the trucks and drove toward the foothills. We stopped at the equatorial line and everybody jumped out for a photo. Right before the flash, one of the ROMP organizers named Taylor, who had lost her leg as a child, called out for everyone to turn around and mooned the camera. We laughed and complied.

As we milled about in the moments after, an older Ecuadorian man from the refuge walked straight toward me. He was weathered and sunbaked in the way of mountain people, and held a wildly colorful and intricately knitted yarn mask with two faces in his hand. I thought he was trying to sell it.

"I don't have any money on me," I said apologetically in clumsy Spanish.

"No, no. For you," he said, placing it into my hands.

"Why are you giving this to me?"

He pointed up to the mountain.

"Recuerda."

*Remember the mountain.*

Then he walked away without asking for anything in return.

I was left stunned, on the exact line of the equator, the line between North and South, and this mask had appeared in my hands.

*Why?* Maybe he had seen that I had told my crew to leave the satellite link we had brought for the community to use as a resource. I rejoined the group and asked what I had been given.

*Aya Huma,* Carlos said. The conquistadors had renamed it the Diablo Huma, the two-faced ghost, but its real name was older than that. Aya meant *spirit* in the indigenous Quechua language. Huma meant *leader.*

"It's the protector spirit of life's rites of passage," Dave translated Carlos's words for me. "A symbol from the culture of this land. Two faces. Good and bad. Past and future. Shadow and light. All one and the same."

Dave pulled up a search on his phone, the image was a spirit with this duality mask and a whip in its hand, "To remind us to dance through the duality."

I stood there holding it, and the name finally reached me. Shadow and light. Shadowlight. She had spent her dying teaching me this exact thing, that the dark and the luminous are one but you can hold both and keep dancing.

On the flight home I watched the mountains pass below. I would be shooting arrows with Charlie and cheering at softball games with Millie within hours of landing. I wondered how I would tell them about the snowball fight we had just had on the line of magnetic duality, while spinning faster than anyone alive.

The plane crossed the equator.

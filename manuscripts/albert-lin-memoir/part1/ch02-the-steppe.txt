CHAPTER 2
The Steppe

Long before I lost a part of my body, I went looking for a missing part of my roots.

The plan was ridiculous.

It was 2006, a decade before the accident. I was a twenty-five-year-old graduate student, wandering with curiosity. Whatever life I was going to live hadn't started yet. But it was about to. And it was going to happen on the back of a horse in Mongolia, because of a birthmark my grandfather told me about in a tiny apartment in Hong Kong.

I had never quite fit anywhere, the wrong kind of kid in England, the wrong kind of teenager for Silicon Valley, the youngest in a family that was fracturing in ways I didn't see. A year before, my grandfather had said we might have roots in "the North." It wasn't much to go on. But it was enough.

I scraped together funds from odd jobs. Campus mail delivery, construction work, writing the engineering department newsletter. I needed just enough to buy a plane ticket to Beijing and a train ticket to the North from there.

The plan: I would take the Trans-Mongolian Railway to Ulaanbaatar, buy a horse, ride into the wilderness to find a nomadic camp that would take me in, live with them until I had learned what it was that lay behind my grandfather's words. Then ride back, sell the horse, and fly out. Ridiculous. I had a handheld GPS, my journal, and one change of clothes in a tiny backpack. "Travel light so the wind can catch me" was my internal motto. Somewhere in the prep I had read that under an ancient Mongolian code called the *Yassa*, failure to offer a fellow nomad food and hospitality was punishable by death, a reassuring custom given my graduate student lifestyle.

My research advisor, Mark Meyers, a former Brazilian military officer who told stories of trekking the Amazon with the same ease he described wave-particle duality, was a blueprint of what I wanted to be. A distinguished professor with the untamed air of a mad scientist, the endearing kind, he was the closest thing I'd met to a Renaissance man, a scholar of science who had also published a book of poems and written a science fiction novel where microscopic life from beyond the stars woke inside ancient Maya prophecy and threatened to end the world. Eclectic himself, and sympathetic to my existential angst, he agreed to let me take the summer off as long as I wasn't drawing a stipend. Although, he was quick to remind me, "The enemy never sleeps," referring to research like it was a war. My best friend Derek's response was to track down obscure videos of a Nobel Prize-winning physicist who had become obsessed with Mongolian throat singing and nod encouragingly.

---

In the summer of 2006 I touched down in Beijing. The first moments were disorienting. I had been there once at six years old. My memories of it were more like flashes of a dream. It was enormous and confronting, a city of concentric circles around the forbidden palace. Within an hour a taxi driver scammed me out of a hundred dollars, a substantial chunk of my funds. But from the beginning I found myself amongst the ancient streets teeming with an energy that felt familiar, my mother's language and food stands that smelled like my childhood.

The train station was its own city under a roof, loud and endless, every sign a wall of Chinese characters I couldn't effectively read. Jet-lagged and adrift, I guessed my way from counter to counter until somehow I had a ticket for a bunk in a four-berth cabin headed north. In that chaos, it was a monumental victory.

I found my cabin early and walked down the narrow interior passage that led to it, my backpack slung over my shoulder. A large man barged through with two huge bags of rice, one under each arm, and slammed into me. Rancid cigarette breath washed over us as he screamed for me to get out of his way. I didn't understand his curses well enough to take offense, but his anger was obvious. He threw the bags of rice into my berth and my heart sank. *Would this be my cabin mate for the next two days?*

Two Mongolian women were right behind him, looking equally shocked. I stepped aside and he glared, ready to fight, but finding only confusion on my face. Then in an equal flurry, he left.

The women stared at me as I sat down, apologizing in broken English for their companion, who was actually their translator in Beijing. "We know you're not from Mongolia," one said with a laugh, "you didn't fight back!"

Their names were Bayarsaikhan, Bibi for short, and Inga.

The train slowly left the station. I stuck my head out the window and watched fragments of the Great Wall go by. Eventually the forested mountains surrounding Beijing opened up until there was nothing but vastness in every direction.

*Horses graze the green grasslands, fields of yellow flowers stretch across the horizon lightly peppered with compounds and smoke stacks in the distance,* I wrote in my small, leather-bound pocket journal that I carried everywhere. We were headed into the Gobi desert.

Bibi and Inga had traveled to buy medicine for Bibi's mother and bags of high-quality rice not imported into Mongolia. Their elegant high cheekbones and features looked slightly Russian, their words sounded like whispering from the sides of their teeth, their personalities exuded grace and strength. Bibi, I learned, ran one of the largest news organizations in Mongolia. Years later she would serve in Parliament, but that was yet to come. For now we were just travelers on a legendary railway, headed north.

Over boiled eggs, tea, and a game of cards, I told them what had brought me there. My grandfather, my mother's father, whom I had only really met in adulthood. He had pointed to his lower back and told me our family all had the "blue spot," a birthmark that fades in childhood. A sign, he said, of an ancient heritage. I was on that train searching for roots.

Bibi's face lit up. "Then you are Mongolian! We are family!" She laughed and squeezed my arm. "We stamped you!" Inga nodded approvingly, slapping my lower back.

I told them my plan to buy a horse and live with the nomads.

"That's nice," Bibi said, her expression shifting. Her skepticism barely masked.

We became friends quickly. They tried to tell me what to expect, and I asked questions about things I had read about their country in books. Hot water from a little coal boiling station at the end of the train car steeped our tea while we taught each other card games from our respective worlds and laughed at my feeble attempts to learn basic Mongolian phrases.

"Saaaain - baaain - uuuuuu... Baa - yaaar - lalaaaaa...," *hello and thank you*, I would repeat to the steady metronome of the train tracks clacking under us.

As we entered the dustier, northern parts of the desert, the air grew cold. I put on my puffy down jacket, and the women joked that I "looked like a marshmallow." As night approached, the conductor showed us how to convert our seats into bunk beds, the upper level folding down like hidden ironing boards. I dozed off in the small space on the top bunk, filled with excitement but also hugging my bag, having read something about thieves who duplicated conductors' keys and filled cabins with sleeping gas.

Hours later I awoke to the train slowing in the middle of a vast, empty darkness around midnight. As we approached the border, a line on a map drawn from ancient cycles of war, speakers blasted patriotic Chinese marching band music as huge spotlights shone down on us. The contrast had the air of a theatrical stage with nothing but the infinite black of the night-swept Gobi to accentuate the moment.

Tall guards with long bayonet rifles lined the platform in rigid perfection. They entered and searched, the butts of their weapons pressing up ceiling panels to probe for stowaways.

Some passengers, presumably without the right papers, were thrown from the train screaming. I watched as they were left stranded with piles of their belongings to the backdrop of loudspeakers blaring into the void that surrounded them.

When the guards reached our cabin, they pushed in. Nervously, I handed them my passport and said, "I'm an American." Two of them flipped through my identification seemingly unconvinced, looking at my Asian face that could have been one of the passengers they had just thrown into the desert. "Call the embassy, American!" I blurted out in English again, restraining the rusty Mandarin I had retained from my youth, afraid to further fuel their suspicion. Eventually, they became uninterested and continued their search, checking Bibi and Inga's documents, then moving on to the next cabin.

The hour dragged into two, while mechanics painstakingly changed the train's wheel gauges on each car to match the track standards of the lands we were entering, a physical echo of the Cold War. Inga stood up, grabbed my green puffy jacket from the rack, and wrapped herself in it. "Now I have big jacket!" she said, with a hearty laugh, and walked out to smoke under the cold desert sky.

*Tonight I see the other side, both of what I am and what I have taken for granted,* I journaled at the small desk above the heater. *As the guards left, I felt a strange awkwardness, as if I didn't belong in my own identity. Now the English words that leak out of my mouth seem ridiculous. As if I am trying to assure everyone I am American. Maybe I am trying to reassure myself... what am I, really?*

Then without fanfare, the purgatory ended. A stamp of papers, a train refitted with a new stance, and we began rolling. That's how I entered Mongolia.

By the time we crossed, the horse plan had been firmly vetoed. Bibi grew serious. "Albert, you will die if you do this. Mongolian horses are very wild. They will leave you in the desert and you will die. It's very cold at night." She paused. "My uncle has many horses. And in a few days, maybe my brother can take you to the countryside, he is between jobs and was thinking of going. You can live your crazy dream!" she said with a laugh.

I didn't know how to respond. Using a brick-like cell phone, she had already called her brother. His name was Amaraa.

As the train slowed into Ulaanbaatar a throng of kids jumped aboard, working the cars for pockets while the conductor cursed and kicked at them. I later learned they lived in the tunnels under the city to keep warm during the brutal nights. Caught in a current of movement through the narrow compartment, I merged with a sea of old dark knit suits and heavy wool coats while carrying the heavy rice bags of my new friends on my shoulder. Suddenly, the piercingly cold air of the outside met me at the train's door.

Amaraa was waiting in the crowd, his broad shoulders softened by a genuine smile and a hat that read "California" across its brim. Bibi pointed to him from behind me as the flow of disembarkation squeezed us out the train door like dough in a noodle press. He grabbed the bags of rice with arms the size of tree trunks, helped Bibi and Inga toward his truck, and told me to join them.

"You're from America?" he asked in a deep raspy voice. Followed by, "Okay, let's go," before I could reply.

With no actual plan other than the dream of a horse, and a defiant trust that the journey would present itself, I threw my bag into his weathered Land Cruiser and we were off.

---

At first Ulaanbaatar reminded me of Moscow when I had visited as a kid after the coup in 1991. Gray apartment buildings rose over concrete squares. The police wore Russian-style uniforms with tilted berets. Out the window, I saw a billboard for a movie about Genghis Khan. To my surprise, one of the men on it was Amaraa.

We arrived at a huge, four-story home in the middle of the city and started to unload the bags. The house was full of commotion. Kids darting between rooms, grandmothers kissing the women. Everyone was speaking in Mongolian while I smiled and bobbed my head politely. Bibi's mother served dumpling soup she had prepared to welcome her daughter home. It was the best thing I had eaten in a week, hot broth and lamb and dough, and my body took it the way ground takes rain. She was small in stature but large in presence, the clear matriarch. On the wall behind her hung national medals for the number of children she had brought into the world.

Amaraa pulled up a chair next to me and leaned in.

"Bibi told me your plan, you will die if you try this alone," he said, then paused. "But my sister says you are a good man, so we should help you. I have to finish some things first, but I was thinking about heading to see my uncle anyway. He is a nomad with many horses a few days away, if I go you could come with."

Again, I didn't know how to respond. Could this all be real? I had just arrived in Mongolia with nothing but a dream to live with nomads and here was someone offering to take me there upon arrival.

"I saw you on that billboard," I said.

He grinned. "YES, I'm an actor!"

I had no idea Mongolia even had a movie industry, but I quickly learned that Amaraa was building it. I had just come from California and serendipitously ended up in the heart of Mongolia's budding Hollywood, with one of its beloved actors who had just finished playing the role of Genghis Khan's best friend in a multimillion-dollar Japanese film. But it sat lightly on him.

Over the next few days we drank at an Irish-themed bar and traded stories. He told me about digging frozen sheep out of the snow during the *dzud*, the brutal winters when ice locks the grass beneath the snow and animals starve by the thousands. I told him stories of California, of the beautiful beaches and the legendary traffic. When I expressed my gratitude for his family's hospitality, he stopped me and explained it as principle.

"This world is small, we have to put good energy into it. For Bibi's children and my children, one day, our children's children." I nodded my head in agreement and bought another round.

We went to a local black market to buy equipment for our journey. Stalls full of things used by the nomads, some new, some very old. Amaraa showed me how to use subtle hand gestures to pass traditional stone snuff bottles, pulling back sleeves when reaching toward someone to show you don't have a knife. He found an old nomad's long pipe which I bought, contemplating the person who had owned it before me. We tried on wolfskin hats and made scary faces. He insisted on paying for a pair of high leather boots for me as a gift, explaining that the boot maker was his cousin. It seemed like everyone was his cousin or aunt, or at least knew him.

That evening he said the truck was ready. "Are you ready? You can't go alone, there are wolves. Your horse will run away and you will die. What will I tell your mother!?" He laughed heartily, then grew serious. "It will be an adventure."

---

We left Ulaanbaatar the next day. His mother and Bibi walked us out and sniffed our faces. "This is how we kiss in Mongolia," Bibi explained. "We smell the soul." As we pulled away, they threw cups of milk into the sky for good luck.

On a mix of paved and dirt roads in Amaraa's old Toyota, nicknamed Dusty, we drove into Mongolia's heartland. The only music he had was CDs of local folk music, Boyz II Men and Neil Diamond. Soon we were singing along with gusto, filling the sweeping skies and golden fields with a mix of American R&B and ancient throat singing harmonies that would skip at the best road bumps.

Along the way he explained the way of the steppe, the ancient grass plains of the North. If you see a wolf, good luck. A fox, bad luck. Horses walking toward your car, very good luck. The name of roasted marmot, *boodog*, cooked with heated stones pressed into the carcass. Wherever we passed someone on the road, Amaraa would stop and greet them like they were old friends. He'd give rides, help push a stuck vehicle, share whatever food we had.

"This is my father's road, always help," he said. Like it had been at the market, everyone seemed to know him, or at least to trust him on sight. He had a unique combination of strength, compassion, charisma, and humility I'd just been reading about in Jack Weatherford's new framing of Genghis Khan.

As the sun crept lower to the west, Amaraa scanned the horizon like a bird searching for its migration path, then suddenly he turned off the deeply weathered asphalt and drove through tall brown grass, over hills and through valleys, with no road at all. Dusty's wheels glided across the steppe like wide skis over fresh snow till a short while later we arrived at a small camp of two *gers* (yurts).

His uncle, a tall older man in a cowboy hat with a sun-bleached face, came out to greet us with a smile and a hug. He wore the nomadic *deel*, a long gray wool coat that ran to the ground like a robe, wrapped at the waist by a nine-foot yellow sash. We unloaded crates of potatoes, bottles of ketchup, a staple vegetable of the North, vodka, salt, and sugar, while a group of laughing children ran circles around us.

The oldest was a girl who commanded the younger ones to help unload. She reminded me of my older sister Jane.

I ducked through the felt door of their nomadic home and slipped into a different world. The sunlight disappeared, replaced by the soft glow of a stove fire in the center, its smoke piped up toward the central hole in the roof. The space felt warm and lived-in, carpets on the ground, beds lining the perimeter, a dry-aging goat carcass hanging overhead. Amaraa's aunt sat on a stool beside the wood stove at the heart of the ger like a conductor stoking the engine of a locomotive. Her round face held a warmth and toughness like Bibi's. She had been stirring a large milk barrel and stopped to hand me a bowl of flour noodles boiled in milk tea with fragments of jerky. It was delicious. Then came the fermented milk candies, less so. Plates and plates of them. I smiled politely, took one, then quietly slipped it into my pocket when no one was looking. The whole life of the ger was centered around that stove.

That night I fell asleep in a cot under the hanging goat, surrounded by nomads, Amaraa on a mattress on the floor in a pile of nieces and nephews. There were no separations, no walls between us, all existing together in the womb of a felt-doored home.

The next morning Amaraa handed me a set of reins. The Mongolian saddle was a piece of wood covered in the thinnest of cloth padding with stirrups, he explained, that were refined by Genghis Khan so warriors could ride standing and shoot arrows at the same time. That hard wooden saddle would leave a mark, literally. The scar from these rides is still with me, a replacement for the alleged Mongolian birthmark that had faded at the age of three.

I sat bow-legged, fumbling for stability, while the little girl, maybe seven or eight, giggled as she circled us effortlessly riding bareback. She had been on horses since before she could walk, and even at her young age was already herding sheep with her father. The look on her face was patient amusement as I continued my buffoonery, but after a few hard falls, I was bruised yet finding the beginnings of a rhythm.

That evening we rode out to bring in the grazing herd, and as my horse galloped across the land, I felt a freedom I had never really known. Truthfully, I was barely in control. Where Amaraa led, my horse followed. Still, it felt like flying, a flock of birds in one coordinated flow of motion.

Days turned to weeks. At night I'd rise from my cot to relieve myself outside and my eyes would adjust to a sky so dense with stars it felt like I was floating in space. I felt weightless amid the cosmos, scored by the murmurs of five hundred sheep humping and farting in a vast undulating sea of wool that spread out in front of me. Other than the sheep, it was the same feeling I'd had as a kid, on a camping trip with my family in the Sierra Nevadas, the sensation that the universe was so large it could swallow me into it and yet it seemed to be embracing me instead.

The smell of burning yak dung filled the air with a sweetness that I came to associate with a feeling of warmth. In the mornings, Amaraa's aunt would stir a tall vat of *airag*, a slightly alcoholic fermented mare's milk that had a hint of bubbles to it. We would drink from a silver bowl passed from hand to hand with sleeves retracted in the customary gesture. The bowl traveled through the center of the ger like spokes of a wheel. "Nomads only drink horse milk through the day and eat food only once, at night," Amaraa explained, assuring me that I would get used to it.

One afternoon, while bringing in the herd from the hills they had been grazing on, Amaraa and I stopped at a well in the middle of a valley. We pumped water and splashed it over our faces and necks, smiling and laughing at nothing in particular.

"This is real life!" he said.

He told me that a lot of people go to Ulaanbaatar looking for jobs, but they'd realize how much they miss the old ways and return to nomadic life. Roughly forty percent of Mongolians were still nomadic. It was getting harder though. Fences were going up as companies bought mining rights, displacing the ancient ways that depended on movement across the land. The nomad's way was to follow the herd where the cycles of the season guided them. I was living in that now.

I did my best to shake off the disturbing thought of mining operations threatening this deeply rooted way of life that I had stumbled into, and soaked in the fact that I was actually there. The ridiculous plan had worked. My back was sore from the wooden saddle and my skin was raw, but the evening light stretched across the grass and my horse had begun responding to the gentle squeeze of my legs.

Later that week Amaraa's uncle asked us to help him brand the baby horses of the year. It was a time of celebration for the late summer cycle. He rode through the herd, lassoing the foals with a rope sling attached to the end of a long wooden pole. It took three of us to brand each one. One holding the tail, another holding its mane tight, and Amaraa's uncle holding a hot iron right out of the fire.

I watched him place the iron against the foal's hip and smelled burning flesh. At first the horse didn't feel it, but as the hissing continued it suddenly bucked and knocked me in the face with my own hand, then ran off. Amaraa laughed heartily. Then we did another, and another and another.

After the branding we rode to neighboring camps on the horizon with invitations to a celebratory feast, stopping at each micro-community for another silver bowl of vodka. I quickly learned one turned into several bowls passed around. No bottle opened could be put away half full. I was eventually riding across the plains of Mongolia completely drunk. At least it loosened my back.

That night nomadic families throughout the valley gathered in Amaraa's uncle's ger, singing the ancient Mongolian songs of horses, family, and the land. I started to hear something familiar, a cadence that reminded me of Native American songs in documentaries I'd watched as a kid. Was I hearing echoes of a connection across the Bering Land Bridge from tens of thousands of years ago, melodies and tones that may have traveled far? Or did the plains themselves have a song that had been tapped into across continents?

Later, a man who'd been sizing me up all evening said something to Amaraa.

"He wants to wrestle you," Amaraa said with a raised eyebrow.

"Tsa, tsa, tsa!" the man said smiling like he didn't trust me. He stood up and walked in my direction.

I motioned to stand up too.

"Albert, you shouldn't do this. These guys are strong. Every day they're working the land."

But I knew if I backed down I would truly be out. I'd spent most of my life on the outside of things, scanning for a table that might let me sit down. Here on the steppe, they settled that by wrestling.

We walked out of the ger. A throng of excited nomads poured out behind us. "First person to touch their knee or elbow to the ground loses," Amaraa yelled over the commotion of what felt like a schoolyard fight.

The guy squared up shirtless, so I took my shirt off. Then everything around me began to blur. The cheers, the stomping feet, the landscape. All I could hear was their muffled sounds as everything faded into the tiny distance between him and me. I wasn't on the steppe anymore. I was back in high school wrestling matches, in childhood scraps with my siblings where my older sister by four years would make it a point to humble me.

He was stronger than I was, but I had one chance. I shot forward, low at first, arched my back, swung behind him, and took him to a knee.

For a moment I couldn't believe it. Then the quiet in my mind shattered and the roar came flooding back. Everyone jumping, cheering, clapping. Amaraa ran over beaming. "You won! You must do the eagle dance!" He raised my arms like a bird and instructed me to do leaping arcs with my legs. A woman poured airag down my back. Now when the silver bowl was handed to me, it was passed with a bigger smile and gestures of the eagle dance. Years later, when I'd return to Mongolia, Amaraa would name me NaChing, the Eagle.

I lived in that way of life for nearly two weeks, all of us in that small ger camp. My back seized up and then recovered. I started to acquire an appreciation for the fermented mare's milk we drank each day.

As the days ticked by, Amaraa told me more about Mongolia. Genghis Khan had promoted religious freedom under Tengri, the eternal blue sky. "Everything is under the sky anyway," he explained. Under the Khan's law, fouling a river or killing an animal you did not need was punishable by death. And a man's place had to be earned, not inherited. I thought about the wrestling. To his people, Genghis was the greatest nomad who had ever lived.

Eventually, I asked Amaraa if he would be willing to take me further north, to a lake I'd seen a documentary about years earlier. It had told the story of the few remaining reindeer herders in the world. I was as enchanted as I had been when reading about the steppe nomads, universes away from the epicenters of technology and innovation that I had grown up in. So after living the "old ways" with Amaraa's uncle and aunt, we packed up, said goodbye to my newfound family, and headed further north. Deeper into the rabbit hole.

---

Dusty rambled along, the steppe falling in and out of remote stretches of tall pines which engulfed the narrow muddy tracks we followed. We would come across tiny communities here and there, stopping to buy makeshift paper bags of pine nuts or wild berries from foragers until we reached a truly giant lake surrounded by green hills, Siberian taiga forest, and the snowcapped peaks of the Russian border. Khövsgöl. On the northern rim I could see dwellings that resembled the teepees that once covered the plains of North America. Here, they were the homes of the Tsaatan, the last remaining echoes of their people.

One Tsaatan man invited us into his conical tent, its tall pointed ceiling a contrast to the low-hanging gers that had been my home days before. A small fire warmed the space, its smoke rising through the hole at the top. The smell of drying skins reminded me of my mother's shed, when the child version of me had unsuccessfully attempted to do the same with a bloody deer hide I had received from a neighbor. Leather straps and stirrups for the reindeer hung on the vaulted poles along with a seasoned but well-cared-for long-rifle.

While Amaraa spoke to a hunter about the coming winter, I pondered the breadth of the human experience. There on the Russian border, a small family of reindeer herders, a Mongolian movie star, and a California engineering student huddled in one conical tent.

That bitterly cold night we made a fire and camped at the edge of the pines by the lake under a brilliant sky of infinite distant suns. I poked the embers with a fallen branch, bundled in the giant sheepskin coat I had borrowed from Amaraa. Across the fire his face changed, the easy grin gone, and he began to talk about his people's true story, that of Genghis Khan, and how it had never properly been told.

Nothing had been written by his own people during his reign, no portrait was ever made while he lived, and to this day neither his resting place nor the tomb of anyone in the imperial family has ever been found. It was almost like he had been imagined, a boogeyman to the armies of the past. But to Amaraa the Great Khan was much more than the caricature of brutality that the West had painted. He had shaped the world with a spirit that came from the heart of the land itself.

"He was born with a blood clot gripped in his hand, a sign of destiny," Amaraa continued.

He described the life of young Temüjin, as he was originally named. His father a tribal chieftain, poisoned by rivals. Then the clan that should have protected the grieving family cast them out, abandoning a widow and her children to the forest to die in the hopes it would placate their rivals.

"He learned survival," Amaraa said, and fed another branch into the fire.

With nowhere to go, Temüjin turned to a childhood friend from happier times, Jamukha. They became *anda*.

"An anda," Amaraa explained, "a sworn brotherhood, a bond beyond blood."

Jamukha fought beside him, helping him claw back from nothing and recover his stolen love, Börte. For a time it seemed the two of them would rule the steppe together. But they wanted different worlds. Jamukha's was decided by blood and birthright. Temüjin's was defined by those who proved able and loyal in the ways of the nomad, regardless of birth. They went to war, and when it was over Temüjin grieved him.

"At a great gathering of the clans they gave him a new name," Amaraa said. "Chinggis. You say Genghis. It means ocean, the biggest thing on earth."

The fire had burned down to a low orange. I sat there looking out at the vastness of the moonlit lake, its horizon seemingly boundless.

Then Amaraa described a sacred mountain, the Burkhan Khaldun, where the young Temüjin had first stood at his lowest moment, when everything in his life had been taken from him, his father, Börte, his community. There, on that sacred mountain, he somehow had willed his fate into existence, and from it the course of the world would change.

The mountain had hidden him when he was nothing, he said, and he had promised to pray to it every morning for the rest of his life and told his children to do the same. Its location today is still not fully verified.

Then he poked the fire and said, "it's complicated."

I was hearing the stuff of legend. Maybe this is what I had come to find, the story that had been missing from my childhood spent in the west. But the power of his story had been dispersed like the spoils of war, other countries even claiming that his final resting place was in their lands.

He told me some Mongolians thought the tomb should never be found. That the not knowing was the point, that they could worship him without having to know where he lay. Others thought the story had been taken from them once already, by people who banned the script and burned the books and made it dangerous to say his name out loud, and that finding it would be taking that power back.

On the long drive back to Ulaanbaatar, we passed the scars of foreign mining companies extracting gold and uranium, often poisoning the rivers that had sustained Amaraa's people for generations. The pain was apparent on his face as the smell of burning chemicals filled the air, and it remained as the columns of smoke lingered in the rearview mirror.

The last day, driving across the steppe in Dusty, Amaraa paused for a moment, then said, "We should be andas."

I said yes without hesitation.

Before I left, he and Bibi gave me a curved sword from one of Amaraa's movie sets. They had it engraved: "To our brother, we wish all the best to you in your life. Your Mongolian family."

After a childhood of being transplanted from one country to another, always the outsider, I had found friendships and a world that felt natural to me. And I had learned of a hero from the land my grandfather thought we came from. A man who had forged the largest land empire the world has ever seen, who left no portrait and no grave, and who in every telling I had grown up with was a barbarian in a comedic TV sketch. I had been taught how the world became what it is. This story had not been part of it, and I had been born with its mark on my back.

Had the truth been buried along with his tomb?

I flew back to San Diego with the sword in my checked bag, walked into a burrito shop near campus still smelling like yak dung and fermented mare's milk, and blinked under the fluorescent lights. Days ago I'd been galloping across the steppe, now I was ordering a California burrito and trying to remember my lab access code. Had it been a dream? The curved blade and the scar from the wooden saddle confirmed it had all been real.

Prof. Meyers welcomed me back with a hearty handshake of a fellow explorer, told me to make a presentation of the journey to the lab meeting that week, and then instructed me to get back to work immediately, adding that he was already reviewing a list of applications waiting to take my place if I hesitated. The road fed the soul, and the work earned me the road. But something persistent from the steppe had followed me home. Where was the tomb?

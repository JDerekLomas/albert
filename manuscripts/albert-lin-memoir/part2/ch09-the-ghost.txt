CHAPTER 9
The Ghost

I had been pulled from one drawer of infinite realities. Whether they had slid me back into the same one on the other side of the operation, I had no way of knowing.

A week after the amputation they discharged me. My friends wheeled me out and the sun hit my face for the first time in a month, and the warmth and the wind and the smell of grass came at me all at once, so much that I could feel my head tingling at the edges. I raised my fists to the sky like I had won a fight. Then I looked down at the space where my leg had been. Air.

On the way home from the ICU I asked Bruna to stop so I could get a haircut, a month on my back having left me looking like a marooned sailor. The barber took in the wheelchair, the wrapped stump, and my wild eyes, and asked, without missing a beat, "Alright, what'll it be?" When she was done, the face in the mirror looked almost human again.

Was this me now?

The house I had built with two legs did not fit the body that came back to it. Derek had moved my bed downstairs to the living room. Every trip to the bathroom was going to be a bouldering problem. Rich screwed grab bars into the shower and anywhere else I would reach for a wall.

Amaraa flew in from the steppe without warning, the Anda who had knelt beside me on the summit of the sacred mountain with his belt across his neck and his own hands raised to the sky in homage, now filling my doorway with a smile too wide for a stoic Mongolian. He'd come halfway around the world to help carry me into my own house. "I see you will be okay," he said with a knowing nod toward Bruna and a clandestine thumbs up.

That night we sat around my bed in the living room like kids at a sleepover and threw what was more a party than a vigil, a rebirth without candles on a cake. Amaraa told stories of our adventures while Derek cooked a feast. Bruna tucked herself under my arm and would not let the wine anyone poured come near my lips, as the pharmacy of meds was now running through my liver. I played silly made-up songs, and things felt okay. But every so often I would glance down and see what my subconscious was still not fully prepared for.

Was I still in the same reality?

When the kids returned a few days later the family dynamic rearranged itself the way the furniture had. Millie, four years old, appointed herself my morning steward, waking me with my clothes laid out in endearing ensembles, and bringing a breakfast tray bearing a note in her half-invented handwriting. Charlie, six, took the trash and the dishes without being asked, moving through the house with the quiet discipline of a boy who had decided, privately, that he was going to step it up. Once, when the pain caught me and I locked up, he laid his small hand flat on my chest and left it there until it passed. At night I would listen to Bruna carry them up the stairs I could not climb, and lie there wanting nothing in the world so much as to be the one to tuck them in.

---

Halloween arrived not long after. *Look for the perks,* I thought. So I fashioned a makeshift peg leg and an eyepatch and we dressed as pirates in what the kids said were "the best costumes we had ever made."

"We were really committed this year," I told their parents, who laughed awkwardly. At Disneyland we skipped every line, and got the good parking too. "Dad," Millie said, "your leg is amazing."

And then, the night my documentary from China aired, something else began.

I sat on Adam's couch and watched a ghost of myself on the screen, two good legs carrying me through ancient tombs, telling the story of an emperor who had built a whole army out of clay because he could not accept that his body would end.

A man who wanted to live forever, narrated by a man who had just lost a piece of himself. Charlie and Millie played on the floor while I failed to reconcile the person on the screen with who I was now. Could I really be an explorer without two legs?

That was the night the phantom came.

It came as sharp electric darts firing out of a foot that was not there. I would reach down into the air where it had been and feel it burning, folding in half, every tendon torn loose, except there were no tendons, no bone, nothing but a glitch in reality. The leg was gone, and still it suffered. Haunted by my own ghost.

The feeling would arrive without warning and with increased frequency as time passed.

I would be in the middle of dinner, or halfway through a bedtime story, and freeze mid-sentence in a contorted pantomime of pain while the kids watched and waited for their father to come back. It reached its crescendo when it found the nerves running up through my groin, the narrow place where every signal from below had to pass on its way to my brain. After that, the simple act of going to the bathroom set off an agony I would do almost anything to avoid. So I stopped drinking. Then I stopped eating. I did not realize at first that I was starving and dehydrating myself to keep a leg I no longer had from hurting.

Make it stop. Make it stop. Make it stop.

---

Bruna wheeled me down a hallway that smelled of chalk dust, the same smell as the physics buildings my father had worked in my whole childhood, to a dim beige door marked The UC San Diego Center for Brain and Cognition. I knocked. A tall, elderly man in a golfer's cap opened it and looked at me like he had been expecting me. His name was V. S. Ramachandran. Everyone called him Rama.

"You must be Albert," he said, with a mysterious smile.

I was pushed into an office that was a cabinet of curiosities. Brains floating in jars. A dinosaur skull on a shelf beside leaning stacks of paper. Artifacts from every continent, and graduate students orbiting him like disciples around a saint. He waved me in, waited until I had settled the chair, and leaned forward with the delight of a man about to show a child a magic trick.

"Where does the body end," he asked, "and the mind begin?" I swear his eyes sparkled as he asked the question.

He had spent his life exploring that border. In a landmark book called *The Phantom and the Brain* he proposed that a single kind of brain cell, one that fires when you reach for a cup and again when you watch someone else reach for one, might be the hidden architecture beneath two things that seem to have nothing to do with each other. Phantom limbs and human empathy. Both were acts of imagination, the same magic that could conjure a leg out of nothing was the neurological phenomenon that let me feel what was being felt by someone else, mirror neurons.

Then he showed me the mirror box. It looked like a small wooden guillotine with a mirror standing down the middle. "I am famous," he said, already grinning, "for removing things that were never there." Bruna helped me set my good leg on one side and the stump on the other, and I looked down into the glass, and there they were. Two legs. Whole, ordinary, mine. He had resurrected the old me. I knew it was a trick and my brain did not care. It saw two legs, and for the first time in weeks the invisible screaming stopped. The muscles I no longer owned unclenched. The fire cooled. I sat and stared into the mirror and breathed, and something in my chest that had been braced for a month let go.

When he lifted the illusion away, the fire rushed back into its ghost and I was left grasping at air again. But I had felt it. For a few seconds a simple mirror had reached into the worst and strangest agony of my life and turned it off. The pain was not a fact about my leg. It was a story my brain was telling me.

Rama had me back again and again, and each time tried something new. Bruna's leg under a blanket where mine should be, moving the way mine once had, so that for a few minutes I was a man with two legs again, one of them significantly better looking than the other. From a bag of rubber Halloween severed limbs he would create props to slowly draw the old fixator pins out of, one by one, until my body released a tension it had held since the hospital. A famous scientist playing with toy blood-covered legs to talk my brain out of its grief. Each trick helped for a moment, but only a moment.

Then one day as I was leaving, I lingered in front of a giant mandala that hung on his wall.

"An ancient technology," Rama explained. "A tool to help bring the mind into meditative focus."

We stared at it in silence for a few minutes as I did my best to let the patterns hypnotize me, without success.

"It takes years of training and discipline to get to those states," he continued as Bruna pushed my wheelchair toward the door. "If you could get there, free yourself of your thoughts, it's in those states where the brain is most neuroplastic," he concluded.

---

The mirror had shown me a different story. But months of constant pain had cut that path so deep it had become part of who I was. To lay a new path I would need to create the conditions that could break up the old. I wheeled out of there knowing what I had to chase.

For years I had gone out into the world to find out who I was, sure the answer lay buried somewhere in the ground. Now the ground was my own brain. Who was I, if the map my brain draws of me can be changed?

I went after the ghost the way I did everything, systematically, obsessively. The bed became a desk. Laptop, books, papers, EEG sensors, and before long a set of tuning forks and ancient manuscripts. A one-legged man in San Diego surrounded by biosensors and sound healing gear. I knew how it looked. California at its best.

But none of what was happening to me with the phantom obeyed standard logic. The mind itself was a frontier, mysterious more than explainable. As a student I had looked to nature for lessons, reasoning that it had been running design experiments far longer than we had. The same held for the mind, that ritual and tradition had been working this territory for thousands of years before science arrived. I set out to find every door that mirror had cracked open.

My old university director, Ramesh Rao, a member of the National Academy of Engineering who was also, improbably, a full-time yogi, came by and set a heart rate monitor on my table. He had been using it as a way to measure biorhythms in the heart as a window into mental states. "The higher the variability the more open-hearted you are in a way," he told me. "It's tied to your state of being, your autonomic nervous system. When you're stressed your heart rate locks into a single rhythm."

Then he told me about Kundalini, described in Sanskrit as a way of connecting mind and body. It was about finding "flow states" between the two. I read until the reading itself began to feel like vertigo. Nearly every human culture had built a formal doorway into some altered state. The trance of a gospel choir before a sermon. The shaman's drum on the sacred mountain, how it had beaten through my chest until I could no longer tell it from my heart. I had thought I was inventing my own way out of the pain, but the answers lay in the oldest practices there were, technologies, older than writing, old as the first hands pressed to a cave wall.

I asked Jamie and Leo for help, the friends I had been making music with since the divorce, and we played with the HRV monitors clipped to our chests, eyes closed, in search of that magical flow state. We had one rule between us, never the same thing twice. There was almost always a moment our minds let go all at once, and the music began to play itself through us instead of by us, and afterward the screens would show our separate hearts fallen into a coherence with no one leading, like a flock turning without a bird to follow. "Entrainment," Ramesh had suggested, could be a way for multiple people to rhythmically sync together into group flow states.

In part it was just an excuse to play together, regardless, in those moments I would completely lose myself in the music and the pain would ease as the friendships built.

---

Bruna took me to Joshua Tree, our first trip since the hospital. At the campsite there was a handicap parking spot. It was the first time I had ever needed one. She set up the tent while I sat in a camp chair and watched, unable to hammer a stake or carry a bag.

Years before, when my body was still intact and I had spent every weekend climbing there, someone had shown me a hidden cave tucked behind a few loose stones. We moved the stones aside and crawled in on our backs. The air was cool and carried the mineral smell of old stone. Above us the ceiling held petroglyphs, handprints pressed into rock, a painted map of some long-ago starscape.

The chanting began, she passed a string of 108 beads between her fingers while reciting a mantra she had learned on the banks of the Ganges. I followed her. My breathing steadied. My mind settled into the space between thoughts, and for a moment the pain let go.

I know it sounds strange, a Brazilian woman chanting Hindu mantras over an Asian man in a cave covered in North American pre-history. But as the cave resonated with abstract sound it felt like we were tapping into something beneath the language and labels.

---

My research kept circling back to entheogenic plants, the strongest door of all. Wade Davis, an ethnobotanist and friend from National Geographic, had once described to me how certain plants mixed with ritual could carry a person to the very edge of death and return them forever entranced. Years earlier he had handed me a San Pedro cactus. It had been out there in the back yard growing the whole time I was in the hospital.

I read about María Sabina, the Mazatec curandera, who said the sacred mushrooms gave her a language she did not so much speak as report, and that her healing came from seeing with the heart. I read that doctors at Johns Hopkins had put terminally ill cancer patients under the same mushroom and watched the pathways in their brains physically change, the fear of death loosening its grip.

Could a plant rewire me?

Desperation began to take hold. At my lowest the pain had become a continuum with no shore. The feeling was like being stuck just inches below the surface of the water, not being able to get a gasp of air. It was the persistence of it that wore me down. And as it compounded it grew to a moment where I wondered whether I should have just died out there in the dirt. Then the sight of my kids knocked that thought clean out of me.

I did not need the leg back. I needed to stop refusing the loss at a neurological level, and to do that I was going to have to let the version of that man die, not the man himself. I was scared, but something had to change.

One month after they cut my body apart, in mid-November, the kids went to Kara's and Bruna packed the truck. I looked out the window as she drove me north once again. Eventually we turned east from the 395, off the pavement and a few miles over a range into a wilderness in the most remote corner of Death Valley where I had heard there were hot springs.

My stump was propped on a pillow beside me, the cab cluttered with my guitar, snacks, and her prayer beads and, of course, a mirror. It was the same desert my kids and I had crossed the spring after the divorce, when I had filled a rented RV with toys and tried to imagine a life remapped. The lowest ground on the continent, an hour's drive from its highest peak, the one place I knew that held both ends of everything at once, the way my life did now.

In her bag were the mushrooms. I would take what the researchers called a heroic dose out here, where there was no one but us for a hundred miles and nothing to hold onto but the sky.

She spread a blanket on the sand and we brewed the fungi into a tea, held it in our hands and visualized a life without my phantom, where my body, mind, and spirit could accept what had happened. Then with my heart pounding like I was about to dive into a vast sea, I took a deep breath and drank it down.

For a while, nothing. Then a tingling opened in my chest and spread, and about forty-five minutes in I hopped to the back of the truck to urinate, braced as always for the ghost of my leg to knock me down, and the ghost did not come. It was simply gone. I stood in the middle of the desert, free for the first time in a month.

The world began to breathe. Every grain of sand under my hand had an edge and a shadow. The mountains heaved and unfolded along the horizon like a giant belly breathing in its sleep. The high clouds thinned into layer upon layer, ribbons of color drifting through them. My chest tightened as everything careful and gripping in me fought to stay in charge, and Bruna looked over at my anxiety and laughed, releasing one word.

"Succumb."

I took off the bandages and then my clothes and laid the raw thing that was left of my leg out in the open sun, skin that had known nothing but gauze for a month. The distal end looked like a baseball with long lines of stitches holding it together, the sand cool against it. As the grains ran over my skin I thought of Millie and Charlie on the beach a few weeks before the accident, burying me through bouts of laughter. "You're a mermaid now, Dad!" Now I did the same with what was left of me and slowly drew it back into the light, watching the shape of it emerge, choosing not to look away.

I closed my eyes and imagined what was happening in my brain. Filaments lighting along the axons like neon out on the playa, reaching across whole regions of cognition that had gone quiet, or that had never spoken to one another at all. I was threading through the dark to redraw the map.

Bruna was feeling it too. She moved through the dunes like a piece of the wind, and we laughed until the laughing turned to weeping and the weeping turned to holding each other. The fear I had been living inside had nowhere to stand out there, and I saw, at last, that the fear had been the thing feeding the pain all along.

I did a handstand in the warm sand, flexing like an idiot trying to show off, and rolled down the face of the dune like a kid who had just discovered a new trick. I was flawed, unfinished, but more alive than I had ever been whole.

In the last light of a small fire, on that blank sheet of desert, I visualized life as an explorer again. Not remember one. Imagine one. This was the trick underneath all of it, the phantom, the mirror, the mushroom, a life. We imagine the world before it unfolds. Down in the heart of the place called Death Valley, at the lowest point on the map, I was reborn.

The pain was gone.

On the last night, headlights crossed the valley. Tom Garrison and his partner had tracked a pin we dropped before driving in, on their way to the hot springs, knowing we were out somewhere in the same land. I had not seen him since the jungles of Guatemala, before any of this began.

By the fire he told me about a survey he and a few other archaeologists had finally got funded over the Maya world, a plane flying grids above the canopy with lasers firing down through the trees to map the ground beneath a forest no one had ever seen through. Not one ruin at a time, the way we had attempted with balloons and makeshift drones. All of it at once so he could delete the trees and look for what was still unknown. "The groundtruthing begins soon, it's going to be really big, Albert," he said. I would quickly learn how right he was.

Sitting there with a rewired brain and no right foot, listening to the plan, I felt the ground tilt, very slightly, forward.

Two days later I sat across from Rama and my pain doctor and told them what had happened out in the sand. They listened, and then they got excited, and asked if we could write it up as a case study. That one write-up would grow, over the years that followed, into the first clinical trial on psychedelics for pain in the country, and a Center for Psychedelic Research inside UC San Diego's own School of Medicine for which I asked to maintain the title of Director of Exploration. The thought that all of what had happened might one day pull a stranger out from under their own pain was the first time since the accident that there might have been a point to it all.

But the long-term impacts are still mysterious, and possibly not all wanted. There are whole stretches of that season I cannot account for, memory gone the way the pain went. Maybe pain is itself a memory, and when you burn enough of it away you lose some of yourself in the fire. The door swung both ways.

---

The last thing to rebuild was the body itself. The prosthetist Brent had found, Peter Harsch, was an Ironman who treated every patient like an athlete he was building from scratch. Just as it had been serendipitous to find out Rama happened to teach at my own university, San Diego also happened to be a military town which carried the unfortunate byproduct of needing a strong prosthetics community. Peter's clinic was full of young amputees, some of them mountain climbers, some of them military still in uniform, all of them carrying a confidence I had not felt in months.

He took a mold of my residual limb and shaped a socket with the artistry of a sculptor until it held my whole weight on nothing but vacuum. Standing on the worst injury of my life was terrifying, and every instinct in me said don't. I gripped the parallel bars and pressed down and stood. From across the room a man missing his leg above the knee yelled, "Aw, that's just a paper cut, you big baby!"

Then Peter showed me a shelf of feet. One built to grip a motorcycle peg, one that rotated down for a swim fin, one that clipped straight into a ski binding, each of them imagined by engineers and prosthetists and the people who would wear them back into their own lives.

The sensation of gratitude rose in me, and right behind it the knowledge that this shelf, this whole ecosystem of care, was something almost no amputee on earth would ever get to stand in front of.

Sometimes Charlie and Millie would come along and turn the clinic into a playground, jousting down the hall on borrowed wheelchairs. "Time for the leg shop!" they would announce, like we were headed for ice cream. Weeks later they traded the test socket for one of sleek carbon fiber. I stuck a set of phoenix wing stickers on each side, stood up in the mirror, and started, slowly, to walk again.

---

By spring, six months out, the medical debt from the year was stacking into something I could not see the top of. And the love between Bruna and me had deepened and sharpened at the same time, the two of us trying to become partners after months of being patient and nurse, having somehow never really even dated. It had all come too fast, and like with Kara, something was starting to give.

I was strong enough now that she could leave, and when Bruna took a trip home to see her family in Brazil I decided to attempt a trip with the kids back to the dunes, alone.

Once again I needed to prove to myself I could still be the dad I wanted to be, only this time as an amputee. I threw some crutches in with the camping gear just in case the prosthetic would break, and we started to drive with my left foot on the pedal.

It had been one year, almost to the day, since I had first taken them up the 395. The road looked the same.

The little rented RV rolled up to the dunes and the kids were already at the windows, pointing at the tallest dune, miles off across the flats. Lying in my own blood, I had been certain I would never get this back. Here it was.

"We have to go to the tallest one!" Charlie shouted.

I looked down at my sand-filled prosthetic and started to build an excuse. "I don't know, it's kind of..."

But before I could finish they were out the door and running up the slope. "Shit..." I murmured to myself. Quickly I packed a bag with water and oranges, grabbed the hiking staff Jamie had left with me, and stepped out onto the shifting ground to go after them.

They pulled me the whole way. Past mud flats cracked into dragon scales, over the blade-like ridges, and every time I slowed they threw my own words back at me over their shoulders. "Come on, Dad. Put your mind to it." Charlie was six and Millie was four, feeding me my own parenting on a mountain of sand.

When I muttered that they did not seem to understand I was missing a leg, they did not hear me. They had wonders to find. At the top they turned around, breathless and shining, and gave it back to me whole. "See, Dad? When you believe it, you can do anything."

I had said that to them a hundred times. Standing on the summit I understood that it was true, and that they were the ones who had made it so. For a year I had been carried, by Bruna, by Derek, by Amaraa, by friends who loaded me into cars and built me beds and drove me to appointments. And now by these two small people who had once ridden on my shoulders and were hauling me, breath by breath, up out of the ashes.

We lay in the warm desert sand, the three of us, looking to the sky. The spring had brought wildflowers that peppered the dunes, and bugs that zoomed by in all directions.

Had I returned to the same reality, did it matter?

I had spent my life looking for what was hidden. Maybe the largest unmapped mysteries are right behind our eyes. Could one help explain the other?

It was time to go back out into the world and find out.

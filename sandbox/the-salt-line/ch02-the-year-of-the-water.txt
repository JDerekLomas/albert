CHAPTER 2
The Year of the Water

In the spring of 1989 the lake came up over the causeway and kept coming, and by the first week of May there were eight inches of standing water over the whole course. You could stand at the four-mile marker and see the mountains twice, once in the air and once underneath, and no one who had built a season around that ground thought it was beautiful.

Danny was eleven that spring and he took it harder than any of us. The lake had never come up onto the flats before, not once in the whole history of the place, and so there was no one to ask what happens next or how long it takes to dry.

In that moment, I came to understand that the water was not merely water. It was a mirror held up to everything our family had failed to say to one another, and as I stood at the edge of that vast and shimmering expanse, I realized that some floods arrive from the sky and some arrive from within, and that the hardest ones to survive are the ones we make ourselves.

The Bonneville Salt Flats are the remnant of Lake Bonneville, a pluvial lake that covered approximately 20,000 square miles of what is now western Utah during the late Pleistocene. When the Red Rock Pass in southern Idaho failed catastrophically around 14,500 years ago, the lake discharged into the Snake River drainage in a flood that ranks among the largest known on the continent, dropping the water level by over three hundred feet. What remained evaporated over millennia, leaving behind the potash and halite crust that racers would eventually measure their lives against. The crust is thickest near the centre, where it can exceed five feet, and thins toward the margins. Mining operations beginning in the 1960s are generally understood to have accelerated the loss of the salt layer, though the extent of the contribution remains contested among hydrologists.

Dad went out to the shack anyway, every morning, and wound four clocks in a plywood box in the middle of a lake. Uncle Ray drove out to get him on the third day. He told him he'd never forgive him for what he did to her, and he left, and it was years before either of them said the other's name in front of me.

What I remember is the sound of the truck going through the water. Not the argument, not what anybody said. A truck moving at ten miles an hour through eight inches of standing water makes a noise like a long sheet of paper being torn very slowly, and I stood on the running board of Ray's Chevrolet with the door open, going out to fetch my father from a place that had stopped existing, and I thought: that is the same sound. That is the car. Somebody has slowed the car down to nothing and this is what is left of it.

We ate dinner at the Wendover diner that night because the kitchen at home had been packed into boxes for reasons nobody had explained to me. Danny ordered a grilled cheese. He always ordered a grilled cheese.

By July the water was gone and the course came back wrong — soft in patches, ridged where the wind had worked it, three-quarters of a mile shorter than the year before. Dad measured it twice and wrote the number on the wall of the shack in pencil, under all the other numbers, and did not say it out loud.

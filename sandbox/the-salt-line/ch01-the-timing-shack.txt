CHAPTER 1
The Timing Shack

The salt starts about a mile past the last fence, and the first thing it does is take away your sense of how far anything is. My father used to say the flats were the only place on earth where you could see a man walking toward you for an hour and still not know if you liked him. In August the crust dries into hexagons, each one the width of a dinner plate, and if you scuff one loose with your heel it comes up like a broken tile and there is grey mud underneath, cool as a cellar.

The shack sat at the four-mile marker and it was not a shack so much as a plywood box on a trailer with four clocks bolted to a shelf. Dad kept them wound. He kept them wound the way other men kept a lawn. Every morning before the first run he'd open the door, let the heat out, and touch each one — index finger to the glass, four times, left to right — and then he'd say the time out loud to nobody, to the salt, to me if I was sitting on the step.

Danny was seven that summer and had a bicycle with no brakes, which on the flats is not the problem it sounds like. There is nothing to hit. He would push off from the trailer and pedal straight out toward the mountains until he was a stroke of colour with a wobble in it, and then a stroke of colour, and then nothing, and then he'd come back an hour later with his lips cracked and white and say there was a lake out there. There wasn't. There never was.

That was the summer of 1987, the summer the record fell, and everything changed for our family. Dad's crew timed the run and afterward there were interviews and a man from Los Angeles came out and there was money for a while and then there wasn't, and by September the whole thing had turned into something we didn't talk about.

The car itself was the colour of a bad bruise, a purple so dark it read black until the sun got low, and it made a noise I have never found the right word for in thirty years of trying. Not a roar. A tearing. Like a bedsheet coming apart lengthwise, held down and pulled, and then the doppler drop as it went past the shack and the clocks did their work and the sound came off the mountains four seconds later, thin, secondhand, the salt's version of what had just happened.

My mother came out to the flats exactly twice. The second time she stood in the doorway of the shack with her arms crossed while Dad was writing numbers on the wall in pencil — he wrote them on the wall, he never used paper — and she said, "Don't you dare tell him it's coming back." I was eleven. I did not know what *it* was and I did not ask, and that is the whole story of me between eleven and about thirty-four.

Salt gets into everything. Into the truck's wheel wells, into the seams of your boots, into a wristwatch. Into film, back when there was film. My mother's word for the flats was *bleached*, which she did not mean kindly, and my father's word was *clean*, and they were describing the same eighty square miles.

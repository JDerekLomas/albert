# Epigraphs and titles for *The Door in the Mountain*

Written 2026-09-08 after a full read of the Sept 7 draft (21 chapters; Ch14 is not in
this drop). Quotes were found in Source Library and are cited by shortlink so anyone can
check the page. Nothing here has been applied to the manuscript; it is a menu.

## Licensing note before quoting any of these
Source Library's English translations are CC-BY-SA 4.0. That is fine for a working draft
but awkward in a trade book (share-alike). For anything that goes to print, quote the
public-domain original (Latin, Greek, Sanskrit, Chinese) and either translate it fresh
or use a public-domain translation (Taylor 1823 for Porphyry, Long 1877 for Epictetus,
Müller 1884 for the Upanishads, the Ante-Nicene Fathers for Lactantius). The Cleaves
*Secret History* is Harvard-Yenching 1982 and still in copyright; a six-line epigraph
is ordinary fair use but the publisher will want it cleared.

## The themes the titles have to serve
- **The margin.** The father's Big Bang story: one particle surviving per billion.
  Returns in the ambulance, under anesthesia, at the camel birth, in Charlie's survival.
- **The search for the source.** Roots, tomb, phantom, imagination, and finally love.
  The Cambridge question in Ch13 ("did all the doors open onto the same place") is the
  hinge; the last page answers it with k'uh, hiimori, mana.
- **Imagination meets reality; it does not bend it.** Parts I to III build "we imagine
  our realities into being." Jamie's death breaks it. Cayambe reframes it. Charlie's
  TEDx line "we don't live reality, we create it" lands with both senses audible.
- **You don't choose the wave.** Hospital pain, "succumb," Pohaku, Inti, Cayambe.
- **Community as the oldest technology.** Anda, crew, Tomnod, ROMP, Tebo1, the
  handprints on the cave becoming the photos on Charlie's ICU wall.
- **Om at every threshold.** Accident, Varanasi, Jamie's death, Charlie's extubation.
- **Duality, danced through.** Sky/earth, wolf/deer, Shiva, Aya Huma, Shadowlight.
- **Loss as metamorphosis.** Rebirthday, Burning Man, Shiva's five heads, monarchs,
  "recovering is not about coming back, it's about becoming."

## Epigraphs

### Book epigraph (currently none): the passage the title comes from
*Secret History of the Mongols* §103, Temüjin coming down from the mountain after the
Merkid raid. This is the "mountain that hid him when he was nothing" Amaraa describes
in Ch2, and the belt-on-the-neck gesture Amaraa repeats on the summit in Ch6.
> By Mount Burqan Qaldun, / As to my life which is only so much as a louse, / I escaped.
> ... Every morning I shall sacrifice unto Mount Burqan. Every day I shall pray unto it.
> Let the seed of my seed observe this.
> — trans. F. W. Cleaves, p. 102 (pp. 101–102 for the whole speech).
> https://sourcelibrary.org/q/Bioqutze2spmVT2YpxW
"The seed of my seed" is the blue spot on Charlie and Millie's backs. Putting this on the
first page tells the reader what the title means before Ch2 has to explain it.

### Part I, The Ground (currently Machado, "the road is made by walking")
Keep Machado. It is the right note for the wandering years. If the *Secret History* is
not used as the book epigraph, it belongs here instead.

### Part II, The Fire (currently "[Better quote]")
Ranked. The first is the recommendation.

1. **Lactantius, *De Ave Phoenice* (c. 300 AD), lines 165–170.** The phoenix is already
   in the book: the stickers on the first prosthetic (Ch9), the story whispered to Charlie
   at extubation (Ch21).
   > That it may be able to be born, it desires first to die. / It is its own offspring,
   > its own father, and its own heir, / Its own nurse, ever a foster-child to itself. /
   > It is indeed the same, yet it is not the same, / Having attained eternal life
   > through the boon of death.
   > Latin: *Ut possit nasci, appetit ante mori. / Ipsa sibi proles, suus est pater et
   > suus heres, / Nutrix ipsa sui, semper alumna sibi.*
   > https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BgUulnu3knOpLXtCY6w
   "It is indeed the same, yet it is not the same" is the filing-cabinet-of-realities
   question Albert asks on waking from the amputation.

2. **Heraclitus, fragment 36 (Diels 67).** Duality and fire in one sentence.
   > God is day and night, winter and summer, war and peace, surfeit and hunger. But he
   > undergoes transformations, just as fire, when it is mixed with spices, is named after
   > the individual scent of each.
   > https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BhEyCwo494MYNtP24iC

3. **Salomon Trismosin, *Aureum Vellus* (1600).** Alchemical, blunt, one line.
   > For that very thing which kills also makes alive again.
   > https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BhbmpwxosXOpLGyaVeg

4. **Sumerian proverb, c. 2000 BC.** The oldest thing in the pile, and the bleakest.
   > What is placed in the fire has a valuable role to play but leaves nothing behind
   > when it's gone.
   > https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BhF3jRmHHbcatfZ1294

5. **Māṇḍūkya Upaniṣad 1**, on Om. Probably better as a book epigraph than a Part II one,
   since Om spans all four parts. (Source Library files this page under a Milarepa
   collection; the Sanskrit on the leaf is the Upaniṣad, so attribute it to that.)
   > Om. This syllable Om is all this. What was, what is, and what will be is all just
   > the syllable Om. And whatever else exists beyond the three times is also just the
   > syllable Om.
   > https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BiWomGmYOHAbZ8OX11b

### Part III, The Door (currently María Sabina, "a world far away, nearby, and invisible")
Keep Sabina; she is the right voice for the cactus and the mushrooms. Two alternates if
the door image wants an older source:

- **Porphyry, *On the Cave of the Nymphs* (3rd c.).** Homer's cave has two gates, and
  the ancients kept silence passing through any door.
  > the wise forbade speaking while passing through either doors or gates, venerating in
  > silence the God who is the principle of the universe.
  > https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BhNfViNqCqqdiI5kB4X
  > both are gates of souls, but one is for those coming toward humans, and the other
  > for those ascending to the gods.
  > https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BeuqrTRbjvzvU6nPxn5
  The second line would also sit well over Ch18 (the lake "between the living and the
  dead").
- **Ge Hong, *Baopuzi* (4th c.), quoting the *Dunjia zhongjing*.** Albert's own ancestral
  tradition, and literally the title.
  > If you seek the Dao, enter the mountain.
  > https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BjXz6MLufZIQMtuYRIq

### Chapter 15 (Wave Riders): Pohaku's creed has two ancient cousins
Not epigraphs necessarily, but Albert may want to know the line "you don't choose the
storm, only how you ride it" has been said before, and could nod to it.
- **Seneca, *De Providentia* II.4.** *Non quid sed quemadmodum feras interest.*
  > It is not what you bear, but how you bear it that matters.
  > https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BiPweMIYPLUg37NUWwT
- **Epictetus, *Discourses* II.5.** The sailing passage.
  > What is in my power? To choose the pilot, the sailors, the day, the opportunity.
  > Then a storm descends. ... I do that which I can: I do not drown in fear, nor
  > screaming, nor blaming God, but knowing that what has been born must also perish.
  > https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BilNqLWaPaFpVAdU8MG

### Part IV, The River (currently Hesse, *Siddhartha*)
Problem: the same Hesse sentence is already quoted inside Ch11 (line "The river flowed and
flowed, and yet it was always there...") in a different translation. Either unify the two
wordings so it reads as a deliberate callback, or move Hesse out of the part opener and
use Seneca here, which says the same thing with the book's own subject in it (the name
stays, the water passes: Jamie, the old Charlie):
> Our bodies are carried away like a river; all that you see runs down with time.
> Nothing remains the same; even while I say these things are changed, I am changed
> myself. ... The river still keeps its name, but the water passes away.
> — Seneca, *Epistle* 58. https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BhAuQfr3YJfYNHgYo3z
Heraclitus fragment 41 is the terser alternative:
> You could not step twice into the same rivers; for other waters are ever flowing on
> to you. https://sourcelibrary.org/q/BhEyCwo494MYNtP24iC

## Titles

### What is working now
The strongest chapter titles are concrete images that mean one thing on the first page
and another by the last: *Rebirthday*, *Limb Salvage*, *The Ghost*, *Burn the Ships*,
*The Crux*, *Shadow and Light*. The weakest are bare place names that could head any
travel chapter: *The Steppe*, *The Desert*, *The Holy Lands*. The four part titles are
elemental and clean (Ground, Fire, Door, River), and "Ground" also puns on
ground-truthing, which the LiDAR chapters use.

### Three collisions to fix
1. **"The Door in the Mountain" is used three times**: book title, Part III title, Ch13
   title. The phrase should belong to the book and to Part III only. Rename Ch13.
2. **"The River" is Part IV and "The Sound of the River" is Ch11**, which is in Part II.
   Rename Ch11.
3. **"Shifting Grounds" (Ch3) echoes "The Ground" (Part I).** Rename Ch3.

### Suggested renames (each one is a phrase from inside its chapter)
| Ch | Now | Suggest | Why |
|---|---|---|---|

| 6 | The Sacred Mountain | **Hiimori** | The wind-horse energy he feels on the summit and lists at the very end beside k'uh and mana. Keeps the mountain phrase out of a chapter title. Alt: *The Soul Banner*, *The Ikh Khorig*. |

Keep as they are: 1 Rebirthday, 4 The Wayfarer, 5 Burn the Ships, 7 The Breaking,
8 Limb Salvage, 9 The Ghost, 10 El Robotico, 18 The Axis Mundi, 19 Shadow and Light,
20 The Aya Huma, 21 The Crux, 22 The Boy and the Butterfly.

### A pattern worth making deliberate
Several chapters already take their title from a word a culture gives him: *El Robotico*,
*Heʻe Nalu*, *The Aya Huma*, *The Axis Mundi*. With *Anda*, *Hiimori*, and *Om* added,
the received-word titles would mark exactly the chapters where the book's closing
sentence is being assembled ("every culture had reached for the same space and found a
different word"). That is a structure a reader can feel without being told.

### Part titles
Leave them. One optional tightening: Part III is the only non-element, and it could be
argued into *The Wind* (hiimori is wind-horse, the waves are wind-born, the mountain
breathes, the Andean flute). But "The Door" carries the book's thesis and should stay.

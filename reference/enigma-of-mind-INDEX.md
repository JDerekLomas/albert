## The argument in brief

Evolution needs three things: variation, replication, selection. Popper's inaugural
Darwin Lecture claimed these forces also run inside the conscious mind. This chapter
says what they are: **variation via imagination**, **replication via sympathy
(resonance)**, **selection via harmony**. The through-line is *convergence* — that
disconnected cultures arrive at the same cognitive technologies, the way Darwin and
Wallace arrived at the same theory, and the way China and Greece arrived at harmony
within a few decades of each other around 550 BC.

## The best concrete facts, with anchors

Ranked by usefulness to the memoir, not by importance to the paper.

1. **The fish glyph** (L1425–1439). The Mayan glyph *tzak*, "conjure (in the context
   of vision)", is written as **a fish held in a left hand**. David Stuart reads it as
   a metaphor: "a slippery fish in hand" for the elusive nature of grasping the
   ineffable. — The memoir's spine is a fish: Millie's first fish (Ch01), Bob Lomas's
   prophecy and the trout (Ch05), Millie's fish hours before the accident (Ch07),
   Charlie and "There was something on the other end" (Ch22 close). Albert's own
   academic chapter contains an ancient glyph in which catching a fish *is* the act of
   conjuring a vision. Nothing else in this repo connects the book's central image to
   its central question this directly.
2. **4 Hz drumming** (L498–502). Accelerating drumming reaching 4 Hz / 240 bpm recurs
   across shamanic traditions worldwide, possibly converged there because it meets the
   brain's natural theta response. — Ch14's litany has three drums (Mongolia, the Shiva
   temple beneath the Ganges, the dreamed Black Sea drum). This is the mechanism under
   "did they all reach for the same place?"
3. **Convergent evolution as the frame** (L495–502). Shared traits imply common
   ancestry *or* convergent evolution from common biology. Albert's litany question,
   stated precisely.
4. **The dedication** (L2012–2015). "We dedicate this chapter to our respective
   fathers, Douglas Lin and Robert Lomas, who inspired us to explore and contemplate
   the furthest realms of our imagination, and thus our universe." Robert Lomas is Bob
   from Ch05. Also the only place the manuscript's world names Albert's father.
5. **Popper** (L242–262). Karl Popper gave the *first* Darwin Lecture, "Natural
   Selection and the Emergence of Mind," arguing selection operates within the
   conscious mind — "the greatest marvel of our universe."
6. **Venus and flutes, 70 cm apart** (L422–454). The Venus of Hohle Fels and the
   earliest known bone flutes were found 70 cm from each other: the emergence of a
   capacity "to shape the material environment for the purpose of evoking experiences
   in other people." Art as technology for producing states in other minds.
7. **The plant asymmetry** (L716–722). Over 200 species of hallucinogenic plants in
   the Americas; about a tenth as many across all Eurasia and Africa. "The reason for
   this imbalance of botanical diversity is an outstanding scientific enigma."
8. **Drums answering drums** (L1085–1089). "Even in ancient times, people may have
   noticed how beating a drum can make another drum rattle or hum, even at a
   distance." The Stoics held the universe was in sympathetic resonance with itself;
   modern cosmology uses resonance to explain the Big Bang's particle production.
9. **Durkheim's collective effervescence** (L1239–1245). "A sort of electricity is
   generated from their closeness… all of whose parts are united and vibrate
   sympathetically."
10. **Sacks on rhythm** (L1134–1137). "There seems to be an actual binding of nervous
    systems accomplished by rhythm."
11. **Huygens, 1665** (L1264–1273). Entrainment discovered by watching two pendulum
    clocks on a shared beam fall into step. Entrainment improves cooperation and
    prosocial behaviour.
12. **Animals resist entrainment** (L1274–1322). Holding a beat is near-uniquely
    human; animals resist external entrainment, plausibly because a predator could
    entrain their heart. So humans may have evolved ways to "let down the guard."
13. **Hyperscanning** (L1323–1334). Two conversing friends show greater neural
    synchrony than two conversing strangers. Ramesh Rao measured heart-rate coherence
    across people in Kundalini meditation; fire-walking produces heart-rate
    synchronisation between walkers and watchers.
14. **Mayan bloodletting** (L1377–1395). Kings pierced their own tongue or penis with
    a stingray spine atop pyramids before gathered subjects, transferring the *ku'h*
    within them to the people. "Bloodletting was resonant but it was not rhythmic."
    "Perhaps ritual experiences that resonate more will replicate more."
15. **Survival of the harmonised** (L1495–1501). Harmony classically means diverse
    elements joined into a well-fitted whole, so "'survival of the fittest' could also
    reasonably be termed 'survival of the harmonised'." Wallace: "man is kept in
    harmony by his mind."
16. **Harmony twice at once** (L1502–1533). Harmony emerged as a dominant theme almost
    simultaneously around 550 BC in China and in Greece — "a coincidence perhaps
    comparable to the simultaneous discovery of Darwinism."
17. **The guitar string** (L1646–1691). Pluck a string and a huge range of frequencies
    is generated, but only those that fit its length survive; the rest are damped.
    That selection is what produces the instrument's sound. Proposed as the brain's
    fitness function.
18. **Octaves in the brain** (L1585–1607). Brainwave bands sit roughly a doubling of
    frequency apart, like musical octaves, which supports periodic synchrony; the
    golden mean has been proposed for *preventing* interference. Harmonic modes in
    the brain resemble Chladni-plate patterns — the direct scientific counterpart of
    Jamie's cymatics work in Ch19.
19. **Stonehenge** (L820–838). Over 100 mushroom-shaped prehistoric carvings,
    interpreted since 1953 as bronze axeheads; the authors argue they are plausibly
    mushrooms — "why carve a weapon of war or labour on a spiritual site?"
20. **Taboo as power** (L940–954). Entheogen taboos may persist because the
    experiences threaten dominant power structures: "if only the high priests could
    access entheogenic experiences, lesser priests and common people were less likely
    to be having different ideas."
21. **The Turrell pyramid** (L1868–1972). March 2019, spring equinox, Yucatán: a stone
    pyramid over a cenote, a cornerless Ganzfeld room, a domed "Metasphere" whose
    flicker was tuned to disrupt the brain's rhythms, a skyspace, and a channel down
    which the equinox sun dropped into the water. "Mystical ego-death" with no
    entheogens — architecture as a cognitive technology.
22. **Ramachandran's question** (L143–144). At their first meeting: "Do we really know
    where the body ends and the mind begins?" That is Ch09's whole chapter in one line,
    in the man's own voice.

## Structural reading

Ch14 states the question in argument and delivers only the imagination leg. Part 4
answers the other two in lived form and never says so: Jamie's violin driving standing
waves in water is a Chladni plate; the jam where "the three lines locked into one that
none of us was steering" is the paper's group-flow passage; Ch22's "harmony and chaos
in nearly equal measure, and harmony won" is selection-by-harmony as earned conviction.
The book's architecture is better than it currently signals — a small amount of setup
in Ch14 would make Part 4 read as the answer rather than as unrelated grief and
recovery. See the comments on Ch14 in the editor.

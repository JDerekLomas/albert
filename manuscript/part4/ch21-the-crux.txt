CHAPTER 21
The Crux

Long ago, my father said it all started in the margin, that infinitesimally small sliver of something over nothing left after the Big Bang, enough to build everything. It was the same margin I had felt during the amputation. It was the margin Charlie would need to survive. Galaxies, stars, oceans, civilizations, and every ruin I had crawled through, music, the particular shape of my children's smile. All of it, from an imbalance so small it shouldn't have mattered. But in that margin anything is possible. Everything I had ever gathered was about to be needed at once.

---

The kids were growing fast now, each year shedding a little of who they'd been. Predictably I had overdone it as softball dad/coach, and that winter Millie, twelve, hung up her bat and turned toward something I couldn't have coached if I tried, figure skating, and fell instantly in love. Charlie, at fourteen, was stretching his wings deeper into the mountains, he yearned to be in its embrace all season long. For a family that lived in San Diego, I had somehow raised two kids who identified as winter athletes.

Every Friday, we would traverse the 395, eight hours north through the dark, past hot springs and dry lake beds of their youth, and into the mountains.

Charlie now skied with his Mammoth freeride team. He had earned it with a deal: he would handle every logistical detail, the gear, the schedules, the parent-volunteer requirements, and clean the car and do the dishes, forever, in exchange for the program fees and the drives. He kept up with it all year, carrying the bags, taking out the trash, even doing Millie's dishes.

On the way he would do his homework in the passenger seat for hours. When we took breaks we played Valorant, an online game he had talked me into learning, my way of staying in his world. After our butts got kicked on my old-man skills, he would tell me to train so we could become the ultimate father-son skier-gamer team. We even designed jackets with badges for the team name. *Vicious Virtue.* We would arrive and he would be off on the mountain all day long or training with the team at a local trampoline facility.

Millie didn't want the long commute every weekend, so she came every other one, a plan worked out amongst her, her mom, and me. Those days I would take her to the mountain's ice rink and watch her skate with a wonderful coach she had found for a while, then sometimes when she had found her flow I would head to the river. She did her homework beside Charlie on the drives, the two of them locked in the same focus, before they fought and played and fought again the way I had with my siblings.

They were growing into themselves and I could feel the years counting down, dreading the morning I would drop them off at a college somewhere and say goodbye. So the long drives each weekend, and the few runs we could get in together as a family before Charlie would peel off to his team or Millie would head to the rink, were something I savored.

---

We talked up and down that special stretch of highway, about the things that surface when the road grows dark and there is nothing else to see but headlights stretching into the distance. I was still moving through the fog of the year Jamie had left us.

"Dad, what do you think happens after we die?" Charlie asked me one night.

It was for a school essay he was writing on world philosophies, but he had already been witness to it once. I told him about the meditations I had learned with Jamie, about the stillness between thoughts. I told him I didn't think things really left. They changed state, and that I had felt her presence appear in the many synchronicities of each day.

"I've noticed that too," he said.

I described what I had seen across my travels. The tombs and temples of the world, from the sarcophagi of the Cloud Warriors to the sacred mountain in Mongolia. How in Varanasi the rituals on the Ganges were designed to release the living from their pain, but also the dead from the cycles of existence entirely, to skip reincarnation and go straight to nirvana.

The headlights of oncoming cars washed over his face and disappeared as he stared into the darkness of the night.

Then he said he understood the concept, but wondered, "Why would you skip the cycle if you liked being in it?"

---

When they were off in their respective winter worlds I would find a long stretch of the Owens River where it wound through a mesa of pale volcanic rock, a place holding some of the most enigmatic rock art in the American West. As I entered the land I passed figures carved into the stone thousands of years ago, some of them facing straight up at the sky.

I waded in from the snowy banks alone.

Long ago Gordo had taught me to read the water. Now it felt like it was reading me. I stood in its embrace and watched the winter arrive and peak and fade in the movement of the water, the spawn coming and going, the catalog of bug hatches revolving with the weeks. It was breathing with the seasons and so was I.

I was grieving.

One weekend the rink at Mammoth closed and Millie said she would come to the river with me. We drove south along the Upper Owens. Snow on the pasture, dark water sliding between white banks.

We pulled over at a bend and gathered our waders from the back of the truck. Then, sitting on the tailgate with one boot of her waders on, she said it.

"I wish I'd spent more time with Jamie before. I was scared to see her at the end."

I told her like I had said to Charlie, that I still talked to her all the time. That she seemed to be everywhere now.

Millie gripped my hand as we stepped down through a foot of crusted snow over mud, the silt sucking at our boots, and into the river. The water was so cold it felt like it had teeth. We picked our way slowly over the slippery rocks below the surface. At some point it wasn't clear who was supporting who, where she helped me balance as much as I helped her. On the far bank we stood in the frozen grass and watched the river move past us.

That night a text came out of nowhere, one of Jamie's oldest friends, up the next day for a gig and wanting to come by. I showed it to Millie. "See."

---

It was "miracle March," late-season storms, the mountains buried in powder. We woke hours before the lifts opened and chased first tracks to the sound of hoots and hollers from skiers ascending on the chairs above us. The first morning the whole operation was held back as avalanche conditions kept the top closed. On the second day, we made it on the very first gondola up, the anticipation overwhelming.

The storm had laid an untouched blanket of white across the whole peak. Charlie dropped in ahead of me. His skill had passed mine years before. I watched him fly in tight arcs through the powder, jumping off mini cliffs, his happiness reaching back up the slope. I felt free with him, gliding along with joy on one leg made of flesh and the other a prosthetic ski foot that clipped right into the bindings. It was the best single morning we had ever had.

Then he was off with his team, honing their freeride skills all over the mountain.

Millie had her "breakthrough day" too, bravely facing down her first real double black diamonds with a grace she had been refining on the ice. She looked at me with joy, proud and excited to show Charlie what she had accomplished. I beamed back, I finally had my two ski buddies.

Then I took her home to San Diego before leaving for Chicago to give a talk, while Charlie stayed with his teammate Piper and her family, wanting to squeeze the last ounces of winter before the seasons changed. It was their spring break.

Piper's parents, Deena and Andrew, had moved there decades ago to train at altitude.

Deena was baking cookies with them.

---

I was in the Field Museum in Chicago when the first phone call arrived.

I had come in the hours before a talk I was giving nearby to look for a world thirty-one thousand years gone, and there is almost nothing of it. Just a few worked stones in a case.

What I was really there to do was to try and imagine Liang Tebo.

Maxime Aubert's team had been excavating it for years, a limestone cave in Borneo, and in the floor of it a child with the lower leg taken off below the knee, just like mine. Stone cut marks on the bone. Years of healing afterward. The kid had survived.

And it was old. Older than anything I had ever stood in front of. Roughly forty times older than Genghis Khan, seven times older than the Pyramids of Egypt, six times older than the first fish hooks which I had held on the shores of Oman. There were over twenty thousand years separating this find from anything I had ever seen.

"It's the oldest successful operation, Albert. The handprints are there too, we also just found the oldest rock art in the world nearby too, much older," he had said on the call. "It's the cradle of care and imagination, and we think they were amongst the first seafarers too."

The handprints were ochre blown imprints onto the walls, like the ones I had seen on the Maya Lake. But here they surrounded a story of a single person who represented a nucleus of compassion in the human record.

This group of humans in the dawn of our species had stopped their lives to invest in the care of another, and did so remarkably. Long before modern medicine or metal blades and in a time where mobility meant survival. How did they do it?

I started referring to the kid as Tebo1, the first. The source.

No one can truly know what our human mindset was like thirty-one thousand years ago, but I was doing my best to imagine it.

I wandered the Pacific exhibits, looking over outriggers and a video of a ceremony where they deactivated a canoe by breaking it apart, the belief being that the canoe itself was alive and had a spirit, and when its job was done its energy needed to be returned to the water. I thought of the Lacandon and how they had arrived at a similar understanding.

I was visualizing those voyagers, going into the unknown together. I pondered Tebo1 as a point when we humans found our strength in each other through empathy and compassion. I had lived that story, so many had been my handprints on the wall.

That's when Deena called.

She was forcing a calm demeanor. Piper had called her from the mountain in a panic, asking on behalf of the ski patrol whether Charlie had any allergies. No details yet. She was driving up to find out.

"Does he have any allergies?" she asked me.

The phone went quiet. I sat there turning the question over. Such a simple thing for them to need. And then the cold came. Why were they asking me? Why wouldn't Charlie just tell them himself?

The phone rang again. She had gotten to him now, and her voice had changed. "I'm handing you to the patroller."

A pause, then a man's voice. "Is this Charlie's dad?"

"Yes. What's going on?"

"There's been an accident, it's serious. All I can tell you right now is that he's breathing and he has a pulse."

Frigid Chicago air. A taxi. The hairs on the back of my neck. Then the call from the life flight medic, and through the phone the mechanical rhythm of a ventilator breathing for my son, the beep of his heart monitor.

Stillness.

My mind reached through the phone and I was there with him, seeing the stretcher, the tubes, the aircraft. They had cut his Mammoth Mountain team jacket off him with medical shears, the jacket he had worked all year for. I imagined his small ship of blinking lights and strangers' hands, carrying my boy through the darkness. A thousand miles between us.

I had held the bones of the dead across the world. I had chanted over the dying. I had lost my own leg and rebuilt myself from what was left. None of it could protect me from that sound, the most brutal of my life.

---

Memory fades from that stretch, a swirl of plane rides and airports. I don't remember much of arriving in the pediatric ICU, the way it is when the brain protects you from the deepest traumas. But there, beneath a manufactured sky of tiny lights designed to soothe injured brains, lay my boy. Tubes, bandages, neck brace, a pressure bolt in his skull. I could see Piper and Deena holding his hand, having been first to reach him on the mountain. Kara there too, shaking, we hugged as two parents of the same being. The doctors' words. Brain bleeds. Coma. Diffuse axonal injury. Severe traumatic brain injury, a TBI. Reset.

I looked at him and wondered, was he still in there?

Nine years before, my parents had stood at the foot of my own bed asking the same question. Now Charlie was the one in the bed, and I was the one standing.

Fight or flight?

Fight.

I would need to pull from everything I had ever learned in life to help my boy.

---

Kara cleaned his nails and picked the EEG adhesive out of his hair while I imposed my own protocols on the staff, a low hum of binaural tones around his wounded brain, reading through the night for anything that might help him. Our son needed both of us.

"We have to build the environment of calm for his brain. Silence the monitor alarms inside his room, stop the constant string of nurse rotation visits," I stated, pushing back each time they wanted to rub his sternum for 3 AM assessments and scream "Can you hear me!" at his blank face.

His body's rhythm had shattered along with everything else, and I knew from the nomads I had lived with that the body's natural rhythm follows the earth's cycles of dark and light. Each morning I opened the curtains and let the desert sunrise fill the room. Each night I sat beside him in the dark, guarding the calm, staring up at a single painting on the wall of a wolf looking to the moon.

I had only packed for a two-day trip to Chicago, and as the days clicked by I looked increasingly like someone who should be a patient, not an advocate. I didn't want my son's first real memories of his rebirth to be that his father stunk. For everyone's sake I would have to do something about that.

At one point the doctors had wanted to restrain his arms, worried that he would accidentally pull the tubes out as he slept. I refused. "A brain this open would absorb whatever surrounded it," I argued. I would not let fear and constraint be the first thing written into his new mind.

Kara and I took turns keeping guard instead. Those were long hours through the night, silent, searching for answers in a darkened room. At some point in the stress I started developing shingles. "Self-care," the nurse said to my closed ears. There was no time for that.

I watched the monitor, as I had watched Jamie's less than a year before. Brutally, the whole world had distilled again to a few lines of data tracing across a black screen. The entirety of my life had pointed at this moment, and still I could not stop asking why. What cruelty of fate would hurt my own child? My children were what mattered to me more than anything. Had life not shown me enough already!?

I wanted to be angry, but there was nothing to point it at.

The Volcano, the river, the wave, my phantom. They had all been trying to teach me the same thing, over and over, that you do not get to choose what comes, only how you meet it.

I would not let self-pity or rage take the room while my own boy lay there fighting for his life.

Deep breath, then back to the monitor.

Charlie, behind those closed eyes and sustained by machines, was on a journey of epic proportions.

Stillness.

---

Days had passed and not even a twitch of a finger.

I had called out to the world for visualization, not sympathy. Everything is imagined first before it becomes real, I told myself once again.

*Picture him whole...* I asked the world through openhearted posts.

Baba Lutlo called his name from the Ganges. Pohaku chanted to the ocean from the North Shore. Qasim's mother slaughtered a goat in Pakistan and fed an orphanage in his karmic honor. The prayers came from every shore I had ever set foot on, from churches and temples both carved and natural.

And then once again, the light in the room seemed to thicken, just as it had for Jamie, until you could almost reach out and move it with your hands. Piper saw it too.

Piper brought a can of yerba maté from her bag one afternoon, Charlie's favorite drink. She held the open can under his nose. The EEG monitor next to his bed spiked.

He was still in there! He was still alive in his unconscious mind.

Then one day while Kara and I were out of the room talking to the doctors, Deena sat alone with him and counted him down from ten.

"Ten. You're walking through the doors of the Mammoth Mountain Inn. Nine, you step out into the snow. Eight, the cold air rushes over your skin." She whispered him all the way to chair one. "Now pick up a handful of snow and bring it to your lips."

Charlie's lips moved ever so slightly around the breathing tube that kept him alive.

They had told us the gray and white matter of his brain had sheared due to the rotational forces of impact, that the axons that connect thoughts and signals throughout had been damaged. But there he was, responding to an image in his mind.

To me Deena was the team mom that had welcomed us into the tight-knit mountain community that traveled around with their kids on competition days: firemen, former ski patrollers, others from the mountain who had their kids on the team.

But Deena Kastor was also one of America's most celebrated distance runners in history, although she wore it so lightly that you would never know unless told. She had won an Olympic medal in the marathon and set the world records, the American records, and had written a book on the one thing that carried her through the miles, the mind.

The power of visualization through vast trials of endurance was what she brought to his bedside.

She took the bracelet off her wrist and put it on his. It read *connections.* That's what he needed. To make the connections.

---

If he made it, if his eyes opened he would need to see things that could help him rebuild his connections. "Scaffolding to construct identity from," Leo had explained over the phone.

So we began hanging photos and messages on every wall until the room looked like a cave. The marks of his community were now his handprints.

When he heard what we were doing the man who operated the local printing store began personally delivering orders to the ICU after work.

Images would pour in from his life, buddies in their giant jeans posing with him at their favorite hangout on top of a parking garage, or his teammates with him after his first competition, his schoolmates in their scuba diving cert training. Kara found a photo of their trip in Hawaii, his arms wrapped around Diana, her fiancée, her, and Millie. Kara had found love again and it was a good thing for everyone. Diana was at home with Millie, we asked them to be on standby till we knew which way things were going.

I placed a giant photo of him shooting his bow next to another with his sister and me standing on a dune in the desert. I even put a photo up of our video game load-in screen. We were creating a mural of his life for him to see upon arrival, so if even one image triggered a memory he could build upon it. Without memories what are we?

Beyond the photos, gifts kept arriving from afar, both in the hospital mail and in moments of synchronicity. Like with the guitar.

I wanted a way to play for him, so Deena and Piper drove me to a small store nearby, not so much a store as someone's home that had turned into a music shop. It was the first time I had left the hospital since it all started, and at first stepping out of that vortex into a world that was not in a state of emergency was strange, like being in a psychedelic trip surrounded by people who were not.

There was a beautiful one covered in inlays, but something about an old dusty and cracked nylon stringed Spanish guitar in the corner drew me. Piper pointed to it. "That one," she said. It played more beautifully than anything I had ever held. The fractures were part of what gave it its voice. The white guitar strap that came with it had a tag on it that read *Lakota buffalo leather.* Holding it I felt Jamie reaching through.

For those brutal days I strummed softly next to him, trusting he could hear my familiar melodies and it would hold him in the realm of the living.

---

Then there was breath.

Just over three days in, they made the first attempt to take him off the ventilator. It failed. They dropped the sedative dose slowly, and when the time was right turned the ventilator off. He was not conscious but his body should have been functioning enough to breathe on its own. Thirty minutes in, his lungs couldn't sustain it and the machine kicked back on. He still couldn't live under his own power. I stood listening to the mechanical rhythm resume and my heart sank.

The next day we tried again. He would need to be able to sustain an hour of independent breathing before they would remove the tubes into his lungs.

Piper at one hand. Kara the other. Deena against the wall, sending what she called a beam of light into him from her mind. I stood next to his head. I hadn't planned anything. But you use what you know, and I just started chanting, softly.

The same sound Bruna had chanted over me in the dirt when my own body had been crushed. The sound I had chanted over Jamie as she crossed the threshold. Baba Lutlo had led me to it on the banks of the Ganges and called it the primordial sound of everything, older than any word.

I knew this doorway from both sides now. I had been carried through it myself on an operating table and come back, and I had knelt at Jamie's side as it opened all the way.

Now I bent to my son's ear at the same threshold and called him back through it, toward life.

Om. Om. Om.

I had stood at a lot of these doors by then. What decides who comes out the other side is the story you tell yourself going in.

"This is your rebirth," I whispered into his ear. "You get to choose now. This can be the thing that ends you, or it can be the beginning of your hero's journey."

His body struggled. We held our positions. Minutes passed. Then his breathing began to change. The rigid cadence of the machine softened into something uneven and imperfect and unmistakably his own. His lungs, remembering what they were made for. He was coming back.

Between the Oms I told him the story of the phoenix, the mythical bird that burns down to ash and lifts itself back out of it, again and again. I kept my voice low and even, doing what I could to hold him calm, my eyes on the intracranial pressure (ICP) numbers coming out of the bolt in his skull.

Was he hearing me? Was I annoying him? Monitor is stabilizing, keep going.

The long hour of purgatory was tense, nobody moved. Nobody spoke except me into his ear. We stood where we were, in the positions we had chosen, afraid that leaving them would break whatever was happening.

We knew it had passed when a rush of doctors entered, sat him up like they were balancing a limp noodle, and began extubating.

I held my breath as they reached for the tube.

What came next was both violent and holy.

Fluids and bile erupted from him as the tube was pulled from his throat. His body convulsed with the first unassisted coughs, raw and guttural, the sounds of a body reclaiming itself.

I have witnessed three births in my life, my two children and a camel. This was the fourth. None had been gentle arrivals.

A rupture.

The boundary between one existence and another torn open by force, and now he was on the other side.

Like a person entering the world for the first time.

Suddenly, he was breathing on his own.

His eyes opened briefly, glancing through a room full of alarms and voices and the bustle of nurses adjusting monitors. They asked him to try to move his hand.

His thumb went up. Barely. Unmistakably a thumbs-up.

Piper grabbed Kara's arm. Deena dropped her head and cried silently in joy. I fully exhaled for what felt like the first time since walking into that ICU.

Then he dropped back into sleep. There was a lot of sleeping at first.

---

A day later.

"Do you know where you are?"

His head shakes indicated no.

"Do you know your name?"

No, again.

"Do you know who this is?"

"You're the guy with the backpack. Derek," he said through a barely audible whisper.

"I'm Albert, son... Your father," I managed to respond.

He raised his eyebrows before falling back to sleep.

I didn't make it to the hospital garden before the sobs overtook me. My son didn't know my own face. It was worse than death. All that time looking for ancient memories, and the only ones I wanted to recover now had been wiped from my son's mind. I cried the deep cry. Then when there was nothing left I took a long breath.

Live in the duality with him, dance through it. All we needed was the tiny sliver in between the margins, I reminded myself.

---

Slowly he began to come back, the conscious and unconscious functions of his being returning in whispers. A year before I had watched the parts of a person I loved go out one by one. Now I watched someone I loved return.

Before he could find words his eyebrows were his primary language, two tufts of hair bouncing up and down. It was how he said *yes, I'm here.* How he told you he understood. A little wave of approval from above his eyes before he had words.

He scanned the photos on the walls for hours. When he began talking again, his questions were almost infantile. "Why is he washing his hands? Why is he turning on the sink?"

After a few days the photos started working as we had hoped. He'd look at one and remember a name, then another. But his eyes kept drifting back to the desert photo of him and Millie, the two of them years ago, running across desert sand, and he stayed on it longer than any of the rest. The truth was she was the only one he had remembered from the start. Before he knew my face, he had known his sister.

"Where's Millie?" he asked. I explained that she would be coming soon, that we were far from home and that we had wanted to keep her home till he was a little stronger so as not to scare her. "I hope this experience doesn't hurt any of you," he said.

His left leg would not answer his brain. He was seeing double. The long axons that carried the signal had been severed at their source. The doctors had warned us to expect anger, personality changes, frustration. Instead, Charlie asked to kiss everyone who came into the room on the forehead.

As he spent more time awake he was constantly reorienting himself. One day when he realized he wasn't wearing any clothes a sudden wave of self consciousness took over and he asked for his team jacket. Piper repeated to him that he had hit a big jump and had an accident and all his clothes had been ripped off of him as a result. He was amazed. "I hit a jump so hard that my pants fell off!? That must have looked incredible from the lift!"

Finally the feeding tube came out, and then came the food.

I was obsessed with the right fuel for a rebuilding brain, the same instinct that had once had me chasing a pregnant Kara around with packets of iodized salt. This time was easier. I had the advisor of an olympian super human to help me design a nutrition plan. And with the memory of a goldfish he complied happily with whatever I handed him. Deena and I filled carts at the organic market with blueberries, nuts, yogurt, cod liver oil, and mountains of salmon. Salmon for breakfast. Salmon for lunch. Salmon for dinner. More salmon. "The body needs the whole foods to absorb the nutrients that we regenerate with," she told me.

The room must have smelled like a wharf. One day he turned to me, confused. "My skin feels funny, kinda burning." We pulled his shirt up and his torso was so full of food it looked like he was pregnant. The wires in his brain that once told him when he was satiated had been detached.

I added supplements too. Whatever the research pointed to for a brain in the acute phase, NAC and creatine amongst them. I logged each day's micro-progressions and sent them out to a network of friends, neuroscientists and cognitive scientists and AI people, my crew from Roatán, then met the attending physicians in the hallway each morning with whatever had come back overnight.

I was relentless. My thinking was that in the early days every small adjustment could steer the whole long voyage, the way a few degrees at the start decide which shore a ship reaches.

Most of all we needed joy. If he was rewiring himself back together it should be a happy state. When I realized he had forgotten how to smile or frown, I propped him up in front of the bathroom mirror.

"Frown like this. Now big smile like this."

For a boy who had never lived a day without a giant smile, this simple most human thing, to share emotion, had become a skill to relearn. I had taken so much for granted.

Rama once told me that the same cells that let you feel what someone else is feeling were the ones that conjured a leg for me out of nothing. I do not know whether those were the ones that broke. From his deeply considerate demeanor I knew my son felt everything, but he could not get any of it to the surface, the opposite of what my own body did to me.

---

When Millie arrived a week after the initial accident she came with Diana. At first she walked in like nothing was wrong. She and Charlie had traveled between two homes their entire lives, each other's fixed point through every upheaval. It must have been brutally hard for her to see him like that, but the cracks didn't show. Her silliness covered them up as she joked he looked like an *umpalompa.*

She didn't stay long in the room. She sat in the hallway with Piper and played Uno, her eyes never quite landing on him, while Kara gave me a look that said *let it go,* knowing what was really happening underneath.

Later that night she called me sobbing from the room at the Ronald McDonald house where she, Kara, and Diana were staying while I slept in the hospital. I had bought her a journal and told her to write, and told her we would get through this together.

*He says he remembers me and he held my hand. He said he was in Anza Borrego but he is not. Today Mom me Diana and dad cried,* she wrote.

From the next morning on she was at his side feeding him salmon, playing cards on his legs, never leaving.

"Would you put some salmon on the honey?" he said in a soft monotone voice.

She obliged, giggling at his word salad.

She told him about all the friends he had at school. How word had spread and everyone, both her middle school and his high school, which were on the same campus, knew and was asking with care. "You're famous!" she said. He raised his eyebrows again.

That day she took him to the garden in a wheelchair. Pulled off his shoes. Pressed his feet into the grass.

"Wiggle your toes, Charlie."

He looked down for a while, staring intensely at his foot. The electrical signal of his eyes to his brain, then his brain to his toes traveled across an odyssey of synapses and axons linking a forgotten pathway between mind and body. "Where does the mind end and the body begin?" Rama had once asked me. And now my boy was pushing through that same frontier. "You can do it Charlie," Millie repeated. All of a sudden his left toes moved for the first time!

We jumped for joy and the sides of his mouth turned to the sky.

Millie had been beside me through my recovery too. She had been the one who refused to let me stay still, with the broken wires of a phantom limb. Now she was twelve, doing the same thing for her brother. She had faced what had come and grown into the most powerful healer in the room.

But his fire came from within. "Ten," Millie said, holding up the breathing exercise device for his collapsed lung. He did twelve, then raised his eyebrows in satisfaction.

Eventually it was time to go to rehab. Long hugs from his wheelchair as he said goodbyes to nurses, doctors, and staff that had cared for him. Piper and Deena said their goodbyes too. Millie had to return to school, and Kara and I agreed at least one of us had to keep working to pay for whatever was to come. We decided I would go with Charlie and they would fly Millie home. They could barely walk away when the car came to pick them up.

Then it was just us.

"There's roughly six months of hyper neuroplasticity following a TBI like this," the neurosurgeon had said. "That's when you can see the most gains."

Six months was what they had given Jamie to go. Now I had six months to bring Charlie back.

Now I stood at the foot of my own son's mountain, my hands out for him, the way he had once held them out for me.

The rehab hospital we had moved to sat near the giant Santa Cruz redwoods, a place I had hiked long ago as a boy his age. When we left a few weeks later, earlier than anyone had expected, it felt like breaking out of jail. It had been nearly a month living in medical facilities, and we both yearned for nature.

So we went.

With walking sticks for support, thirty minutes after he was discharged, he looked up at the enormous towering trees. Their roots extended into the earth, the mycelium network connecting them to each other like a giant brain more than a forest. He traced the knots in their bark with his fingers, and together we took a deep breath, purging the processed air from the weeks before and breathing in the freshly generated oxygen of the forest. It felt like it had when I had first stood over the Maya jungles, the oxygen tingling in our brains. Together we were bathing in it.

"I am gonna figure this out dad," he said to me.

He smiled. I smiled back. He had survived.

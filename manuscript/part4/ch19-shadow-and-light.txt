CHAPTER 19
Shadow and Light

The latest season of filming was done, and narration work was scheduled for the week after I got back.

The dirt was barely off me as I headed out the door to the recording studio, that's when the text came in.

---

For years, in the windows between the lost cities, I had been finding a sound at home. I would come off an expedition still carrying whatever the last land had sounded like, and pour it into fatherhood and into jam sessions with Jamie and Leo, reforming myself through the strums of a guitar and the instruments and tunings I kept bringing back from the world. It seemed like every land had a specific sound. We never played the same thing twice.

A drum had come the year after Varanasi. National Geographic had arranged a spotlight on the story of how I got my start in Mongolia, and a film crew from Bulgaria had shot a recreation of the night on the sacred mountain, the seven shamans, the fire, the drums. They arrived at the house with a gift from the shoot, a hand drum made by a woman in the forests above the Black Sea. We unwrapped it on the living room floor. It was octagonal, broad as a serving tray, the same shape as the drums that had arrived on the Burkhan Khaldun years before. The hairs on my neck rose as I held it in my hand, so much had happened since the crescendo that had flowed from that mountain.

When I lifted it to the window, the sun light came through from behind and threw the shadow of the leather ties across its face in triangular struts. Jamie went still. "That's the drum I dreamed!" she said, describing what she had seen in detail. She never let anyone but the two of us hold that drum. Her stage name was Shadowlight, and it was the shadow from light that connected her to that drum.

People had started asking whether something was developing between us, but it wasn't really like that. One afternoon, on her way out the door, Jamie stopped and told me she was never going to be the woman who drove my kids to soccer. It wasn't her. That settled it. Spirit twins, we called it. Friends from a life or version of us that couldn't really be placed.

But the kids fell in love with her anyway, only more, as an auntie and as the magical wizard she'd revealed herself to be. One night the whole city lost power, some unforeseen failure in the grid. Jamie and Leo were over for a jam when it happened, and we continued by candlelight as the kids ran down from their rooms to gather around the remaining light in the house. She told the kids they could move fire with their minds by watching the flame, accompanying their attempts with the mystical sounds of her violin. Every time the orange would flicker, they screamed with delight, convinced she was a wizard.

The kids were with their mother the week Jamie, Leo, and I loaded the gear into the truck and drove east until the city dropped behind a ridge. "We're the Infinite Vortices," Jamie said. "We open a portal every time we play." Leo brought the LED rigs he had built for these nights. There was no one out here to perform for. That was the whole point. We would have our tiny concert in the desert, playing as loudly as we liked for the rocks and the open sky above.

We set up in a dry wash on a pile of blankets, the battery amps and pedalboard setups that we'd built, a microphone, and the instruments that had followed me home from other countries, none of them tuned the same. The drum in my lap. I struck it into the mic and it reverberated across a long delay so each strike came back a half-beat behind itself and stacked into a looped pattern that seemed to hold itself up in the air. I accelerated, and the beat began to smooth my mind. As it had been with the shaman on the mountain and beneath the temples of Varanasi, the beat accelerated into an explosive release that shook the earth beneath me. Then silence.

Slowly, I picked up my guitar and began to strum. For a year we had been loosening the strings together, dropping one a quarter turn and striking it and waiting, then dropping it further, dowsing for the place where the notes stopped needing to be held down and rang into each other on their own. That night my hands found it.

I slipped off it once and stopped. "Just do it again," Jamie said, the way she always did. "Then it's part of the song." So I did it again. Her five-stringed violin had no frets, nothing fixed on it anywhere, freer than the doctrine of my guitar, and she slid up into the tuning and flowed through it. Leo felt the rhythm pulsing through the drones of our harmonics and laid beats underneath. The three lines locked into one that none of us was steering.

We had played this way a hundred times, but that night a tiny bit more of me let go that hadn't before. I stopped trying, and the music started playing itself. We fell all the way into it, further than we'd ever been, running for hours. The moon crossed the wash. Coyotes answered off to the east. At one point Jamie lifted her electrified violin into the air and let the wind take the string, and it sang a tone none of us was playing, the sound of whales crossing the invisible sand, so big it put tears on our faces.

---

The last time I saw Jamie play in public had been a few months earlier, a sold-out performance in an IMAX dome theater in San Diego. She had built a projection rig that fed the resonance of her violin into a shallow plate of water at the front of the room. The shapes the water made under the bow were thrown up onto the curved space above us.

The lights went down. I sat near the back with Charlie and Millie.

She started low. A single note held for a long time. The water on the screen above didn't move at first. Then it did. Rings opened. The rings became hexagons. The hexagons broke and reformed into dancing shapes. I could feel the violin in my sternum. The whole theater vibrated.

She moved her bow with deep intentionality and the water kept moving casting brilliant images all around us, transporting our minds into a space where magic seemed to form in the harmonies of waves.

"We are made of water," she used to say when she was developing her cymatics work with a performance billed *Resonance*.

When the show was done the kids ran up to their pirate wizard auntie.

She was wearing earrings painted as monarch wings. In the summer she turned her tiny apartment patio into a thicket of milkweed, laughing with delight at the idea of playing for the chrysalises hanging there, the tiny beings in metamorphosis absorbing her vibrations as they formed their wings.

---

"They found a tumor and said I have six months to live."

It felt like the wind had been pulled from my chest as I read the words on my phone. I dropped to my knees in the doorway and screamed "No!" into it.

Four days later Pohaku called from the North Shore. Giant storms had been sweeping across the Pacific, filling the dried-out desert lake beds she and I used to make music on, for the first time in tens of thousands of years. "We come from the water, and we return to it," he said.

I walked through the doors of the hospital where my children had been born and my leg had been removed. Dozens of Jamie's friends overflowed her room into the hallway. When she saw me she called me over.

"They said I'm dying."

She looked scared. She looked fierce.

---

As a gigging musician she never had much money to start with, but she was beloved and that made her rich in community. Michelle offered her the guest apartment on her property on the cliffs as the staging ground for the fight. It was a beautiful Spanish-style estate full of ritual masks and drums from around the world, garden spilling vegetables and fruit, one dog dyed like a rainbow candy cane that would wander into the heaviest moments and plop down on Jamie's feet.

Musicians and friends from all parts of her life came with offerings and care, it was like watching a deity forming right in front of me. And while a genuinely massive outpouring of love surrounded her, she turned to me to be her primary caregiver, to help her navigate decisions that had giant consequences. She had become like family to me, and her parents had died years before. And so it began.

Santiago, a Colombian guitarist who had been in a band with her and loved Jamie like a sister, became my right hand in supporting her through what was to come. His was a massive heart that seemed to almost burst out of him in every direction. He stayed at her side on the nights I couldn't. And still, exhausted beyond reason in the ICU, he'd find the energy to hit on a doctor as she walked through, then, when she gently turned him down, ask her for a warm blanket instead. We called ourselves the guardians of the temple.

Jamie was determined to fight, and we followed her lead. If she had one word for what she wanted, it was sovereignty. Sovereignty over every decision about her own body, her treatment, her death if it came to that.

So even as her caregiver, I learned to sit as an observer, doing as she instructed, playing music beside her and learning from her experience as she moved through her own transformation. Foods that might help her body resist. Tinctures. And eventually, singing bowls that I carried into the infusion centers, awkwardly explaining to the doctors that we needed to resonate the medicine first, per her command. No one would dare question her.

Yet she knew how to have fun with her power. She commanded Leo not to cut his hair until she was healed. His usually buttoned-up tech entrepreneur look transformed with a giant fro that emerged over the months to her joyous delight.

And all the while, beneath the lighter moments, she was facing the impossible.

She would stand stoic on the cliffs staring into the storms, holding her father's walking stick like a staff. In the gaps between torrents of wind and rain the sun would emerge, casting rainbows in rapid succession.

Santiago turned to me one afternoon.

"Hay Belleza en la tormenta."

There is Beauty in the storm.

I was jumping between worlds. Not expeditions, but now between emergency rooms and school pickups.

Sometimes I would return after a long stretch at the hospital and find Millie, now eleven, had made dinner. Charlie, at thirteen, had set the table. They had grown through transformation time and time again. I was proud of them. But it also broke my heart. They had known her most of their lives.

That fall Charlie had begun waking me at five-thirty each morning to drive him to the gym before school, building his body for his dreams to become a pro skier. On the pre-dawn drives he would ask me about how she was doing. "Remember when she taught me to move the fire of a candle with my mind?" he asked. "Maybe she can fix this with hers."

Millie as a tween was emerging into her own world too, navigating the start of middle school with a piercing wit. One night Charlie tried to pull rank, "you don't get it, I'm older," and she told him being older just meant he'd been wrong about things for longer. But when she could feel I was struggling, she would quietly come sit beside me, wrapping her arm around me in long hugs. She too had grown up to the sounds of Jamie's violin ringing through our home.

---

Months passed. One spring afternoon Jamie's strength rebounded. She picked up the violin and played as she had long before. The brain fog that had been breaking the connection between her mind and her fingers vanished, and suddenly you could see the layers of awareness return. Her vibrations sang through the small apartment while a few friends and I watched in awe.

She set the violin down to go rest.

"There is so much love," she said, looking over her dramatically thinned shoulders. "It all turns into love."

That night I finally opened *The Tibetan Book of Living and Dying*. Handed to me on the origin lake of the Maya, under a full moon months before. I had not been ready for its preparatory prose and neither had she, but the time had come.

We started reading it together. The hospice nurses had left a pamphlet on the stages of decline, to my surprise it mirrored so many of the observations within the Tibetan book. The body slowing. Breath shifting. The senses turning inward. The same progression, described one way by palliative science and another by fourteenth-century Tibetan monks. Richard Davidson had once told me about the meditators he had spent his life studying, and how their people left the body alone for a time afterward so the mind could finish. Jamie's friend Koffe said his people in Uganda did the same.

It described the simplest meditation to ready the mind for its reentry into the entirety of the universe.

"When one past thought has ceased and a future thought has not yet risen, in that gap, in between, isn't there a consciousness of the present moment?" it read.

"Expand the gap between thoughts."

---

Her body continued to shut down, and she began to see people the rest of us could not. An indigenous chief. Children. Musicians. Cats. She had entire conversations in Korean with her ancestors.

Friends came and played, and her creative output intensified the more her body failed, terminal lucidity, the hospice nurses called it, the unexpected clarity that sometimes precedes death. Hers seemed to last not days but weeks.

She started living only by her stage name. Shadowlight.

One afternoon, exhaustion in every line of her face, she summoned the strength to hold our octagonal drum in the air and sing. Sunlight filtered through a prism on the window and cast a rainbow over her.

She looked at me and smiled.

"It's fun, you know? All of it."

I laughed. "It's true, but how can this be so fun?"

"Because when there are no options left, all you have is your imagination," she said mischievously. "And if you have a good imagination, everything can be magic."

She told me about the place she was visiting. Regular day stuff, she said. Normal activities. "Just better lighting." She was making friends there.

I felt like an awkward guest sometimes, sitting quietly as she communed with the unseen. Cultures around the world have described this. Christianity saw saints and angels gathering near death. Hindu scripture describes Yama's messengers, the Maya had their dogs at the threshold.

Every tradition described what Jamie was experiencing.

The thoughts of Hesse's *Siddhartha* were in the air. It turned out it had been her father's personal favorite too, and he had read it to her countless times before. I now read it softly next to her, contemplating the infinite depths of a single being.

One night, to catch my breath, I took a long walk through Michelle's kitchen and into the atrium where ceremonial masks from around the world hung on every wall. At one end was a large painting I had passed countless times. I stopped in front of it now.

A small family on the tundra, two children being sent off on a reindeer-drawn sled in front of a teepee-like yurt. Years before, Amaraa had taken me north to visit the reindeer herders who lived in tents like those, at the far edge of Mongolia. I looked at the faces of the people on the faded canvas, their high cheekbones the same ones that gave away Jamie's Korean blood. It felt like a version of us was up there.

I couldn't help but wonder, had we done this before, in some earlier turn of the cycle? I stood there a long time.

---

On June 6, 2024, in the soft morning light, on the mesa above the Pacific where one of the most ancient burials in North America had been found a decade earlier, Shadowlight crossed.

She wore the Mongol fur hat I had gotten long ago on the steppe and given her, she called it her magic hat. In her other hand was the bone of a buffalo wrapped in white fur with a lone feather that had been given to her years before by a Lakota woman who had said she reminded her of the White Buffalo Woman of her people whenever she played.

I had watched parts of her fade and turn off one by one, slowly, painfully, but also beautifully, with all the magic she knew. Now the last of her earthly being came down to a single green line blinking on the monitor. When even that grew too faint for the machine to read, I took a rose from the many flowers surrounding her and laid it on her chest. The tiny vibrations of its petals became the last physical flutters of life I could see in her body. But the thing that never left her, even into those final flutters, was the invisible strength of that magic. The same thing her music had always felt like.

We began to chant softly beside her.

Om. Om. Om.

I swear the light in the room became thicker. The veil between the realms collapsed. She took one last long breath and exhaled the wind from her body. She passed through.

Then there was silence.

The words of her last song looped in my head, "together we'll dance across the universe."

I would not let anyone take her body.

Death is not a single moment. It is a slow unfolding, the separation of the mind and body finalizing long after the heart stops.

So we stayed with her. For more than a day I kept vigil with her amongst her flowers, and let the invisible continue to play out around us.

In the dark hours before the next dawn, Santiago and I put on a recording of hers, *Cosmic Butterfly*. We played next to her earthly vessel, in the opening tuning we had found in the desert years before. I danced with her one last time. At 2:22 AM the song ended.

The oldest ritual of all had been completed, to be present with someone through the threshold of their existence and whatever it is that comes before or after it.

---

Home. I took a long shower to wash the death from my body. I had been in the deepest meditation for what felt like weeks, and I was still only emerging from it as I stepped out.

Millie and Charlie arrived an hour later into my care. I held them as deeply as I ever had, and we sat down at the entryway, next to my mother's piano, and cried together in one embrace. A part of me was relieved, for her pain was over. But even after all I had processed in the long lead-up, the finality was profound, a raw feeling of loss.

At one point near the end of that long year and a half, Millie had told me she was so sad Jamie was dying, but that they were still alive and that they would need me too. She was right.

I hung the drum on the wall.

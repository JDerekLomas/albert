CHAPTER 7
The Breaking

I returned to San Diego to enter a next chapter of my life. But the sound of the shaman's drum seemed to follow me home, felt in the rhythms of the highs and lows. Each strike of the hammer seemed to come faster by the day, pulling me toward a crescendo as it had on the mountain.

---
[EDIT]
The call had come from Ulaanbaatar soon after the last expedition ended, while Charlie was still pulling himself up on the coffee table. The voice on the line was lower than usual. "They want you to hold off publishing the details, you're going to have to embargo most of the finds."

The words stung. 

It wasn't clear what had changed, but local tensions with China were rising in the wake of mining exploitations, and my Chinese surname, Lin, had become a factor. Maybe they didn't want me to be the one that found it?  Or maybe it was because the tomb and what it represented was better served LOST.

I sat out on the porch a long while. 

I thought of my whole team, Shay, Nate, Fred, Ishdorj and Bira and everyone that had been on that journey with me.  All that work was now being swept under the rug.  

But I could see the frustration was mostly my ego reacting. The mountain was bigger than us and it didn't owe us anything.

I could still feel the shaman's hand on the back of my skull. He had only wanted to know our intentions.

I watched Charlie run after a sunbathing lizard, as the memory of the night I had been judged swirled through my mind.

What had my intentions really been? 

I had come for the story that was missing in the history books. I thought that finding the tomb would revive that story for humanity and more personally I had hoped it would reveal something in me. 

What had I found? Looking into the eyes of the nomads, I saw resilience and connection, and through that could know the story of a person who had been broken to the point of nothing, then showed up to the mountain in a way that let him change his world.

At times, it had almost seemed as if the mountain wanted to say something, the fallen tree roots revealing their hidden story like windows blown open by the storm.

But beyond the tiles and artifacts, we had been transported through time. I had stood next to Amaraa who reached for the *hiimori* on top of the Burkhan Khaldun, next to the soul banner that blew in the wind. What I was searching for was there. Not in a tomb.  And it wasn't lost.  It was thriving in the spirit of the Mongols regardless of some archaeological project.

That last day the shaman had said to "remind the world of the Great Khan and the power of the mountain," but to "respect the old ways." Maybe that meant realizing the invisible had power. Sometimes what was lost was best remembered in feeling, not found in hand. It had taken me a long time to understand this.

So I compiled three years of geophysics, scans, radiocarbon dating, and findings into a confidential report, had it translated into Mongolian, and sent it to Ishdorj and Bira to be submitted to the ministries. It was for the guardians to decide what came next.

The quest for the tomb of Genghis Khan was over.  And I was sad.

But four years later Mongolia had the mountain and the sacred landscape around it inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site. Not for a tomb, but for what the mountain had meant to Genghis Khan, as described in the Secret History of the Mongols, the one account his enemies had not written. Which mountain was the true Burkhan Khaldun had been argued over for generations, and now it had been named. I will never know whether our confidential report ever reached the people who made that case. But regardless, the intentions of our quest were being realized.

I stood up from the porch and walked over to my son, who was digging up worms with a small shovel. I took a deep breath and chose gratitude for the drumbeat as it passed through me.  

In the wake of this closing, I tried to really think about what had been the meaning of the whole quest in the first place, really.  

It had not started with a tomb search. It had not even started with a blue birthmark. Before I had even been told of the blue birthmark that had once been on my back, I had already felt the question Why do I feel the way I do? As a human. Since as long as I could remember, I was in search for what made me feel and think and be? What was beneath everything. 

This was why I had been searching for what was lost. This was the true quest.

---

For a while, everything had been idyllic.

The day before Charlie was born, we had moved onto a quiet San Diego street. The oldest nursery in the city sat on one side of us, free-range chickens wandering through its aisles, and on the other the oldest cemetery turned into a park where the city's ghost tour would stop each night. A fitting twist of fate for a tomb hunter turned stroller pusher. A hammock was strung across the full width of our tiny front yard.

Our neighbors, Rich and Katrina, were ten years older than us and became the family we didn't have in the city. Rich had the no bullshit cowboy wisdom of someone who grew up on his own. Underneath it he was the kind of friend you always want in your corner. I would need that. Katrina was a teacher whose patience and thoughtfulness were as large as her heart. Their daughter Pearl was a kind-hearted ten-year-old nature lover who adopted the role of big sister to the kids.

When Charlie was new I would sit with him asleep on my chest for hours, his heartbeat against my skin. He had a gentle thoughtfulness in his eyes.

The beats were picking up now, happy ones.

Millie was born under a Wolf Moon in January, two years after Charlie. I'd woken at 3 AM to find Kara reorganizing the entire pantry in a frenzy of instinctual nesting, and by morning she was in labor. When I held my daughter for the first time, her whole palm closed around my finger and my heart melted into a puddle on the floor. Again, the clichés were surprisingly true, a father-daughter bond is primal.

She had the same blue birthmark at the base of her spine that Charlie had been born with. Her tiny mouth yawned, then she looked straight up at me with the whole cosmos in her eyes. Charlie came into the room and we handed him a toy fire truck, telling him she had brought it for him from wherever she had been. They were best friends from the start.

My girl was fierce, lived in an Elsa dress until it hung off her in tatters, and made sure her voice was heard. But under the fierceness ran the same kindness as her brother. He was calm and curious, she was caring and willful. Together they were everything.

We made home movies in the canyon, Charlie the wizard, Millie commanding the set with a single cry. A wooden sail skiff the size of a bathtub sat in the yard, *Bouncing Betty* painted across the bow. Kara sewed a sail I cut from canvas and our tiny boat was rigged with an oversized pirate flag. We spent days on the bay, the kids wedged between my knees and the boom an inch above their heads.

On summer nights I hung a bedsheet over the garage and set lawn chairs in the driveway and we watched Indiana Jones or Back to the Future under the stars. The neighbors would drift over with wine in plastic cups, a long-distance runner, a researcher at the university, a yoga instructor. When the movie was over we would race matchbox cars down the driveway. Some afternoons I would bring home a carton of ladybugs from the nursery and set them loose, and the kids would go still, watching them contentedly.

At night I'd read them novels of nautical adventure, filling my imagination but putting them to sleep. I had always loved the stories of a team locked on a ship sailing into whatever situation was on the horizon. Millie always fell asleep first. Charlie would do his best to hold on for one more chapter. When the book closed I'd linger in the room, scared to step on a Lego piece, surrounded by toys sprawled across the floor like booby traps.

Then back to Kara, to swab the decks and do our best to clean the never-ending messes.

This is how it went for a brief, wonderful time. Locked in so much joy with the kids that I didn't see how far Kara had already gone.

---

Professionally, life had become a whirlwind.

There were invitations to secret societies. Debriefings at the Pentagon and with the head of the National Geospatial Intelligence Agency about tools we had built to find a grave. Even without publishing the full results, we had gained notoriety.

And from the crowdsourcing platform, Shay, Nate, Luke, and I started a company we called Tomnod, after the Mongolian word for big eye. We approached building it just like we had the search for the tomb, bootstrapped form nothing and singing silly songs. On the windows of my tiny apartment we would write algorithms and pitch decks, then show up to high-powered meetings with hand-me-down suits that covered the Superman underwear that I would wear for luck.

We turned the community of "Tomnoders" that had searched through imagery of Mongolia toward mapping disaster sites, humanitarian crises, or even counting solar panels on roofs. When Malaysia Airlines lost a plane, eight million volunteers swept a million square kilometers of ocean. It felt like we had built a fire hose and gone looking for a fire.

But everything in this world was about bottom lines and adding value, not the quest I had spent my life pursuing.

What I really wanted was just to be back on a search for the truth of what lay deeper in my own humanity.

Then, a deep strike of the hammer. Gil, the poet climber, vanished in the Peruvian Andes. We turned Tomnod to search for him from space while rescue teams worked the slopes below. A line of tags traced a set of tracks across the snow, and the teams followed them to his body.

He had once written, "Perhaps mountain climbing has become a way for me to recover all the beautiful things I have lost. But all I ever find are more mountains to climb."

After that I stopped waiting.

I convinced National Geographic to give me some funding to build a small program at the university that I called Engineers for Exploration.

Around the same time I got a mysterious phone call from a man who introduced himself as Jason Paterniti from the equally mysterious Explorers Club. "I read about your work and wanted to see about combining it with a guy who's got similar ambitions in the Maya archaeology world." And that's how I was pulled into the Guatemalan jungle to map Maya ruins for the first time.

A few months later he was introducing me to Tom Garrison next to a fully built out truck loaded up for the Petén. The flight in had come with some suspicious tasting food, and I had arrived with a handful of literally green in the face engineering students who had never left the lab. But Tom was an archaeologist whose ambitions would change how we understood the Maya, and he embraced us.

"Everything in the jungle is trying to kill you," he explained casually when we first arrived. It was the start of a friendship that would span many years.

The Maya were a universe away from the steppe. But after Mongolia, I had felt the need to search for something deeper that could connect me to all cultures. They were parts of the human condition that were within my own potential.

Years earlier, when I was just starting the Valley of the Khans Project, Tom had been a PhD student and published a paper with his mentor Stephen Houston, "Evaluating the use of IKONOS satellite imagery in lowland Maya settlement archaeology." A dry title to interrogate a wild idea, that you could find a buried city by the signature of chlorophyll in the leaves above it, because the limestone underneath had leached into the roots. That paper had inspired me to turn toward the skies, and I had never forgotten it.

My group of undergraduate engineering students began experimenting with giant balloons and makeshift drones to carry the sensors that promised to see past the trees. There were many crashes and a lot of broken gear, but it was also incredible to crawl through the excavation tunnels and map the stories hidden underneath the layers of construction with the nascent technology. The students would arrive to the visceral shock of the jungle, and leave each expedition either more rugged or completely broken. Jason laughed at it all, knowing he had catalyzed a good convergence.

"National Geographic was co-founded by an engineer," I would tell them. "Alexander Graham Bell, the inventor of the telephone!"

My explorer's life was reforming.

What I didn't see while all this was happening was that Kara was steadily growing more distant. Something in her was changing. The fights grew more frequent. The silences between us grew heavier. Something beautiful, buckling under a million tiny fractures.

We were driving home from a weekend with the last friends who still felt like ours and not his or hers, the kids asleep in the back, when the silence in the car told me it was already over. That night I carried Charlie to his bed, then Millie, and stood a moment watching them sleep. Then I went into the kitchen, where Kara was waiting, and it broke open. In the middle of arguing about nothing she said the thing that rearranged the previous five years. She had been living a lie. She had known for a long time that she didn't love me anymore, and with that she had realized she was a lesbian, and she could not carry it any longer.

Boom, this drum hit was harder than the rest.

The person I'd known seemed to vanish in an instant. I don't say that to diminish her courage, coming out must have taken a lot of it. And I can only guess at the loneliness of holding it through a marriage and two children. But in that kitchen I couldn't yet see her bravery, I could only see betrayal. And under that, a grief for the family I thought we were building, gone in a sentence.

I was seventeen again, in a hallway, listening through a door to my own parents' marriage coming apart. My children were three and five. I had failed everyone.

---

I moved into the construction site of what had been our dream home, just as the longer nights of fall approached.

We had broken ground on it with the money from the sale of Tomnod to the company that had given us satellite imagery in Mongolia. The sale had happened the day after Millie's birth, the final call taking place in an empty delivery room. I left much of the buyout on the table so I could keep exploring instead of taking the golden handcuffs.

My sister Jane, an architect, drew the plans, and we began. But when Kara told me it was over, it was still just framing and sawdust, with one room upstairs closed in with plywood, a toilet, a sink. There was no front door, so I came and went through a sheet of plywood that I'd screw shut behind me.

Standing in the wreckage of what had been our future that first night, I put Johnny Cash on my radio and cried myself to sleep next to a crowbar where a partner had once been. With few real walls it was cold and drafty at first.

A friend left an old electric guitar, and I played it alone in that raw room until the backs of my fingers bled over the strings. Rich, from across the street, put a surfboard in my hands and said, let's surf it out.

But then I began to pour the grief into the house, building it like I was building myself. I needed to get it to a place that I could begin co-parenting, and fast. I asked the contractors to teach me, and set outlet boxes in the mornings before the world woke. "Rewiring my world," I thought.

When the shower was finally installed the kids started staying with me every other week. We cooked on a camp stove in the one finished room and slept, the three of us, in one bed.

I thought living in a construction site would be hard, but the house became a playground, their joy seeping into the walls as they came up. Downstairs, a single green carpet on unfinished cement floors became the permanent island for a table with art supplies and two mushroom-shaped stools that Charlie and Millie would sit on.  That carpet, it really tied the room together.

Anything the kids dreamed up, we had the supplies and tools to build it, in piles anywhere you looked. Race tracks for toy cars, furniture ideas like bedroom tree houses. For dinners I would cook the one chicken meal I knew how to make and we would have competitions to see who could finish enough so as to be rewarded with "unlimited dessert."

But beneath the joys of fatherhood, most nights when they slept, I grieved the loss of partnership, the loneliness of the fact that there was no partner to share these memories with.

One evening, with tears swelling for no clear reason, I told my kids they were my best friends.

Charlie looked at me with steady eyes. "More than friends, Dad. Family."

They were taking care of me now. That was the part that broke me the most. I had been that child once. They had not asked for any of it.

The long nights when they were at their mother's were the hardest to bear. I sought comfort in community as a small group of friends formed through jam sessions that I would throw late into the night. Jamie, Leo, and a few others. The music filled the void that had been left and carried the heartbreak through me like the water of a river.

Slowly, the house rose.

---

In another life I had loved Maya in Dali. What would have happened on that path? She was in Brazil now, a mother herself, and we had not spoken in all that time. I wrote to her. She wrote back. That winter we would meet in the mountains where we had found each other, and I would take Charlie with me.

Millie was only three and not ready for travel like that, so Kara kindly offered to stay with her while Charlie and I flew. I think she wanted me to be happy.

Somewhere over the Pacific our bags vanished, and we arrived with nothing but the clothes we wore. Charlie watched me worry and said, "It's okay, Dad, we will find new clothes." He was right. Our clothes came from village stands. *Going full immersion,* I thought as I caught a reflection of us in the threads of the local rice farmers.

Maya appeared at the bus station with her son Govinda holding her hand, and eight years fell away with only a few awkward laughs. "Yu-Min, it's good to see you," she said. Charlie was a year older than Govinda. The boys took to each other before they had a word in common.

Charlie had never heard me speak Mandarin. For five years his father had been a man with one language, and now I was ordering food and laughing with Maya in a tongue he had no way into. He watched me the way you watch a stranger who has your face. That night in the guesthouse he asked me to say something else in it. Then something else. He was collecting it.

We rode into the hills on a three-wheeled motorbike, Maya and Govinda in the cart behind. In Dali the cobblestones and the curved rooftops were where I had left them, but tour buses had found the village and swallowed it whole. The friends we had known, and our former selves among them, were echoes in those streets now. We drove further toward the mountain-lined lake.

On the way we laid a picnic under a tree on an irrigation road beside the emerald green rice fields, and let ourselves sit for an afternoon inside the life that might have happened. Farmers walked past like we could have been their neighbors. We loved each other in that parallel universe, but in the grass in front of us were our two boys, proof that it had not been our path, and the reason it did not need to be. Soon we would both have to return to our lives.

We made our way to the lake, and watched them play along its shores. The mountains were still there. Years before, she had put a book in my hands. *Siddhartha*. A man who leaves everything to search the world for something deeper than the trappings of our human illusions. I hadn't understood it when she gave it to me. I had carried it ever since.

"See you in another eight years, Yu-Min," she said, with a loving smile that communicated everything we did not say.

---

The wounds of divorce were still fresh. That spring I loaded our lives into a rented RV and pointed it north. Camp chairs and microscopes, princess dresses and rock hammers, diapers and mac and cheese, even a ceramic chiminea found their way into that rickety home on wheels. I was still figuring out how to see life as a single father, with two small children, five and barely three. I had been so focused on getting everything packed that when I got in the cab and turned the key, I had no plan, no destination, just an open road.

The passenger seat was empty. It wasn't supposed to be this way.

The city thinned and the desert opened the way it does entering the steppe, and for a mile or two I could breathe again. Then the questions came rumbling up through the floor of the RV, lodging themselves in my throat. Could I do this alone? I had to try.

My hand gripped the wheel. The rolling hills of Southern California were full of color, flowers brought on by the heavy rains that winter.

The muffled laughter of my kids carried forward through the RV, over the hum of the engine and the song on the radio. Bob Dylan's melancholic tones pulled tears out of my eyes for thoughts and feelings I couldn't quite identify.

I had always believed that I would be able to rewrite what had happened to my parents through my own success at a marriage, that camping trips into the wilderness with my kids would be a chance to reclaim what had been lost when my parents split. But I guess life had other plans.

Somewhere near Ridgecrest. The long silhouette of the Eastern Sierras rose slowly on the horizon, the road ahead a single long line traversing the otherwise barren landscape. I had made this drive so many times through the different phases of my life. The mountains never changed, but I did.

I looked over my shoulder and saw Millie and Charlie sleeping in the RV's bed. Even though we were just twenty feet apart, if they needed anything they would have to do it themselves. I was stuck at the wheel.

I still didn't really know where we were going to end up, but as the horizon drew closer I could feel the 395 pulling me. On one side were the peaks that reached for the sky, on the other the valleys of the deep desert.

I pulled off at Diaz Lake and killed the engine. The kids slept in the back. Everything that year, the marriage ending, the house rising, the remaking of myself, had come down to one question, and I had driven out here to ask it. Who was I going to be now, for the two of them and me?

Mount Whitney stood in the moonlight, the same granite I once climbed with Gil. I looked up at the stars a long time.

In the morning we fished trout for breakfast and turned east into Death Valley, where dust devils spun across the flats and we put on ski goggles to stand in the wind. Through desert scarves we laughed into the howl of it, the warm air sparkling with dust particles zipping by.

That night at Mesquite another storm came up through the valley and rocked the RV on its springs the way a parent rocks a child, and the kids shrieked with joy at every gust as we played with a bucket of matchbox cars in the bunk. When it passed the stars came out, and with no music downloaded on my phone I opened a stargazing app whose ambient celestial hum became our soundtrack, as we lay under a blanket of lights. We were on a cosmic journey.

On the last day we reached a giant sea of dunes and walked toward the tallest, Millie on my shoulders, miles from where we parked. These were not mountains of stone. They were mountains of sand, their beauty made by the same wind that was forever tearing things down. I had been tired since the first night I slept alone in that plywood room, and this was the first tiredness that did not hurt.

We climbed to the top and lay on our backs with our hands and feet in the air, pretending we were astronauts flying the earth through the universe like a ship. "Turn on the hyperdrive," I said, pushing imaginary buttons, and Charlie called it back, "Hyperdrive on, check!" Millie pointed out the stars she knew. Orion. The North Star. At three she could already navigate through deep space. Then we slid down the face of the dune headfirst, swimming in the lingering warmth of the day trapped in the sand, laughing the whole way.

When the trip had started I had driven, unsure I was going to be able to pull it off alone. What I hadn't realized was that I had never really been alone. We were a crew facing the unknown together.

When I dropped them at their mother's, Millie's hair was a nest of dreadlocks and Charlie was covered in scrapes and bites. Kara gave me a look and asked me to learn to comb the tangles out. Other than that she was glad for us.

We were going to be okay.

---

The final drumbeat came as fire, though it arrived looking like nothing at all.

National Geographic wanted to test me as a television storyteller and sent out a media coach to teach me the tricks of the trade. They suggested it might be an easier way of getting new expeditions going rather than sleeping out of my car.

The first would be a new documentary about the mysterious tomb of the First Emperor of China and his terra-cotta army. The plan was to use satellites and drones with thermal cameras on them to see if we could find traces of ancient roads that would lead us to other undiscovered parts of the sprawling burial complex. I was excited and nervous, not knowing these were the seeds of what would become my own series, Lost Cities. More about that later.

For a few days I learned the facts and details. The coach had me say them out loud, over and over, until they stopped sounding like facts.

A tomb built by a man who wanted to cheat death. A version of his whole world, buried, so he could take it with him. An army of clay soldiers in ranks, every face made different.

The irony was that his fear of dying is probably what killed him. The legend said he swallowed mercury, believing it was the elixir of youth.

A century after the tomb was sealed a historian named Sima Qian wrote down what was supposed to be inside. Crossbows on hair triggers at the entrance. Constellations set into the ceiling, the shape of the land laid out beneath them. A hundred rivers of mercury, the Yangtze and the Yellow and the great sea, made to flow by machine.

That was the one I kept coming back to. He had taken the thing that killed him and built an ocean of it around his own body.

Two thousand years later the ground above the mound still tested high for it. It had never been opened.

The coach walked me into a coffee shop one afternoon as a final test, having already scouted ahead without me knowing.

"A good presenter," he said, "is someone who can connect with people, even through the bullet points of a story. Someone a person would want to grab a drink with. Now go deliver the lines we just rehearsed to that woman behind the counter."

Deep breath, I ordered. She asked what I was preparing for, pointing at my stack of research papers, and I told her about a two-thousand-year-old unopened tomb and the quest to make sense of it, trying to own every word. She handed me my coffee. A Brazilian woman with feathers in her hair and a smile that pulled the ground out from under me.

That was how I met Bruna.

Things didn't happen instantly. I filmed my first commissioned National Geographic documentary in China while she wandered India, and every so often a message would arrive, just to say hello and describe the magic of the cities she was traversing.

The filming went well. I had crawled into the pits of excavations where I held the skull of an ancient prince, and walked the ranks of clay soldiers where some of the faces looked like mine. Then I returned to San Diego to pick up the kids from Kara and resume single adventure dad life.

That summer Derek had an extra ticket to Burning Man, seventy thousand people building a city in the dust for a single week only to burn it down. Bruna sent a message saying she would be there, dropping straight in from having seen the pilgrimage to Varanasi, an ancient city also built for burning.

Next thing I knew I was on my bike amongst the wonders of the playa, experiencing the magic of human beings revealed as creativity and generosity, the kind that everyday life can make you forget. It was hot and dusty and absurd, and I loved it.

Derek and I were leaving early, both due back to our kids, when I heard my name across a sea of RVs and turned and there she was, running toward me through the dust. We held on and laughed and wept for no reason. We did not even kiss, but in that instant there was an electricity between us that felt like fate. Of all the places for our love to truly start, it was in the city built specifically to be burned to the ground.

A few days later she came to San Diego, and everything clicked. Charlie and Millie loved her instantly. She had a heart that could not be held in one body, the kind of person who would run into a fire to pull someone out, whose laugh could turn any afternoon into a party. She was the most alive person I had ever met, and being near her was like standing close to a flame. She swooped into the whole complicated mess of my life without flinching.

For the first time since the dissolution of my marriage, the pieces seemed to be there to build myself back.

I believed it would hold.

Then on a sunny late summer day, Millie caught her first fish. A few hours later, the last fall of the shaman's hammer, a vehicle flipped and I was crushed beneath its frame. The trance had begun, and the sound of everything had arrived at my shores.

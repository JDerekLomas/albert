CHAPTER 8
Limb Salvage

Darkness. Everything still.

I woke to beeping. A heart rate monitor somewhere to my left. Fluorescent lights. The smell of disinfectant. Voices in the hallway, muffled. Someone's chair scraping the floor. Pain.

I lay in a bed of confusion before I tried to move. When I looked down, my right leg was impaled with metal fixators and wrapped in bloody bandages. The accident rushed back into focus. Where were Charlie and Millie?

Bruna was in the chair by my bedside. It didn't seem like she had ever left it. How long had it been? Days?

The doctor walked in.

"Will I ever be able to surf again? Or play with my kids?"

His response: "We're working on limb salvage." The word lingered. Not rescue. Not recovery. Salvage.

I was the thing being salvaged.

"We're doing what we can to save your leg. But this is the X-ray of what you came in with..." The doctor showed me images of something that looked more like a pile of splintered wood than a body part. Then he showed me what they had built to fabricate something that resembled a leg, more metal than bone.

A burning ache reached through the shield of pain medications. More injections, then more sleep.

Embarrassment took over when I woke next. My feet stuck out from under the sheet. Years of climbing and expeditions had done a number on them, but at least they were mine.

"If it doesn't work, at least you could pay half-price for a pedicure," Bruna said with a playful smile.

I had never had a pedicure.

Kara brought Charlie and Millie later that day. Whatever the accident had taken from me, it had not taken them.

Charlie walked over to me cautiously, worried at first that he would hurt me. Millie grabbed at whatever she could reach and began wrapping my face in bandages until I looked like a mummy. Then Charlie joined in. Their laughter cut through the pain.

I made jokes. I performed feats of strength for them with my arms. Underneath it though was the fear, that the version of me they had needed had been crushed under the roll cage along with everything else. What kind of father could I be to them now?

When they hugged me goodnight, they did it carefully. I had been invincible to them days before. Now I was a wreck. The tears flowed in the wake of their visit.

---

More blood draws. Infection was the thing to worry about now. The word "amputation" began floating about as an option to consider.

Others started arriving. Friends, in waves, which Bruna coordinated with surprising efficiency, as though she had been in my life for years instead of a few weeks. Someone strung lights from the bed rail to the chair. Derek smuggled in some contraband that the doctors immediately confiscated. There were prayer flags draped over my bed, and the smell of Bruna's palo santo battled the chemical odor of disinfectant. More instruments arrived. By the time anyone walked in, the room looked less like a recovery ward and more like a festival camp, the reverberation of Burning Man from only a month before seeping its way into the hospital.

Then my mother from one direction, and my father from another. They stood near the foot of the bed without quite knowing what to do with their hands. They had been divorced almost twenty years and had never healed, but here they were now, forced into the same room by my injury.

My mother moved to my side quickly and began organizing the cards and flowers piling on the side table. She had raised me, now here I was wrapped in bloody bandages.

My father stood on the other side. He looked down at the leg, the metal pinning it together, then at my face. We had not talked for a decade after he left my mother, but he had always loved me deeply. I had not seen that look in him before, the helplessness of a parent whose child has been badly hurt.

But the visits felt good and surreal, a procession of the people in my life. We would make up songs and visualize the positive.

What kept happening though, when no one was looking, was different. I'd lie there staring at the ceiling tiles and wonder how I was going to provide for my kids. Then the overwhelming pain of the injury would wash over me and take any conscious thought with it.

When visiting hours were over, the room lights would dim, and then it was just Bruna, me, and the steady rotation of nurses tending to the pain medicines and antibiotics.

One night Bruna carefully navigated the IV tubes and climbed into the bed with me. Within seconds every monitor in the room started going off. Someone came running. The nurse took in the two of us, then the monitors, and left without a word. In destruction our love blossomed, and that was a medicine in and of itself.

---

More surgeries. The limb salvage was losing ground.

They would put me under and work on me like mechanics on a car, and when I woke I could read the result on their faces before anyone spoke. Afterward they slid me into a hyperbaric chamber shaped like a submarine, pressurized oxygen forced into the blood to speed the healing. I sat in there for hours with my novels, pretending it was just one big adventure at sea.

Then came a long night. They had scrubbed the wound extra hard, the fear of infection everywhere, and the pain found a new register. Pain management was about getting in front of the wave. Bruna set her alarm every hour to click the dispenser before the dose ran out. Click. Sleep. Alarm. Click. Somewhere in the night the wave got ahead of us. I was writhing, gripping the rails, my body clenched against a pain so total it erased thought.

I resisted. That made it worse.

Desperate, at the point of breaking, I had nothing left to fight with. So I stopped trying to push it away, and instead stared directly at the pain. A sliver of relief. Then I went further, meeting it, showing it something like love, literally speaking to it. The pain didn't leave. It changed. Still enormous, still consuming, but no longer crushing me. Resistance to what was happening was its own suffering. I lay there breathing, tears on my face, the sensations moving through me instead of against me. Bruna's hand on mine. The beep of the monitors. The fluorescent hum.

---

The fork came at three weeks. The surgeon laid out two options. Months more in the hospital, more surgeries, a muscle transplant from my back to fill what the leg had lost, and no guarantee at the end of it. Or amputation. The choice was mine alone.

Mark Meyers had come by and mused that if I amputated, the engineer in me could take over. But I had never seen an amputee explorer. The only images I had were from old Civil War documentaries. And what they were asking me to decide could not be undone.

Then Brent walked in.

He had been with us at the bay the day of the accident. While everyone else was focused on saving the leg, Brent had been quietly looking beyond it. He'd found a prosthetics clinic in San Diego, run by a man named Peter Harsch, that looked like a gym for Paralympians.

"Check this out," he said, and brought up a photo on his phone.

A one-legged surfer named Mike Coots, deep in a barreling wave. Bright yellow prosthetic slicing the water. His face lit with pure joy. The lip of the wave wrapped entirely around him. Coots had lost his leg to a tiger shark at seventeen, had never stopped surfing, and had become a vocal shark conservationist. My roll cage accident on a friend's parents' ranch felt pretty lame compared to that.

I held the picture and stared at it. "Will I ever surf again?" That's what I had asked the doctor the night of the accident.

Everything I had feared I was going to lose, fishing with my kids, carrying them on my shoulders, teaching them to ski, even my life as an explorer. There was an answer in that photo.

I gathered my friends and family, the people who had been carrying me through three weeks of limbo.

"I can't cling to the past. It's gone. I need to do this."

The room was quiet. Adam, who had been driving during the accident, was destroyed. He hadn't been able to look at my leg yet. The guilt was in his face every time he came in.

"Giving the injury resentment or guilt would only be paying it more than it's owed," I explained. It had been an accident, and that was how I really felt.

The doctor brought me a consent form. One signature to accept extraordinary irreversible consequences. I held it for a beat.

My Valley of the Khans teammates used to say it best. "When faced with two options, choose the more adventurous one!" Then I hugged my kids, and penned my name to it.

I reached for the guitar. My fingers found something loose and bluesy, making it up as I went. The Goodbye Leg Blues. Someone laughed, then caught themselves, then laughed again when they saw I was laughing too. Bruna and Tim were both filming with one hand and crying with the other. Derek leaned against the doorframe shaking his head with that look he got when I did something he thought was both insane and exactly right. My mother wiped her eyes and tried to sing along. Charlie and Millie watched.

Then my infectious disease doctor Eli, an old friend from the university who had been carefully watching over my charts, rushed in and the music stopped.

The labs had come back. The bone infection that had been lurking was spreading fast. There was no time to wait, the time to operate was now.

---

I lay on my back as fluorescent lights slid past overhead, one after another.

Charlie was in the corridor, holding Millie's hand.

My mother's face. My father's, Adam's. Bruna's, smiling down at me. Then I was in.

The operating room was bright and cold. The surgical team moved around me in masks. The anesthesiologist prepared a cocktail Derek had helped coordinate with my pain doctors, ketamine in the mix, a dissociative that in theory could reduce the risk of phantom limb pain by disrupting the brain's map of the body before the body was altered. It might help. Nobody knew for certain.

Charlie's look. Millie's wave. I tried to keep all of it as the drugs took hold and the room began to disssssoolve...

---

I was flying amongst pyramids, and then I *was* the pyramids. Something else entirely. I was in space that felt outside of the universe I had existed in my whole life. Like I had been pulled out of a filing cabinet of infinite realities and was looking at the cabinet itself. Would I get put back into the one I came from? What about Charlie and Millie? I needed to make sure I got back to them.

Geometric forms built and collapsed and rebuilt around me. A cascade of shapes that seemed to flow like a waterfall and then turned into an avalanche.

I was afraid. The chaos was going to grind me to dust and scatter the particles into the void.

Nothing to fight. Nothing to outrun.

So I stopped resisting, as I had with the pain.

Then, as it was about to crash onto me and swallow me whole, the chaos smoothed out and became a feeling of harmony, a calm that morphed out of the shapes. What had been terror seconds before became warmth, and a reassurance I had no words for.

I was inside the physics my father had described to me as a child. Matter and antimatter annihilating each other entirely, except for the slimmest of margins that created everything. I was feeling the margin.

When it was over I surfaced once, briefly, as the nurse wheeled me out of the operating room. Through glazed eyes I saw her face above me. I heard my own voice say "It's just love, and it wins."

Then my eyes closed again. I was retreating into the harmony I had seen.

With scalpel and saw, the surgeons had removed my leg below the knee. The tibia and fibula I had run with as a kid, climbed trees on, urged horses across the Mongolian steppe with. The leg that had carried Charlie on my shoulders and spun Millie in the living room of a house I'd built with my own hands.

All of it, separated from me, taken away, and burned to ash.

CHAPTER 11
The Sound of the River

The man on the crutches left an imprint in my mind. I learned that fewer than five percent of the millions of amputees in the world have access to a well-fitting prosthetic. So I turned the Engineers for Exploration students toward it, aiming the same photogrammetry we'd used to map ruins at human limbs instead, scanning with low-cost camera phones and printing low-cost sockets. We called it Project Limbitless.

But it was the challenges of the heart that became harder to solve.

I was no longer the patient, and she was no longer the nurse. Bruna and I had fallen in love inside a catastrophe, and outside of one there was not much holding us up. With the successes in the jungle, new expeditions began appearing in other parts of the world. I was gone half of every month, dropping into fatherhood between flights, and each time I came home the distance had grown and there was no time to mend it before the next one.

We went back to Burning Man that summer wanting the thing we'd found there the first time, two strangers colliding in the dust. But you can't summon that, and a year of small fractures had come with us. We looked at each other in the smoldering ashes of a city of art, imagined and then destroyed in the symbolism of impermanence, and knew that our love had played out like its mirror. "Aries," she said, "we are both born of fire." Tears ran down her face as she said it, and we cried together knowing what she meant. A week later she was gone, and I was bewildered and heartbroken. She had carried me the whole way through my burning. Whatever else came, I would always be grateful for that.

I went searching for answers. Was our meeting a fatalistic life raft sent by some orchestration of my existence? The synchronicity of her arriving into my life with that symbolic primordial sound at the single hour of destruction in blood and dirt as we waited for the ambulance, chanting. I began to frame it in my mind like a force, attempting to remove my human emotionality from the agony of a breakup. Where had it come from?

Om.

What was it? Had it found me from across the world, a frequency?

She had just returned from her travels alone in India weeks before we had first found each other on the Playa. Amongst her travels she described a city that sounded almost mythical, an ancient place of ritual on the banks of the Ganges, home to an eternal fire that never goes out, where Hindus burn their dead and shepherd them into whatever comes next. It has many names, she told me. Benares. Kashi. Varanasi.

That November, more than a year after the amputation and a few months after she had moved, I was in Asia for a talk I was to deliver. With some extra time I had I decided to buy a ticket to India. I wasn't going for work. National Geographic hadn't sent me. There was no expedition, no crew, no LiDAR map to follow. As I boarded the flight, I felt like I was chasing the invisible.

I walked down the center of the plane. I came to a woman in the back row. Her long black hair draped over limbs with attached prosthetics. Her backpack was covered in buttons with slogans like "Free Tibet" and "Vote, Fuckers." One said "I Love My Body."

The seat next to her was empty, so I took it. One prosthesis-wearer next to another. I'd been nervous about traveling in India with a disability, and sitting next to her, the nervousness lifted. She had been born in India with missing limbs, something I had read could carry a cultural stigma. But we talked the whole flight in, trading stories, two people who did not have to explain anything to each other.

A flight attendant interrupted us. The crew, he said, had been moved to bake us a chocolate cake in the little oven at the back of the plane. 

What a welcome to India. It felt like a reminder to enter the journey with an open heart, not fear or expectation.

I landed in Delhi, then took a short flight to Varanasi. The airport lies an hour outside the city, but even there it felt like I'd stepped onto the edge of another world. The sky hung in a hazy pink hue, and I wondered if it came from the cremation pyres whose fires have defined this place for centuries.

When I had called around for a room, everything was full. In Varanasi there seems to be a festival every day, but this time it was Diwali, the great celebration of light's triumph over darkness. The largest festival of the year, and the city was overflowing. I finally found a vacancy in a hole-in-the-wall hostel with a shared bathroom.

I handed my phone to the cab driver, hoping the hostel staff could direct him better than I could. I had no plan beyond that.

As we drew nearer to the city, a tuk-tuk slid in front of us. Hanging from its back window was a familiar shape: the same Sanskrit symbol that had adorned the medallion given to me by the Sherpa in Tibet all those years ago.

Om

The traffic grew denser as we neared the city, until it became a great, slow-moving river carrying every vehicle imaginable. Cars and tuk-tuks, motorcycles with three people on them, entire families on horseback. Donkeys wandered between the lanes, and cows held court wherever they lay. It grew tighter and tighter until people were pushing off each other's cars, making gaps to squeeze through. Chaos with a harmony beneath it.

A haze hung over the city. The wood of the pyres was sandalwood. It rose like incense, masking what they were burning for. The sun was setting. There were so many people that they blended together into a wide pastel sea. Deepening shadows cast by three-thousand-year-old temples cloaked buildings built right on top of each other in a grand pileup of mud brick and wood and stone. All those souls were headed in the same direction, the great bend in the Ganges. But I couldn't see it yet. Those ancient roads were built long before automobiles, and as night fell and neon lights came on, my driver stopped in the middle of an impassably crowded street, signaling this was as far as he could go.

"Where do I go from here?" I asked.

He didn't speak English, but he took the phone and called the hostel. He gestured for me to wait. Soon a man came running over. "Come with us," he said.

I grabbed my bag, thanked the driver, and stepped out of the cab into that raging river of movement, trying not to be swept away as I followed my new guide. He took me through tiny cobblestone alleyways that led to a crumbling guest house. It looked condemned. Electrical cords everywhere. Monkeys crawling about the windows, that made me think of Charlie and Millie.

I called them to tell them about it. "Hey kiddies, you would love these monkeys!" "Wow, dad! Thanks for calling, we're late for school!" Then I read a note from the hostel warning me to lock my windows when away to stop the mischievous monkeys from stealing things.

There were people yelling on corners outside. The shared bathroom had a bucket shower and no safety bars, and I spent ten minutes figuring out how to wedge myself between the wall and a pipe so I could wash without my prosthetic sliding out from under me on the wet tile. It was not elegant.

But there was a small room with a bed on the third floor, so I could put my bag down. I ate some curry for dinner and slept for a few hours.

At five in the morning I woke to my alarm. I walked out of the guest house and down streets still buzzing in the pre-dawn darkness. Before I could see the river, I could hear it. Not the water itself but the sound that rose from the ghats to meet the morning: temple bells, conch shells, the low murmur of prayer, and somewhere beneath it all, the notes of a raga played on a sitar, a melodic pattern designed for dawn. I'd come chasing a single syllable, and I'd arrived in a place built of sound.

And then I saw it.

The Ganges.

One of the great arteries of human civilization. The holiest river in India, and deeply polluted by the very rituals that venerate it. Pilgrims bathe in it. The dead burn beside it. Temples with huge staircases called ghats lead down to the riverbank. This entire city, one of the oldest continuously inhabited places in the world, had been built around it like an enormous stadium of worship.

There was no wind. The water flowed gently, an expanse of suspended glass reflecting the grayish glow of the city at night, shot through with the soft pinks of the coming dawn. The only ripples came from little rowboats chugging in all directions. People slowly emerged, milling about, brushing their teeth and washing their faces with water from the river.

I walked to the bank and found a man with a rowboat. With hand gestures and some money, I asked him to take me somewhere I could see the city as the sun rose. We pushed off. When I looked back, I saw smoke rising from a temple at the edge of a great staircase surrounded by enormous piles of wood.

"Burning ghat," the boatman said with a pointed finger. That is where the dead burned.

Even from there I could see human shapes atop the woodpiles, smoldering to ashes that flowed down into the river we floated on. An eerie thought emerged, *some of me had been cremated already*. I asked him to bring me back to shore.

I walked up the steps of the city that rose from the water. There was a man meditating, an ascetic who'd given up earthly possessions in pursuit of enlightenment. Long dusty dreadlocks, white marks on his head and arms, beads around his neck. He looked at my leg. His eyes widened for a moment, then he returned to his meditation. I sat down near him in silence.

I pulled out the one book I had brought, a worn copy of *Siddhartha*. Years ago, in a village in Yunnan, Maya had handed me this book as we said goodbye. At the end of it, Siddhartha stands on the bank of a river and finally understands the sound of all things. Now I was sitting on the bank of the Ganges, the book in my hands, not understanding. The wind picked up. Kites started appearing over the city, kids battling them against each other in the sky, and I thought of Charlie and Millie again. I could hear children playing somewhere above.

I decided to walk toward wherever the kites were flying from. I walked past chattering monkeys and a cow, then found a spot near the water and sat to read my book. A young man stopped me. He was tallish and slim, with the casual confidence of someone who was clearly the alpha of his crew, and a look that told me he might try to hustle me.

"Hey man, where are you from?"

Wary, I tried to brush him off at first.

"You want marijuana?" he asked, calmly.

"Not interested." *he could be trying to scam me* I assumed.

He looked down at my leg. His attitude shifted. He left me alone for a minute, then returned with some friends: young Indian men in their twenties. Outnumbered but they seemed friendly.

"Let me help you, my friend. What do you need?"

"I don't need anything. I'm just here to read my book."

I was still unsure of his intentions. He'd have to be a real asshole to con a one-legged wanderer in the holiest of cities of his culture.

I had promised myself an open heart on the way in. Now would I actually do it?

"Can you show me where we can get onto a roof to watch the kites? I'll buy us a drink."

He said something to his friends. Then he turned back to me.

"I am Baba Lutlo. I know where to take you."

He led me into the backstreets of the city, past markets full of people buying fruit and vegetables. We wound through alleyways, hidden staircases, and the backs of people's homes. Everywhere we went people seemed to know Baba Lutlo. He was like the Fonz of Varanasi. The cool, streetwise leader, constantly reciting catchphrases of his own invention: "24 hour Shiva Power!" and "It's the Luuuuutlo Waaaaaay!"

We came to a rooftop at the top of the city. Kids were flying kites. People were washing and hanging their laundry. One of Baba Lutlo's friends stood there waving a flag and whistling to a flock of pigeons that seemed to obey his commands. He threw something in the air and the flock descended to their little rooftop home. There were a few chairs and a table in the corner, we sat down. He poured some tea.

"Do you have beer?" I asked without thinking. In this Hindu city, of course there was no beer. Or at least there wasn't supposed to be. But Baba Lutlo had his ways, and a beer appeared. I poured it into two glasses and took a long cool sip in the hot evening air.

"What brought you to Varanasi?" he asked.

I told him everything. How I'd lost my leg, and how a beautiful woman had wrapped her hands around my head and started chanting Om. How I'd come to believe that sound was a kind of ancient technology, and traveled here to find where it came from.

As I spoke, Baba Lutlo transformed. The catchphrase-spouting, hash-selling character faded. When I was done, he sat for a moment, then said quietly, "Shiva takes many forms. The destroyer. The creator. Sometimes a man, sometimes a woman, sometimes both at once."

He described Shiva's five heads, each representing a different element or aspect of existence. To be alive, he said, was to cycle through them. The only way to cycle through them was to let each one die so the next could be born.  But maybe it also ment, I thought to myself, that there were many versions of me living within me.

I looked at him across the table. I'd known him only a few hours and he'd already shown me three versions of himself: the hash dealer, the streetwise leader, and now whatever this was, the man quietly handing me a beer.

On the operating table I had seen it as a filing cabinet of realities, and been terrified of not being put back in the right drawer. Maybe all those realities were happing at once.

The crash had drawn a version out of me. The man whose broken body had needed Bruna hands so completely. He was gone. I would have to find out who was left. Before Bruna, I had been others. The son who had sat under the piano bench. The boy who had loved Maya in a Yunnan village in a language he was still learning. The husband to a marriage that didn't know how to hold itself. The father, now always a part of me.

He told me he would take me to Shiva's temple later, hidden beneath the great burning ghat.

"Come to the burning ghat tonight," he said to me. "Ask for me."

Before we parted. "What does Baba Lutlo mean?" I asked.

"That's my name. Baba means guru. Lutlo means thief." He flashed a big, toothy smile. "Because I steal hearts!" he yelled back as he dashed out.

Guru Heart Thief. It suited him.

I walked back to the guest house to change. The sun was high by then and a haze had settled over the city. I saw a line of men getting their heads shaved. Some guys were building a huge boat in a makeshift boatyard. A group of women were making ball-shaped food and handing it out to everyone who passed. A crowd of kids played with a plastic bottle. I paid for a cup of chai and rested at the hostel.

I checked my phone and saw a text from Bruna. "I'm sorry this has happened to us... how's India?" I read it twice. Then somewhere behind my eyes I saw her as she had been before me. Walking down a street I had never been to. Sitting cross-legged on a tile floor across from someone whose face I would never know. I put the phone down. She had returned to her journey, and I was looking for mine.

---

When I left again, it was evening. I'd planned to retrace my morning route to the burning ghat, but the city had changed. The time for business had passed with the setting sun, and now was the hour of ritual. Every ghat had been given over to ceremony. Enormous crowds were lighting candles in celebration of the festival. People painted mandalas on the ground. Each street was filled with processions of music and drums. The city was in its trance.

I arrived at the burning ghat and saw a few familiar faces from Baba Lutlo's crew.

When I asked for him, they pointed up to where he stood amongst the pyres of wood.

He beckoned me over.

"Ah, man. Come, come. I want to show you something."

He started climbing over rafters and through construction sites and up gates like some kind of parkour master while I did my best to keep up on my prosthetic, hauling myself over railings and up the sides of small buildings. We came out on the temple roof, looking down on the ancient crematory with its stacks of platforms for the dead.

Then he brought me to a tiny room that felt more like a cave than a man-made space, looking out over the burning ghat through a missing wall. Three old men sat inside, tending a modest fire in the middle of a truly massive bed of ash. Baba Lutlo lit a cigarette and talked with them for a while.

"This fire has been burning for three thousand years," he said.

Already old when the Khan was born, or when the pyramids of the jungle were built. He had taken me to the heart of Varanasi, the fire of Shiva, the destructive force that returned all things to ash. The men tending it were *Dom*, a hereditary lineage of fire-keepers who had passed the responsibility from father to son across more generations than anyone could count. Every cremation pyre on these ghats had to be lit from this flame. Without the Dom, there was no fire. Without the fire, there was no passage. They had kept it through floods that submerged the ghats and monsoons that lasted months.

"If this fire ever went out," Baba Lutlo said, "Varanasi would cease to be Varanasi."

It was fully dark by then. He led me down through the temple, then beneath it, to where the bodies burned.

We passed so close to the pyres that I could see a skull burst open and brains sizzle out. The flames were uncomfortably hot against my skin. An acrid smoky scent filled my nostrils. No one was crying. Baba Lutlo told me they believed tears stopped the soul from departing, so the living watched the dead with meditative solemnity.

Through a crowded doorway, then deeper and deeper underground.

We came at last to a huge shrine to Shiva illuminated by candlelight. Men were beating drums, led by a guru who stood on one leg, never letting the other touch the ground.

"That's his meditation," Baba Lutlo said. The guru had chosen to use only that leg his entire life.

I watched him for a while. We took our shoes off and stepped onto the stone floor, the plastic of my foot against rock. 

Boom. Boom. Boom. Boom. Boom. Boom.

The sounds of more drums merged in the underground chamber until the walls seemed to vibrate. I couldn't tell where my heartbeat ended and the drum began, just as it had been in Mongolia when the shaman's hammer fell.

BOOM. BOOM. BOOM. BOOM.

I was in a trance. I couldn't be sure how long I'd been there. Minutes? Hours?

Finally, Baba Lutlo grinned at me. "Let's get out of here."

I left that place in a dazed state of shock.

As we walked to the riverbank, Baba Lutlo told me this was where he'd grown up. His father had been a Brahman holy man, and his family had lived here for six generations or more. He knew everyone because this was his home.

We met up with his friends who were smoking hashish. The conversation ranged from girls to existential philosophy. I spent all night with them, visiting one temple and crematory after another. Each was beautiful in its own way. But it was the drums that kept pulling me back.

One temple stood perched above the primary ghat, looking from a distance like a human-sized birdhouse on a small pedestal wedged between the endless scaffolded buildings that rose around it. Its darkened gray stones were caked in candle wax from what must have been centuries of flame. Inside, another ceremony was underway. The rhythm was fast and explosive, like the crescendo of the shaman's drum, except this crescendo never seemed to end. It just kept building and building, a steady pulse.

BOOM. BOOM. BOOM.

Baba Lutlo climbed up and instructed me to follow. We slid into the dark. This time I was handed a two-sided drum and I dropped completely into the trance. Time no longer registered, hours passed as my mind flowed into the rhythm.

When I finally looked down at my hands, I saw what I'd done. My palms had split open. I hadn't noticed. I hadn't felt it. My body had detached from itself somewhere in the drumming, and the pain had simply not registered. Blood oozed from the wounds, mixed with cow dung, temple dust, and the muddy river water that carried the ashes of the dead. If I didn't clean it, it would certainly get infected.

"Baba Lutlo, I need to find a place to clean this!" I said in desperation.

He led me back to the stacks of wood piled high for the pyres. Next to the wood was a little door. He opened it and invited me into the home his family had lived in for generations. There was an older woman inside, and a little girl. Baba Lutlo introduced them as his mother and daughter. He picked up his little girl and spun her around. Once again his entire bearing shifted. He was no longer a thief or a guru. He was a father and a son.

His daughter was six. She'd been coloring in a drawing when we walked in, and she ran up to him and jumped into his arms. Millie and Charlie flashed through my mind.

His mother washed my hand thoroughly with clean water and rubbing alcohol, then bandaged me up. They offered me food, which I took gratefully.

After some time, Baba Lutlo said, "Okay, let's go back out." He opened another door, and there they were, right on the other side. The burning bodies.

That was the moment I realized that every day of Baba Lutlo's life, he'd woken up, opened *this door*, and come face to face with the smoldering remains of people whose families had traveled from every corner of India to bring them here. He'd watched flesh turn to ash and join the river and the air. He'd seen mourning and celebrating, laughter and tears and fights. The entire circle of life, just a few feet from the house where both he and his daughter were born. In a city of pilgrims, the Guru Heart Thief belonged. Next to him, even the sadhus were tourists.

---

Late in the night I stepped away from Baba Lutlo and his gang to find a mangled bathroom along the ancient walkways of the ghats. It was the still hour that lay just before sunrise.

The Diwali candles had burned through, but no one was up to clean them yet. My body exhausted but my mind still in wonder.

How had the vibrations of this place traveled around the world and found themselves wrapped around my head in the exact moment of my physical death and rebirth?

Baba Lutlo found me again on the riverbank. We walked along together, stopping here and there to linger around mandalas painted in the street from the night before.

"Diwali is where we celebrate light winning over darkness," Baba Lutlo explained as we passed scores of spent candles.

We hung out for a while overlooking a central square as the city woke. I still owed him a drink.

"Let me buy you some chai." His favorite chai stand was a few ghats over, but when we got there and I reached for my wallet, I realized I didn't have it. I'd left it near where we had first met. I rushed back through the busiest city I've ever seen, hoping against all odds. And to my astonishment, it was there, unbothered in the hazy morning sun. No one had taken it.

Baba Lutlo and I had our tea.

In the days that followed, I stopped sleeping in the hostel and started living on the streets with my new crew. I ate where they ate and ran the ghats all night long, climbing over the city like we belonged to it. They did. Running through the alleyways they stopped in front of a white cow. *Gau Mata,* one of them said. *Mother Cow.* In Hinduism the cow is sacred, representing motherhood, purity, non-violence. They stood around her with reverence and I snapped a photo. It looked timeless, in a place that worshiped impermanence.

The last morning Baba Lutlo asked some of his late father's friends, Brahman priests, to perform a ritual to Shiva for me. We borrowed a long boat and went onto the river for the ceremony. As we drifted on the water, he pointed to the far western bank of the Ganges, opposite Varanasi. An empty expanse of sand dunes where dark figures wandered. Those were sadhus who practiced black magic, he said. They fed upon the charred and rotten remains of the bodies burned across the river. He'd gone once to those banks and fallen ill for days.

Varanasi, I realized, sat between the realms of life and the afterlife, in the beauty and ugliness of it all.

When we finished the sun had already begun its arc from behind the ancient brick temples toward the river. People appeared through the tiny alleyways at the bottoms of buildings, once again to stretch and clean themselves and wash their clothes. Deep red pigments from last night's ceremonies bled into the Ganges. Kids played amongst the boats, not twenty feet from the burning pyres of the dead.

"Hindus believe that dying in Varanasi grants *moksha*, liberation from the cycle of reincarnation entirely," Baba Lutlo explained, telling me how the sick and dying travel from across India to spend their final days here, not in hospitals but in hospices overlooking the river, waiting for the ghats to receive them. 

"Maybe they'll find some leftover gold teeth, if they are lucky," Baba Lutlo said, pointing at the kids swimming in the waters.

He took out a bead necklace. His face was different now. The mischievous smile he'd worn since the moment I met him, the grin that was always performing, always playing, was gone. 

"Let me teach you the sound. Each time, move one bead as you make it. Meditate with your finger. It is the oldest one there is, older than any word. Everything came after it."

Om.

"Let go of all your thoughts. Focus only on the sound. Truly."

Om.

I tried to let go, but my mind kept replaying everything we'd talked about since I arrived. The destroyer and the creator. Many forms in one.

"Don't think!" he said, seeing the pensive look on my face.

Om.

I watched Baba Lutlo as he chanted. The son. The father. The teacher.

Om.

Drifting in the void of anesthesia and waking up unsure if I was still in the same reality.

Om.

Lying in a pool of my own blood, waiting for the ambulance.

Om.

Picking away at my guitar on a cold night in a half-built house, my kids with their mother on the other side of town.

Om.

I pressed on the beads, one at a time, listening to the water. A line came back to me from the pages I'd been reading the morning Baba Lutlo found me. "The river flowed and flowed, and yet it was always there. Always the same, and every moment new." Then for the briefest moment my mind settled into the space between thoughts.

Om. Om. Om.

---

When I got home I threw away my shoes. They were covered in the grime and dirt of a city built to burn the dead. And then I was back in San Diego, back in single fatherhood, making breakfast and packing lunches.

On the drive to school I told the kids about what I had seen in India. Millie was now five and in the same elementary school as seven-year-old Charlie. Both seemed shocked at the descriptions of the city, and the fact that I had lived with a gang of thieves. When they were finished listening to my stories I'd put on "The Final Countdown" and turn the volume up just as we pulled up to their school. In the final block I'd get them to yell an exclamation of how great of a day we were all going to have.

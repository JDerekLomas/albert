CHAPTER 15
Heʻe Nalu - Wave Riders

A great wave of a giant storm had come from a distant shore. We hadn't chosen it. None of us got to choose.

Charlie was ten and Millie was eight.

I had stocked up months before, because I could feel it coming. In my travels I'd watched foreign countries tighten their health protocols, screenings and closures, long before anyone at home seemed worried. We now had stacks of canned deviled meats and beans the kids wanted nothing to do with.

The kids built a fort of blankets and cushions that took over the downstairs living room and became our shared bedroom for the first months of the pandemic.

The house had become a farm. There was the school's pet bunny, whose stay was extended indefinitely, a set of chickens I built a coop for so we could have eggs as supply chains kept falling, plus lizards, ant farms, chrysalises, whatever came our way. The kids named every animal. The nice chicken was Charlotte, the mean one Tiger, and the cat was Jack. I always thought they gave good names, the first kitten anointed when they were toddlers as Nunu Firetruck.

One afternoon a Cooper's hawk took a mourning dove off the back fence. Charlie reported it to me.

"Dad. Did the dove hurt?"

"I don't know, buddy."

"I think it did. But maybe just for a second."

Millie was still running her monarchy and we were her subjects. She would sit us down in the backyard and guide us through her own invention of what she called "leaf meditations," making us hold a plant from the garden and feel its "vibes." Charlie would do as commanded until a bug caught his eye, at which point he would run over to hide it from the chickens.

As the months wore on, they sat through hours of Zoom classes with the maturity of college students, but they were only eight and ten years old. When they were done Charlie would run around the house with his Nerf guns, targeting mostly me. Millie made slime and chased the chickens around the yard. It was a happy time.

Yet after eight months of being told death loomed around the corner, it was easy to feel like we were under siege.

So we snuck out. I'd take the kids to the Anza Borrego desert and we'd dig for fossils in the sandstone. When they were with Kara in our dual house Covid bubble, I'd find my way to the sea.

It was made illegal in those early months to seek nature. But some nights after sunset I'd slip through and wade in. There was an algae bloom carrying bioluminescent plankton that lit up like stars when the waves churned them. Each step made a tiny supernova beneath my foot. I'd float and let the ocean hold me in a galaxy of glowing microorganisms.

---

Five months before Cambridge, another call had come. A new show being developed called Welcome to Earth, pairing the world famous Will Smith with a handful of explorers, to have remarkable experiences with natural forces. They asked if I wanted to tell the story of vortices by surfing the barreling waves of Hawaii's North Shore.

Shit.

The North Shore wasn't San Diego. Hawaii was where Mike Coots had his leg bitten off. Surfers got crushed into the reef there every winter and didn't always come back up. But the picture of Mike in the barrel had been what gave me the courage to amputate. Would I find out if the image I saw in that photo was something I could actually live, or just a face I'd been wearing?

I said yes.

I spent the following months frantically preparing, not reading papers and philosophizing about esoteric theories, but paddling till my arms felt like rubber. I asked friends for guidance on barrel techniques. I watched hours of videos. I got up at dawn each morning to surf before the wind picked up.

On the days I had the kids, I'd get home just in time to make them breakfast, still covered in sand and salt. Millie would wrinkle her nose at me.

It wasn't enough, not for where I was going.

Before I had cameras pointed at me in one of the highest-profile filming projects of my career, I wanted to experience the North Shore surf firsthand. Not so I wouldn't embarrass myself. So I wouldn't die.

The producers connected me to a Hawaiian board shaper and indigenous leader who'd brought back many of the island's ancient customs. He shaped massive boards from traditional Hawaiian woods entirely by hand, and had resurrected the once-forgotten practice of sledding down lava fields at death-defying speeds. He was in his late sixties but in photos he looked ageless. His name was Pohaku. I flew out early to Oahu to meet him.

I wasn't exactly prepared when the first thing Pohaku said was, "Hey, fucker. You going to buy me dinner?"

He was older but strong, bald, covered in traditional Hawaiian tattoos, and more than a little standoffish. At first he probably saw me as just another guy from National Geographic. They were always calling him.

We met for dinner at the place of his choosing, the priciest restaurant on the island. He showed up with his family and ordered steaks and fish. If he was going to help, he would make damn sure it was worth his while.

I told him about Mike Coots. About the picture. About the hospital bed I'd seen it from. He looked at me, sizing me up.

Finally he said, "Okay. We try tomorrow. Be ready early."

I was too nervous to sleep. I sat at my window watching the waves break in the dark.

Pohaku picked me up in the amber light of sunrise. The water was clear and warm. There were no cameras.

I'd brought a board my friend had shaped specifically for me. The ankle of my prosthetic was a spring that didn't rotate, so I'd cranked it out with a hex wrench to get lower in my stance and attached the leg to the strap of the board. If the waves ripped it off, at least I'd find it tied to something that floated.

"Okay, let's surf."

They were bigger than predicted. Eight to ten feet on their backs, maybe more. But I couldn't back down.

He led me into the water with little explanation. There were channels in the break, places where the riptide pulled outward through the chaos, and Pohaku found them by feel, the way Don Antonio had found paths in the jungle, the way Amaraa had read the steppe.

We were paddling over swells of energy that had been crossing the Pacific for days, a long low pulse. Now the reef was bringing it up to be seen. The floor rose where it found rock. The water heaved upward. What had been a pulse a thousand miles long lifted into a wall before us, stood for a moment, and broke. Some of these reefs could stand up walls over sixty feet tall on big days. Thankfully today wasn't that. They were "decent size," he said casually.

I followed him through the channels. The current pulled us out past where the break was forming. I sat on my board, breathing hard, while he watched the horizon. The salty spray of the waves filled the air with the smell of the sea.

"So what was it like growing up here?" I asked.

"I don't talk when I surf," he said. "I focus. The world is chaos. In the chaos, you wait for the moment. Focus."

The waves came in sets of three, a handful of mellow ones and then the energy would change. They arrived that way because the surface of the ocean was like the skin of a drum, and days out to sea a storm had struck it. The blow from beyond my eye's reach was still arriving, pulse after pulse.

The first hill would build into a wall, and one of the walls would be the right one. If I read it correctly I could be on it. If I didn't, it would be on me.

The roller came.

"No, wait," he said. "Behind it. Outside. Paddle out, paddle out."

A bigger one was rising directly after it. We swam toward the back of the break and rose up over the crest of the first as the next bore down. I barely made it over the second.

"This is the one. Go!"

I paddled as hard as I could and missed it, its energy rolling under me like a train passing by.

"Next one. Get deeper."

Closer to the break. Fully commit. No hesitation.

The next wave came. Twelve feet. It felt like a building approaching. I turned the board and drove my hands into the water. The momentum took me. I dropped down the face, rose to my feet, slid into the slope of a storm born somewhere far beyond the horizon.

And then the lip curled and the wave punched me into the reef.

Everything went dark. I was a rag doll in the churn. My prosthetic slid against something hard. I was at the bottom. "Get up. Don't die down here." I grabbed the cord between my leg and my board and let it pull me toward light.

I broke surface gasping. A heaving wall of whitewater boiled behind me. I climbed onto my board and started paddling.

I didn't get away. Another wall hit. Then another. Each one stamped me into the reef, punishing me for trespassing. By the time I got out of the whitewater I'd been pushed a quarter mile from where I'd started. Bruised, gassed, scraped. Alive.

I paddled back to Pohaku. He looked surprised I hadn't given up.

"Okay brah. Again?"

I went again. I got pummeled again, and again until a massive wave grabbed my board in its whitewater and took my prosthetic with it. Bobbing legless in the wash, I yelled to him that I had to swim in.

I washed up exhausted on a strip of sand, dismembered and spent, next to a group of tourists and some turtles nesting there. The tourists stared with alarm. The turtles couldn't have cared less.

Pohaku walked over, covered in sand and water, holding my board in one hand and my leg in the other, which had been bobbing on the leash.

"Here you go brah. You a crazy fucka. Let's go."

That was the first time I met Pohaku. He liked the craziness in me, I think. And I had seen something in him too, a man who catered to no one, who lived entirely as himself, and whose self happened to carry the quintessential spirit of the islands. Mana, he said, the power felt in the lava itself.

---

Two weeks later I was back, this time with a Nat Geo film crew. We were further into the winter swell. The waves were bigger, twelve to fifteen feet Hawaiian, which is measured by the back of the wave, meaning the faces were twenty feet and more. I had never been in water like that. Pohaku had arranged for two legendary North Shore lifeguards on jet skis, Jeff O and Nolan, to be water safety. In Hawaii, the rescue teams are the heroes who put themselves in the worst of it because that's where the people who need rescuing are.

The first day I tried everything to catch a wave. The increased swell made paddling alone almost useless. The bigger rollers needed near-superhuman strength or a jet ski to tow you in and the skill to be able to pull that off. Jet ski or no jet ski, I failed.

The second day, I thought I'd caught one. Moments later it crushed me and split my board in half. Pohaku got me another from his son's collection. I continued to struggle. The waves kept growing.

Then he called over to Nolan: "Fuck it, brah. Throw me the rope."

He got on the rope and wakeboarded behind the jet ski into position, then whipped into the shoulder of the biggest breaker of the day. As it hit the reef, it spiked upward into a towering twenty-five-foot wall. Pohaku flew across its face. Going down he was smiling like a Buddha, shaved head, infectious grin, pure bliss. It was the first time I'd seen his purely joyful state.

Later, on the beach, he sat with me. I had bleeding slices across my arm from slamming into my fins in the chaos. He told me he'd been on his own since he was thirteen. The world had always been chaos. He pointed to his own scars to prove it, a run of them down one leg, the mark of a deep gash he'd taken wrecking on a wooden lava sled, hurtling down the flank of a volcano on runners built to slide over the rock.

He looked at the horizon for a long moment before he spoke again.

"These waves are generated by storms from distant horizons," he said. "They arrive at our shore. You don't get to choose when they arrive or how strong they are. You only get to choose what to do with them." It seemed to sum up so much of what my life had already shown me.

The next day I was on a plane home and a few months later Covid would come to stop us all in our tracks.

---

The world turned and time passed as we all collectively went through the shared experience of the pandemic. But eventually it eased, and people started to find each other again out of the darkness. We needed each other, as much as we needed air to breathe or water to drink.

Friends began joining me in the ocean. Alex was one of them, a surfer and filmmaker I'd met in Death Valley years earlier when he'd pulled the kids and me out of a sand trap in the middle of the night. That adventure had surprisingly ended up being one of my kids' favorite memories.

Alex had moved to San Diego a year into the pandemic. I woke every morning in the predawn darkness, slipped out while the kids slept in the fort, and drove through fog to meet him at the beach. As the sun rose, the mist painted the water in pastels, the last glimmers of bioluminescence fading.

One morning I told him about Pohaku and the picture of Mike Coots. About the joy in it. About what I was trying to find out.

"You should keep going for it," he said. "You should get that barrel. We should film it."

That was the start of what we'd call the Barrel Project. Johnny Harrington joined too, an underwater cinematographer I'd met during early test filming of Welcome to Earth. Johnny was an East Coast kid who'd cut his teeth on Shark Week and now built a massive camera housing in my garage, and would wade into the water with me each time the waves picked up.

The surfing began in earnest. Between takes, Johnny and Alex floated in the cold winter waters for hours giving me pointers, how to turn, how to slow down, how to read the energy under the board. At first I had a hard time feeling anything through the carbon fiber of my leg. Everything a surfer does by proprioception, I had to imagine mine.

And I trained for the breath holds. The North Shore had taught me something powerful about fear, showing me the impact of the mind's state on extending survival when the breath is gone. I needed to find a way to get used to it so I wouldn't panic when the time came.

Charlie, now eleven, and Millie, nine, both became my training crew. They'd dunk me in the hot tub at home as I held on for as long as I could. Charlie kept the time out loud, his voice steady like a lab partner running an experiment. Millie's job was to make the experiment harder. They would toss me around in the jets of water as they giggled. Through the muffled sound I heard them, and locked the memory and feeling into my mind so that when the wave came I could return to there.

In the winter of 2021, we watched for storms. Pohaku mapped the weather across the Pacific, and when the time was right, we packed our bags.

---

On the North Shore once more, but this time Pohaku began showing me something beyond the waves.

He took me, Alex, and Johnny to his home on a hill overlooking the east side of the island. Under a jungle canopy that pressed against his backyard, he stood in board shorts looking at a giant plank of ulu wood laid across two workbenches. He ran his hand along the grain, feeling for what was inside. He picked up an old planing tool, something that looked like a shuttlecock with two handles, and pressed it into the wood, sliding it up and down to "find the shape." Little curls of wood spun up as he worked, like waves themselves.

He shaped boards the way his uncles had, by hand, no measurements just feel, finished with kukui nut oil. He'd buried his boards in taro fields to absorb the *mana* of the earth. When he was working, the gangster cadence fell away, replaced by the echo of the line of the ones who had carved before him.

This is who he was, a wisdom keeper of an ancient tradition.

A papa holua tattoo ran down the side of his leg. A giant sled, he described how he had built one from a memory almost as faint as a dream, and how his ancestors, the ancient Hawaiians, used them to hurl themselves down lava-rock mountainsides at terrible speed.

The practice had nearly disappeared. "Good thing I remembered it!" he said.

We went to the big island for a few days. There he guided us to a field of blackened lava behind some giant hotel. The rock was rough underfoot. He walked slowly, watching the ground, and then he stopped and pointed.

Etched into the lava beneath us were circles. Then more circles. Then concentric rings, a figure in profile, a row of dots, a long arcing line of marks I couldn't read. The longer I looked the more I saw. An endless mural across the surface of the rock. A canvas his ancestors had pressed into the stone.

"These are my people," he said. "All the way back to the beginning. To the first canoes, the first wayfinders. To the shark, my family's god."

His finger traced one of the circles. The roots of a man whose people had stood on the edge of the only world and decided to voyage into the unknown. His line were explorers who had read the stars and the swell and carried their families across thousands of miles of open ocean. They had sung the chants together to wake the boats before launch.

The chants were kept by elders of both lines, masculine and feminine, sky and earth in the universal duality I had observed in Peru and elsewhere. They merged individuals into a community. No one sailed into the unknown by themselves.

I looked and thought of the kava-pounding stones I'd seen across Oceania a year before while filming an expedition to the mysterious site of Nan Madol on the Micronesian island of Pohnpei. Circular depressions on stone worn smooth on islands separated by thousands of miles of water.

There I had drunk a little too much from the sakau cup while the *Nahnmwarki* (clan chief) stared into me to determine my suitability of spirit to enter their most sacred place. I had seen the stone bell of the community ring as the roots were pounded, and heard the collective spirit of the wayfinders in its rhythm. I had even been bestowed the tribal title of "Luhk Dolen Katau" in the ceremony of belonging. The same rhythm pounding in my chest, then and now. The petroglyphs under our feet looked like cousins of those stones, and the power of community in the face of the unknown was the story I was hearing from across the archipelago of the Pacific, Oceania.

The sun was dropping toward the sea behind him. Light came up over the horizon, then turned amber, then orange, then a blood red that ran like lava across the sky above his head.

"We come from the darkness, from the stone and water," he said.

He explained the meaning of his name Pohaku, the stone. "Our darkness is what other people call light."

He believed he'd been a shark in another life and would be again after his death.

---

One day Mike Coots flew out to surf with us. The last time I had seen him I was in a wheelchair at a Challenged Athletes triathlon in La Jolla, a few weeks after the amputation, when my friends had decided I needed to get back into the world. He had leaned down to my chair and pulled me into a hug, wearing a cap that read JAWS, and told me we would be surfing within the year. Now here he was, in the water beside me. Pohaku called Jeff O and Nolan again with the jet skis, and the four of them began teaching me how to tow into bigger waves.

Mike and I sat on our boards in the lull between sets. He was watching the horizon.

"When I was younger and trying to make it as a pro," he said, "I used to strap a GoPro to my board. When I looked at the pictures I was grimacing in every one. My face locked in terror."

He paused.

"So I decided to start smiling instead."

I listened, bobbing on the water.

"The instant I made that choice I was in flow. Try smiling," he suggested.

The image of Pohaku ran through my mind again, smiling like Buddha as he rode perfection in the chaos.

I told Mike something I had never admitted to myself. That his photograph, the one I'd stared at from the hospital bed, had become a mask for me. That I'd used his joy to paper over my own pain, to build the strong face that carried me through the years of being El Robotico, the bionic explorer, the guy who jokes about snakes.

But I had carried that mask through all the holy lands and back. Underneath it was a loss I had never let myself fully look at. I was out here, I told him, trying to find out if the promise his picture had made me was real. If the joy in it was something honest in me, or something I had only borrowed.

Mike listened. A warm smile spread across his face, lit by the setting sun.

"Well, what's it going to be?" he asked.

---

The day was almost over. A distant gale sent us one more wave. A single wave like any other and at the same time profoundly unique.

I start paddling. *You only get to choose what to do with them*, Pohaku's voice is in my chest.

The swell rises behind me. I rise with it.

Pop to my foot.

The slope opens.

The lip is curling and everything is moving and the body has already chosen. The mask is in my hand. I let it go.

I remember to smile.

The water folds over my head into a Fibonacci spiral. Same shape as a sea shell. Same shape as a hurricane. Same shape as a galaxy arm. The ocean using its one geometry. Time slows. The wall in front of me flattens into a smooth arching sheet and rushes toward me, crystal blue, molten glass in motion. The pocket holds.

I don't remember any sound. There was only the feeling of being inside a vortex of energy hurtling forward, and me sliding along its front edge, barely staying ahead, like a fish trying to outrun the closing mouth of a whale.

Then the wall closes.

The reef.

The churn.

Same as every time before. Except this time I had been inside it.

I come up giggling.

I lie on my back in the wash, exhausted and laughing. Pohaku, Mike, Johnny, and Alex are scattered across the water somewhere. I don't see them yet. The ocean cradles me. The sound the kids made in the hot tub is in my ear. It was *aloha*. What Pohaku said once was "The love emerging from everything." I was feeling it.

I lie there till the next one behind punishes me for lingering, but it doesn't matter.

This time the wave was the door. The community was the board. The smile was the choice.

And once again it was euphoric.

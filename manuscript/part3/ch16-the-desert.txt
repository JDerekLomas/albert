CHAPTER 16
The Desert

By the fall of 2023 the pandemic was behind us and we were deep into filming the last evolution of Lost Cities. Each expedition reached further, the locations riskier and, at the same time, profound and exciting. The rugged shorelines of Scotland, the war-torn stretches of Sudan, the unstable cliffs of Israel. One morning as I climbed to the ruins of a Canaanite watchtower, a giant boulder came loose with me on it, luckily I jumped out of the way barely far enough before it crushed my prosthetic foot. Had I not been amputated, it would have taken me out, far from home, staying in a kibbutz on the coast.

Life on the road was one extraordinary and often extreme experience after another. But back home Millie, now ten, said she wanted to play softball.

I had never played baseball or softball growing up, so I bought a bucket of balls, a bat, and gloves and went to the park to figure it out. With all my traveling, the kids had missed a lot of ordinary childhood, and it was time to make up for it.

I tossed the first ball softly. It fell in front of her glove.

"Throw it harder, Daaaad."

I threw it harder. She caught it. Threw it back. Real throw, the ball coming in fast, my glove almost not up in time.

"Harder."

She had a stance at the plate I hadn't seen in her before. Square feet. Hands on the bat. Fire in the eyes that looked past me at the ball in my hand.

She made a team, they called themselves "Danger." We were both in it, in a way. I volunteered as a sub coach, doing my best not to feel insecure about my athletic abilities in front of a group of ten-year-olds.

The first time she went up to bat in a real game I could barely watch. She took her cuts. Got on base. And I nearly fell out of the dugout jumping with joy.

Amongst my happiest memories of life are those wonderfully long late summer afternoons where we would practice, just her and me and a bucket of balls in a park as the sun set.

The first time she pitched, she walked out to the mound and gave a quick look over at me in the stands. All eyes on her, and she was checking on me. I was more terrified than she was. She stood on the mound and threw anyway, brave enough for the both of us. In a sea of wild pitches, a few zingers shot down the middle. And then a strike out! After the inning she ran back to me.

"Did you see?"

"I saw."

I saw a bravery in her that made my crawling around tombs seem mundane.

One night driving home from practice I told her she reminded me of myself at her age. The youngest of three, scrapping for a seat at the table while Jane and Ed called the shots. The little poop nickname that had stuck for life. She laughed. She seemed to like knowing I had been the youngest once too.

Charlie's was the other season, the one made of snow.

We had been driving up to Mammoth as a family since they were kids and after the pandemic that only increased. High in the Eastern Sierra, and somewhere in the powder and the runs we took top to bottom giggling the whole way, it had become our mountain, the one we kept coming back to.

I would watch him drop in ahead of me, already the better skier at twelve, his hoots carrying back up the slope, and I could see it becoming the thing he was building himself around, the way the tombs and jungles had once been for me.

His obsession spilled into everything, even his school presentations, which he'd pace through at the kitchen table the night before, revising until I made him go to bed. "Did you know the first ski army was from Norway?" he'd open, deadpan.

They were growing, entering new phases of life with each season.

At home Charlie's life was now loud, epic sleepovers, Halloween swords, Nerf battles that went to the extreme, and video games which he wrangled me into. Millie's world was behind a sign taped to her door that read NO when Charlie's friends were over and looking for someone to ambush, a big hand drawn around the letters, while she and her best friend disappeared into immense imaginary worlds under a mountain of crafting supplies. But they were also sweet, there when needed as a consistent presence for each other as they grew up bouncing between their mother's world and mine.

When I left for expeditions during this time, the ache was different than it had been before the lockdown. The pandemic had brought us so much closer, and the world was opening and I was back to juggling my two worlds.

---

The expedition this time took me to Oman, into the sand sea of the Arabian desert. It was November.

My family of immigrants had always celebrated Thanksgiving with a large turkey, stuffing, gravy, and mashed potatoes and yams like the rest of our neighbors growing up. We didn't know much about it. We just did it.

As a young father I had done my best to continue the transplanted tradition, partly out of nostalgia, partly because I loved the feeling of holding a giant turkey leg in my hand and biting into it like I was a caveman. So when I found myself stuck on the other side of the world and feeling homesick, Kara agreed to bring the kids out thinking it would be a good experience for them.

By that point she and I had figured out a working co-parenting relationship that teetered on friendship. But the juggle was relentless. I'd drawn a hard line professionally to be home in equal time between expeditions, but the season had been stretched by delays and disruptions until something had to give. Now in a time of year that I desperately wanted to be home more than anything, the compressed production timeline needed me to stay on the road. I suggested as a compromise that the family come out to meet me during the holidays as an adventure. Kara agreed. Suddenly we were a divorced family on vacation in Oman.

Kara had been nervous, given the conservative nature of Oman and her openness about her own identity. But what we found was kindness. Our Thanksgiving was spent in an ancient desert oasis town. There was no turkey. Instead the hospitality of a local Omani community and a feast of shuwa, lamb slow-roasted underground in palm leaves, and tiny cups of kahwa. We ate with our hands, sitting on a bed of pillows and a worn carpet, grounding in the most literal way. That night the kids slept beside me on mats in an ancient trading post, the stone walls still holding the cool of the centuries. Charlie pulled out some cologne they had found in a local market. The distinctive wood scent of oud engulfed the room. I lay there with my children beside me, in a country across the world from the one they'd been raised in, sharing a Thanksgiving that had never really been about the turkey.

The week before, we had made our way from the Omani coastline, where we entered magnificent mosques and watched hundreds of baby sea turtles make their way from their nests to the water's edge, their flippers churning across the moonlit sand. They were only a few hundred yards from mounds of dirt that hid a five-thousand-year-old Bronze Age boatyard, the ancient Magan civilization, the first metal workers, who had launched their legendary blackened vessels, described in Biblical texts, from this shore carrying copper to Mesopotamia and left humanity's oldest metal fish hooks in the same sand where the turtles had hatched.

Holding one of those hooks, I thought of a shore I'd stood on years before, Alta, at the northernmost tip of Norway, under a midnight sun that hung on the horizon and refused to set. Carved into the rock there was an image seven thousand years old: a figure in a boat, a line dropping into the depths, a giant halibut on the end of it, and a bear beside it for scale. A boast reaching across seven thousand years. *I caught a fish the size of a bear.*

That night I hired a small boat and watched Charlie fish off the back of it with a handline and bread for bait, filling a bucket with perch we grilled and ate by hand while Millie and Kara traded Arabic words with the crew. String, bread, and patience, the simplest technology there is. And here was my son pulling dinner from the same sea, the same impulse still alive in his hands. Someone had to imagine it first.

We took our boat into the Sea of Oman. Soon we found a small cove on a nearby coastal island that looked like a pile of desert rocks rising from a blue plane. We jumped into the warm water and slipped on masks. Schools of fish amongst corals, light coming down through the water in long rays. Millie held my hand as we kicked along the surface. Charlie took dips deeper, reaching toward the schools before coming up for air. Suddenly in the distance sea turtles emerged. At first a few, then many. Kara poked her head above the surface, calling us over to a large cluster feeding below and before you knew it we were all hovering there in wonder together.

I borrowed a truck and soon we were traversing the Arabian desert, through the wadis and into the sea of dunes that spread to the horizon. It was so much like our home dunes in Death Valley, only a darker shade of red and endless. Also home to the Bedouin where ours were empty. Camels roamed grazing areas where small grass fields found their way out of the mountains of sand.

"Follow the desert wash for twenty kilometers, then you will see one tree, and turn right at the tree up the large sand dune to the south and you will see the camp on the horizon," we were told by the tourist camp we had contacted through an online site that advertised geodesic Martian-like domes on flowing red sand.

Without any infrastructure and the shifting sands in front of us, the kids and Kara all laughed nervously. Were these really our directions? A lone tree, our only marker in the blank sands of Arabia. But sure enough, an hour later the tree appeared and everyone cheered and sang as our little four-by-four charged along.

On paper it was a strange picture, an ex-wife and ex-husband crossing faraway lands with their kids. But for a few days it felt like we were in it together, not as a couple, but as two people and their kids on an adventure. The grace we'd both been forcing for Charlie and Millie's sake was, out here, finally starting to come easy.

Millie was kissed by a camel. Charlie became "Habibi" with the Bedouin. That night, after finding our camp, we wore the ski goggles we had used back home in Death Valley to navigate an afternoon windstorm, and we all watched the sun set over the landscape. As the stars emerged we slid down the mountains of sand on makeshift sleds.

We could have been on Mars. But it felt like home.

---

The kids and Kara flew home a few days later and the crew arrived. Soon we would be pushing deeper into the same sands.

We camped on the dunes under Arabian stars that had been used by the Bedouin to find their way for millennia, eating lamb and rice with saffron prepared over a fire built from whatever the desert offered. Gary was cleaning a lens. Pete was adjusting a microphone he'd been fussing with all day. Katy Savage, our producer, was working through the next day's shot list. Nabhan sat across the fire from me, quietly eating with his hands. He was Bani Wahiba, of the Bedouin tribe that gave these sands their name, born in the Ramlat al-Wahiba itself, and roughly my age.

He reached into his robes and pulled out a knife. He handed it to me. It was silver, engraved with patterns I couldn't read, the handle worn smooth from generations of hands.

I pulled out my pocket knife in return. It snapped open at the push of a button, sharp and rugged and efficiently modern. But holding them side by side, there was no contest. Mine was a tool. His was a totem. It had come down to him father to father, further back than he had names for, each one leaving the oil of their grip on the same silver handle. Mine had been manufactured by the thousands in a factory somewhere.

He studied my knife for a moment, turning it over, clicking it open and closed. Then he handed it back and took his own with a speed that suggested he'd seen enough. We laughed, then Nabhan tucked his knife back into his robes like a father retrieving his child from a stranger.

After dinner the crew settled into their tents and the fire burned low. As the light of the sun faded, the ancient night emerged above the lingering warmth of day caught in the sand. I walked out above the camp onto the shoulder of a dune. Below me, the lights of our fire looked like a candle illuminating the walls of our circle of tents, and the voices of my team, dampened by the desert, felt distant. I was alone in the darkness looking in. I sat watching the scene for a while, a moment I knew would soon pass into memory. I was grateful. For the life of adventure after all these years, and for the chance to be part of a team that was somehow bigger than the sum of its parts.

My back was sore, maybe from the constant travel, and I decided to lie on the bed of silica, trillions of micro beads of stardust still holding the thermal vibrations of the sun. My eyes wandered up to trillions more suns floating in the infinite vacuum of space above. No canopy, no city light, no haze. Just the sand that was once stars and the stars that would one day be sand, and me lying between them.

It was the same sky I'd watched as a kid, lying on a sleeping bag in the Sierra Nevadas while my father pointed his telescope at the constellations and tried to explain where everything came from. The same sky Charlie and Millie and I had pretended to float through on the dunes of Death Valley, lying on our backs with our feet in the air like we were astronauts driving the ship that was our planet. And now the same sky over Arabia. A week earlier my kids had been under it with me, here, sliding down the sand on cardboard.

In the morning we drove toward the mountains. They were geological paintings that reminded me of home. But as we drew closer we passed communities that made me feel like I'd arrived on a distant planet. Mud-brick and stone buildings built into the mountainside itself, plastered in earth-colored adobe so that the villages seemed to grow from the rock rather than sit on it.

I'd been told these mountains were once blue, blue with copper ore. The Magan had looked at the blue and imagined something hiding inside, and figured out how to extract it. The copper traveled to Mesopotamia and beyond, becoming the raw material for tools and weapons and art and trade. They must have been magicians of their age. But the blue was gone now.

We ascended into the range, where a wildly joyful local cave explorer was to meet us. "They call me Khalid the Crazy, because I am crazy enough to do this stuff," he said with a huge grin and a curly fro as he and a team of cavers worked on a giant rope system they were building for us. "We're standing over a truly giant cave."

We harnessed in and lowered through a passageway scarred into the mesa. My footholds disappeared beneath me and I emerged through the roof of a chamber feet first into the darkness. The vastness of the opening engulfed my senses as my mind raced through the rigging system in a flash of fear from the void beneath. Slowly I descended, like a spider into a ballroom, only this ballroom was hundreds of feet tall. After a magical, suspended eternity, I found myself at the bottom, in a pool of water that had gathered in the dark.

The Magan had imagined metal inside blue rock. They had also found water inside stone and imagined a way to catch it. Their falaj channels, tunneled at the dawn of civilization, still carried life across Oman. What looks like nothing from above is holding everything below.

---

As we drove through the dunes that afternoon, Nabhan told me about his people. He spoke in fragments of English mixed with Arabic. He explained traditions and customs of the land. For example, in Oman, under Sharia law, you cannot charge interest on a loan, you cannot profit from another person's need. I asked him why. He looked at me like the answer was obvious. Out here, he said, if you take from someone who has nothing, everyone dies. Desert and steppe nomads, on opposite sides of the earth, imagining the same law because the landscape demanded it.

The next day we were heading deeper into the dunes. Our caravan of vehicles had separated across the sand. In a landscape with no roads, each truck found its own path. I was driving with Nabhan and Gary when we saw something that looked wrong. One lone camel, standing in the open sand, with what appeared to be a second set of feet sticking out of its body.

We stopped the truck and ran over. The baby was stuck, pointed the wrong way. We stood there, a one-legged explorer, a Scottish cameraman, and Nabhan trying to figure out how to deliver a camel in the middle of the Arabian desert with no one else in sight.

Then on the horizon, another truck appeared, hurtling up the wadi between the dunes. Nabhan waved them down. A group of Bedouin women piled out of the vehicle and without a word of discussion they ran to the camel, grabbed onto the calf's legs, and started pulling. I grabbed a leg too, I'd been timid but there was no room for that now.

In a giant goopy mess, the baby emerged onto the sand. One of the women put her hand into its mouth and cleared the breathing passage. Just like that, it was alive.

They stayed for a few minutes. Then they piled back into their truck and disappeared into the horizon, the way they'd come.

We stood there stunned in their wake and the sudden gentle silence of the desert, the baby lying on the sand with legs that didn't know how to work yet, wobbling, folding, trying. The mother reaching over to lick her baby's head. And then, the first steps. Unsteady, absurd, and unmistakably alive.

There it was. Something from nothing. Life.

This tiny being had been in the darkness of the womb only moments before. Strangers had reached in and pulled it across. Its pupils were dark, seemingly infinite, its eyes like uncanny opals taking their first glances at the world. And in them, as it had been with my kids, it was almost as if I could see the place it had just come through. I had only witnessed those two births before, and both times I had felt the same overwhelming sense of awe that surrounded us now.

We stayed for a while, watching as the baby's legs found their strength. I made a video message for Millie and Charlie. Then, with the rest of the team waiting in the washes ahead, we left. As we drove away Gary put on a bagpipe tune, he was Scottish after all, and the three of us sang along with made-up words as the dust kicked up behind our tires in a long flowing cloud.

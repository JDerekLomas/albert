CHAPTER 18
The Axis Mundi

I had spent my life facing death from every angle. Parts of my own body had literally already been reduced to ashes. And I had spent long stretches of time with the bones of the dead across all continents. It was strange to realize just how in between the realms my journey had been.

And after all that my biggest fear about the unthinkable was not necessarily the dying, so much as the being gone. It was the idea that I would never get to say everything I wanted to say to Charlie and Millie that scared me the most. I had avoided the thought, unable to even face it. But after seeing the look in Adam's eyes as he emerged from the darkness of the river, repeating "I didn't think I was going to make it," I was once again reminded of our impermanence, and that we don't get to choose when or how the thresholds appear. So one night, with my kids asleep upstairs and my bag packed by the door for the next trip, I sat at the kitchen table in the dark and wrote them a letter, to be read only if it ever came to that.

They were growing, metamorphosing with age. It had all happened so fast. Before I would know it they would no longer be kids, living their own journey. I could feel the cycles turning.

They had been my biggest teachers in life. How could I distill the meaning of it all onto a page for them?

I sat thinking about all we had experienced together, and what I had seen in the world through my travels. The power of the mind to create reality, the choice of perspectives that let us ride the waves how we choose to. I told them I hoped they would go to the dunes together long after I was gone and make more memories, that I would always be there in the magic even if not in form.

All the love that I had felt through life was pouring onto that page as the tears streamed down. And when I finished it I folded its edge and placed it in an envelope as a futile yet intentional artifact of my existence, words meant to carry my heart beyond the threshold of impermanence itself.

---

The hex wrench had been on five continents. The right-side snake gaiter had gone missing somewhere. My garage had become piles of gear for deserts, jungles, mountains, strewn amongst fishing rods and a ping-pong table. The piles had grown into small mountains of their own, a range I kept meaning to sort and never found time to.

Since Oman the expeditions had not stopped. Into the deserts of Sudan, where a brief window between wars let us explore the pyramids of the Kushites and swim in their submerged tombs along the Nile, before tear gas had flown overhead in Khartoum and burned our eyes. Or to the sea stacks of Scotland, where the painted warriors had once left the Romans shaking in their boots.

Together my team and I had gone through time and space. We were a traveling band of explorers and storytellers, and Lost Cities had grown into a journey decades in the making. I couldn't have been more grateful for each of them, men and women I had shared such intense experiences with at the edge of existence, time and time again. The show's future was never certain, though, and this was the last expedition on the calendar with everything in media changing before our eyes. Nothing is permanent, I reminded myself, with even more gratitude for the moment.

---

Chiapas, Mexico. Josuhé Lozada showed me the hidden messages in Palenque.

As a Mexican archaeologist with the National Institute of his country he had the authority to take me into a mysterious ancient stairway that had been locked to the public for decades after its discovery. With each step we were plunging into the heart of the iconic Maya pyramid, the Temple of the Inscriptions. At the bottom, behind a corbeled triangular doorway, was the burial chamber of King Pakal, the mighty Maya ruler, buried fourteen hundred years before.

The chamber was cooler than I expected. It smelled like a wine cellar, cool and damp, all mineral and old stone. Above his tomb, small stalactites formed from water that seeped through the pyramid's limestone.

In bands of carved stone that scholars had spent decades decoding was the name of a place where the gods had been born, a mythical place of origins. *Matwil.* A watery world of cormorants. Nearby, other glyphs. A breathing split mountain.

On the tomb's lid, a giant tree symbolizing the *cosmovision* of a people born from this jungle. Steve Houston had told me long ago that the cosmovision was a term for the universe view, the belief system of the Maya that described their philosophy of existence itself. Time, cycles, birth, death, all embodied in what surrounded that tree at its center, the Axis Mundi, through which all could pass. The pyramid surrounding us was itself a symbol, built in nine platforms for the nine levels of the underworld.

I stood there feeling like I had walked into the movies of my childhood. Even with my career and privileged experiences I could barely believe how perfect and mysterious this search seemed to be, breadcrumbs of stone read in the heart of a pyramid describing a primordial land where, for a people, it all started.

Matwil lingered in the air after Josuhé pointed out its glyph.

Some scholars took Matwil for pure myth, a place that existed only in the Maya imagination. Others thought differently. Many of the inscriptions across the Maya world had been historical recordings, like dedicated logs of the people's story.

A day later I met Joel Palka, a quiet, deeply respected Mayanist who was a peer of Steve Houston and Dave Stuart. Ponytail, rolled-up sleeves, the demeanor and look of a kinder Indiana Jones. He had come to believe Matwil might be a real place, and that it might still be out there to find. The clues pointed to Metzabok, a remote lake district deep in the forests of Chiapas, the headwaters that fed down toward Palenque, and astoundingly into a secret water passage that ran under the tomb I had just left.

The Lacandon who lived there now were descendants of Maya who had drifted back into the jungle after the classic world collapsed, long after the people we had come looking for were gone.

Joel and another archaeologist, Santiago Juarez, had found something at the lake's edge, Noh k'uh, a small cluster of ruins that carbon dating placed between roughly 400 BC and AD 200, near the earliest in the Maya story. It was a small site, but Joel was convinced it was only a corner of something far larger and older, a city the jungle had swallowed whole. The LiDAR we carried was meant to find the rest.

"If Matwil was anywhere," Joel said, "it could be close to that lake."

---

Over the years and through all the adventures the crew had become like family. Gary on camera with a small team of his own. Joe and Duncan on the scans that had become a signature of "removing the trees." Katy producing. Marianne or Tabs hunting down research. Graeme keeping us safe. And I always appreciated the unique relationship that developed with the rotation of audio recordists and directors, who would with me become the intimate triangle of sound that would hear and participate in every conversation I had as we navigated the unknown through its intensity and wonder.

There were others too, the local fixers, hosts, and scientists. On this expedition that meant the archaeologists we were following, and our Lacandon community, who we spoke with through Alicé, an Italian anthropologist who had spent decades living among them and learning their language. Gio, our badass expedition doctor, rode in the back of the truck and patched us up whenever the jungle took a bite out of any of us.

By now I had a rule about what had to come first, before any search.

Alicé walked us into the village to meet the h-men and ask for permission respectfully to enter their world. "An h-men is the keeper of altars and the old ways and this one is the last of his craft amongst his people," she told me. Again, I found myself at the boundary of the ethnosphere.

Suddenly I was sitting in front of a man, old and frail, with high cheekbones and an intense stare. His wild, unkempt hair, black going to gray, lay stark against a simple white robe.

He laid out a small altar in the backyard of his home, under a thatched hut that butted up against the jungle. Clay figures. A bowl. Sunlight coming through the smoke of his ritual. Each figure was an altar for a god, one for the mountain, one for the sun, and one for "the god of the white man" Jesucristo. He had not separated them.

Through Alicé he told us about the trees.

"The trees are connected to the stars. When a tree is cut, a star falls. When all the trees are gone, all the stars will fall, and the world will end."

He tried to describe that all things had a soul, even the rock I was sitting on.

He had run out of the ceremonial drink he would normally have poured for us, balché, the sacred brew the Lacandon ferment from tree bark and honey. Still, he sat with me.

"We would like to look for the land of origins," I told him. "To know where everything comes from."

The ceremony continued with smoke and chants. Eventually he gave us his blessing to do the work and sent us on our way.

Before we left Alicé translated his warning. "The lake holds the souls of the dead and the pre-born. Approach with respect."

I didn't try to explain the warning away, or project my own meaning onto his words. I resolved to lean in and feel what he meant, accepting that in a day, we would be paddling out over the liminal space of a lake's surface, between the realms of existence as defined by the belief system of the land.

---

Camp was on the shore of the lake. Joel's connections in the village had put up makeshift whitewashed boxes with air conditioning, one for Gary and one for me. It was real hospitality and I was grateful. They felt like holding cells.

Each night I sealed myself inside and the ancient jungle vanished into a white windowless cold. Gary swore that he'd rather take his chances with the mosquitos, but every night he climbed in anyway. Joe, for his part, had packed a suitcase of lemon-flavored mayonnaise that became the expedition's most divisive provision.

Kin and Boor were our guides on the water, assigned to us by the leader of the Lacandon community. Kin meant Sun; Boor, small bees.

The first morning Kin walked down to the lake with his oar and stood at the edge a long minute before he pushed off.

"To tell the lake we were coming. So it would not be surprised," he said in a blend of Lacandon and Spanish that Joel translated.

We glided along the lake's edge. The cliffs rose in walls of pale limestone where the lake had cut them. Kin slowed the canoe near a section of the rock that touched the water and pointed.

The wall was covered in handprints. Some red. Some black. Some so faded the stone had begun to take them back. They were not arranged, but rather overlapped where hands had pressed the rock at different turns of the calendar.

"When a soul dies, it flies back to this lake, and a spirit dog, its companion, guides it across the river to that wall, where it passes through into the world beneath the water," Kin explained.

We paddled on.

On a far point of the lake we tied up our canoes next to a giant hollow log. Boor handed me a stick.

"Hit this three times to tell the mountain we're coming," I was instructed.

I hit it. The sound rolled through the forest.

Boor began hacking a path through brush up the mountain.

He looked up after a while and said, "My machete is doing its work well today," with a smile of satisfaction.

Arcelia, a Mexican archaeologist who specialized in cliffs and caves, leaned over. "Everything is talked about like it is alive, with agency, even the machete," she said. "Watch."

Maybe it was what my old friend Tom Garrison had told me years ago, when I had first entered the Maya jungle and asked him to give me the "good stuff."

"k'uh," he said. "This gets a bit Star Wars, but it's the Maya description of the energy force that ran through all things. Soul-level stuff," he added, "during sacrifices or bloodletting ceremonies, they were offering k'uh back to the universe."

Hours later, at the top of the mountain, a wind came up out of a small hole near the peak, blowing Kin's long black hair back in the air. He smiled.

"See? The mountain breathes," he proudly observed in Lacandon.

Inside the mountain was a cave system.

"In Maya the goddess of caves is Ix Chel," Arcelia told me with a smile as we pulled on harnesses and helmets. "The moon is the midwife. The one who brings things into being from the dark."

She rappelled into the cave first. I came down on a different line through razor-sharp limestone into a chamber whose ceiling was a forest of stalactites. The same old wine cellar smell as Pakal's tomb, with the jungle pressing in above. "Look there, check around the stalactites, they have meaning," she said through the crackle of a walkie-talkie.

Suddenly, my headlamp caught a fragment of pottery on the floor.

Arcelia held it under her light. "Pre-classic, earliest Maya. You can tell from the waxy red finish here," she said, running her fingers along the clay. "Someone came down here three thousand years ago with an offering."

I held the fragment for a moment, turned it in the beam, and after documenting it we put it back exactly where it had been.

---

More than a decade had passed since I had first walked into a Maya jungle with a LiDAR map, when we had stumbled over Tikal's giant defense wall in the contours of the ground. So many of those searches had been about the Maya collapse. This one was after a beginning.

Joe ran the LiDAR processing over Noh k'uh and the forest surrounding the lake system, and like magic once again he stripped the canopy away. The site Joel and Santiago had been walking indeed turned out to be one corner of something much larger, a settlement of households spread across the basin, each one organized around its own small ceremonial space, and observatories that pointed toward the breathing mountain.

Beneath the floors of those homes, the previous seasons' digs had pulled out small offerings: jade hammers, ceramic vessels, shell beads. They had been placed in homes, Joel said, to renew the k'uh of the household. Noh k'uh, Place of k'uh.

Other enigmas appeared in the data. A pyramid with three sides instead of four. "Probably something they tested out early and didn't stick," Joel said with a shrug. "Like a fashion." We had seen a square hole carved into a cliff that looked across the lake to nothing we could see. What did it all mean?

Joel lit his cigar on the porch, its aroma wafting in. Next to the hum of the generator, he sketched in my journal the details of the Maya cosmovision Steve had first told me about long ago. A wheel of four colors at four directions (red east, black west, white north, yellow south), with the Axis Mundi at the center, binding the heavens, the earth, and the underworld. The cormorant, the spirit bird, traversed all three.

"For the Maya, time itself was not a line like ours, and not a circle like a clock. It was a spiral. Cycles within cycles. Each turn rhymed with the one before but did not repeat it. Events return, and evolve," he said as he drew.

He laid the sketch over the satellite map of the lake. "Look," he said. "The whole lake is laid out like the map of the cycles. The breathing mountain is the center. Everything else is sitting in the four directions. It's an observatory at the scale of landscape."

The LiDAR had pulled more than hidden buildings out of that jungle. It had revealed a philosophy written into the land itself, birth, growth, death, renewal, rebirth, the whole of it turning in a single never-ending spiral. I found myself holding that spiral up against my own life.

Yet while there was a clear alignment of the buildings that emerged in the LiDAR scans, it didn't seem to have an obvious compass heading. "We should find what it was observing," he said.

The team gathered around the small screen to watch simulations of the sun's arc across the landscape at different times of year. Solstices, equinoxes. We cycled through a number of obvious dates, then one simulation hit, and everything aligned. "August 13th! The day of the sun's zenith here. The Maya New Year, and the start of the Maya Long Count," Joel exclaimed with a look of awe.

The beginning.

---

On the last evening the team paddled out of sight to film back at my canoe from the horizon. I floated along in the middle of the lake. The day's warm air rose into fog as the light faded. Bats flew overhead. Cormorants crisscrossed the shoreline, hunting fish they couldn't see.

A giant full moon came up over the ridge line and cast the entire lake in blue light. The canoe rocked gently on the still mirror. I was afraid to move, afraid to disturb the peace.

I was alone now, in what the Lacandon h-men had described to me as a plane between the living and the dead. I felt the energy of everything.

I had built my whole career around the search for what was on the other side of a metaphorical door. It had been in every place I had been, both out in the world and at home. At Cambridge I had asked what the source of imagination was. Whether all the doors opened onto the same room. Maybe what I had been looking for had been right in front of me.

I was lost in this thought when crew chatter on the radio broke the silence. I turned it off, and sat silently on the mirrored plane as the sound of birds, insects, and life flowed. I was at the center of the geographical Axis Mundi that Joel had drawn in my journal, and under the enormity of the full moon and infinite stars above.

---

Gio handed me the book that night. We were sitting on a log at the edge of the lake after dinner. She pulled it from her pack.

"This has been an important book to me," she said. "You should read it."

*The Tibetan Book of Living and Dying.*

I turned it over in my hands without opening it. Days before, Kin had told me how a soul crosses into the lake returning into the k'uh from which it came. Here was a book from the far side of the world, another culture that had methodically studied its own version of that journey.

I thanked her. We had all grown close in that jungle. I put the book in my bag.

It felt strange to even carry it, and to be honest I didn't want it. I had written that letter to my kids, but I didn't want to go beyond that. The hidden truth was that I was afraid, like all of us.

The thing I'd learned years ago in the wake of my own wrecked body was that imagination was a door and a mind could actually make reality. Was it true? Or was it something I'd only dreamed up to survive? After all these years of searching through all these cultures, I had circled the world and returned to the same Maya jungle I had first stepped into after amputation, and had found the answer in many forms.

The next morning I flew home.

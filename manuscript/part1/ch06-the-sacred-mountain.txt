CHAPTER 6
The Sacred Mountain

"Guys, we're fucked," I said.

Everyone stared at me in the cramped Ulaanbaatar hotel room, jet-lagged and bewildered, as I explained that the entire expedition was falling apart before it had begun.

This was not the plan.

Six months earlier, when the grant came through, doubt was replaced with elation. The funds were enough to barely cover the cost of plane tickets, but because National Geographic had backed us, the yellow rectangle made us legitimate. Bira and Ishdorj had agreed to champion the plan and take on local logistics and full permitting as equal project partners. July was the only weather window, one month a year when the high passes cleared. From the start, the roadmap taped to my hatchback had mapped the search as a multi-year campaign, each summer unlocking the next phase. We had six months to prepare for the first.

I illegally stitched the yellow Nat Geo rectangle onto gifted North Face jackets, the "dress for the job you want" approach. Our team workouts intensified like we were training for a big climb. Fred, the archaeology guru from Nat Geo, would provide adult supervision. Everyone was working for free. Nobody cared. We had jackets. With a new sense of confidence I was raising money and in-kind support from wherever I could. I had never organized anything on this scale. But Ishdorj was responding to the emails with assurances that he could get all the horses and gear we would need, so we just kept moving forward. I rushed to raise more cash but when it became clear our logistics budget was going to be as bootstrapped as the rest of the dream, the correspondence went silent. But it was too late.

With only weeks to go, no refunds within reach, the choice was to scrap it all or take the leap and simply show up. It was now or never. I boarded my flight, messaged Ishdorj one more time that we were on our way as had been planned, and hoped.

Crossing the Pacific, I finally got an answer, disaster. "Your budget is unrealistic," he wrote, "and insulting to the gravitas of what we are undertaking."

Everyone was already in the air, coming from various locations. How did I let it get here?

When I landed I called Kara from the airport. "Just do what you always do," she said. "Pivot, and find another way forward."

That was how I found myself in the hotel room, explaining to a group of people who had given up their summers, their jobs, and their savings on my word, that there was no headquarters, no vehicles, no plan, and no local support. The imposter syndrome I'd been fighting flooded in. I felt sick. Furthermore, Amaraa was filming a movie in the far west of the country and wasn't there. He was my Mongolian sworn blood brother, and everything felt weaker in his absence.

Pivot.

After the initial shock, the team split into pairs. Shay and Nate found a cramped apartment, beds and sleeping bags laid end to end, a comically ornate throne of a Russian artisan toilet. The others scouted food and got the drone through customs. Bibi and Baika told me who to call, and I started chasing every meeting I could get.

Fred landed the next day. When I brought him up to speed, he was surprisingly unfazed. As a seasoned archaeologist, he was used to things going sideways and rolled up his sleeves without complaint.

Slowly we started over from the beginning, meeting with ministers and academics. I'd bring the binder, Fred would hand out Nat Geo pins, and we'd try to charm our way forward. The first few were catastrophes. At the Institute of Archaeology, the director stumbled in, slurred a long speech about how we were outsiders with a shit budget, threw his cup of tea on the ground, and kicked us out.

We licked our wounds at an Irish pub near the apartment and refocused our approach. "Judo moves," I said. Anything negative, we'd deflect with a positive.

Finally came the meeting that mattered, Ishdorj and Bira. We sat in Ishdorj's office surrounded by stacks of Mongolian history books. Ishdorj was stout and guarded, weighing each thing we said from behind narrowed eyes, deferring quietly to Bira, whom he served as something like a lieutenant. I could feel them both sizing us up. It wasn't about the bureaucracy or the money really. It was about whether they believed we deserved to be there. Maybe whether the search itself was for the right reason.

I leveled with them. "We might not have a ton of money, but we are here in service of the story of Genghis Khan. We are a team of engineers with PhDs who volunteered to be here, we have remote sensing and geophysics tools to contribute that can help reveal this story in a respectful non-invasive way right now. And we have National Geographic behind us to help lift his story. This is our chance. We should take it."

Silence. An elderly Bira looked at Ishdorj and nodded. Then Ishdorj said, "Okay. I want to meet your whole team. Bring them tomorrow."

The next morning I made everyone put on the knockoff Nat Geo jackets and button-up shirts.

Since I had first shown up, Ulaanbaatar had been changing rapidly. Next to the giant statue of Genghis Khan with his son Ögedei and grandson Kublai Khan seated beside him in Sükhbaatar Square, glass towers were rising, a Louis Vuitton at the base of one. We walked past the contrast toward Ishdorj's office like schoolboys facing the principal.

Bira had once told me he had stood up to anti-China-fueled complaints that Kublai should not have been included in the Square because his rule had refocused the empire around what is now China. "It's stupid," he said, "he is part of our story." It had been a reminder of the tensions that surrounded me, as it had been on the Silk Road.

As we sat in a line, Ishdorj looked over each of us. Then pointed at Kostas. "Do you know how to ride a horse?" Kostas nodded. He went down the line. Everyone said yes.

Then he lowered his voice. "Where this is, the Ikh Khorig, there are wolves and bears. Avalanches and flash floods. Do you understand?" Everyone nodded.

"I need three days to organize. Give me all your money."

We handed over everything we had left, wads of cash we had stuffed in our socks on the flights in. We saved barely enough for rent, groceries, and one round of celebratory beers. Back in the square, I turned to the team.

"Does anyone actually know how to ride a horse?"

An hour later we were in a field near the airport where a nomad let us learn on his horses for a small fee out of the little we had left, and one by one my team of engineers and computer scientists climbed onto wooden Mongolian saddles for the first time in their lives. Shay instantly seemed to get it. Kostas held on through sheer willpower. Charles, the geologist, looked like a giant riding a children's mechanical bull at a county fair. The nomad watched with a blank expression punctuated by bursts of laughter.

Then finally the day came. After a week of limbo, months of planning, and all the dreaming we were actually going to go.

It was a cold morning when we emerged from our apartment. Massive Russian trucks rumbled into the square one after another, followed by three little lifted minibuses. Ishdorj had come through.

Kids peered through the windows of the apartment blocks looking down onto trucks loaded with generators, carpets, planks, canvas, bags of flour, and massive amounts of ketchup. With only two dollars per person per day our food options would be limited. We bought goats on the way out of town, and we would slaughter one each week.

Ishdorj, who until this moment had seemed like a bureaucrat, transformed into a different man entirely. Riding boots, a long thin cigarette dangling from his lips, issuing commands with a quiet authority that made you want to follow him anywhere.

"Ahhhh Tsaaa tsaaa tsaaa," he would say when someone from the crew gave him an update on the radio.

"In Mongolia, it's not about the truck," he said, as an old man with three missing fingers rebuilt one of the vehicles for the third time. "It's about the mechanic."

There were twenty of us in total. A half dozen Americans, about twice as many Mongolians. Only a couple spoke English. But as we traveled together the barriers fell away quickly. The young horsemen grew close to Shay, Nate, and the crew. The grizzled mechanics with missing digits were always working. Ishdorj found common ground with Fred and me.

Soviet apartment blocks gave way to sprawling street markets, markets to a sea of tightly packed ger encampments, and then open grassland.

We stopped at Ishdorj's family camp after a long day's drive to gather horses. I had met him as a historian, here he was a leader of his camp. He mounted a horse effortlessly and galloped across the plains, standing tall in the stirrups, his shoulders barely moving as the horse glided under him. I watched and remembered what Amaraa had told me about Genghis Khan's tactical advantage, an ability to ride and shoot accurately at the same time with this technique. The next day the horses were loaded onto a large flatbed and we continued north toward the Ikh Khorig.

At the big wooden gate to this mysterious Forbidden Zone, the guards met us. Lightning cut across the sky as we set up camp, knowing this was going to take a while. Inside the small guard house were the descendants of the Darkhads, hereditary protectors whose order to guard this threshold had originated with Genghis Khan himself. They were stationed here now at the edge of the modern Biopreserve that surrounded the ancient Ikh Khorig, potentially as a convenient, innocuous buffer to maintain an ancient secret map. We had come as a Mongolian and international expedition with clear intentions of surveying.

Cigarettes and tea, and an endless string of calls on the satellite phone to the various contacts and Ministries who had given us permits in the capital. This went on for a long time. "This is our work, we are historians and this is our job," Ishdorj would turn and say to me assuredly.

Late in the night he came back from the guard house and sat down beside me. "It's our job to know our history," he repeated. "I told them about the non-invasive technology, that we are here only to look with respect."

We had the papers from the capital. But these were the old guards, and the papers were not the thing that would decide it. They deliberated a long time among themselves.

I lay in my cot that night, contemplating the centuries of lore surrounding this place, with the likes of Marco Polo having told tales of it but never entering. The winter before I had been stopped by snow at the edge of it. Now the snow was gone and we were equipped to see what was actually there.

In the morning the gate lifted. No ceremony, just the sound of an old hinge giving way.

At dawn we entered the dense pine forest of rivers and bogs. The Kherlen wove its way through the mountain range, swollen now with rainwater that made every crossing a gamble. Ishdorj slung a Kalashnikov across his back, a Soviet-era rifle. "For the beasts and bandits," he told me. Then he took his horse and rode ahead to scout the next crossing. A truck nearly slid into a gorge; the driver held it as we winched it back.

We crossed ridge after ridge to the last big river, with the range I had seen from beyond the snow now looming on the far bank. The trucks went first, the valley filled with the roar of engines as the old military behemoths lurched up the opposite side with water pouring from every seam. Then the horses. I'd jumped at the chance to cross the way Genghis Khan might once have crossed here, but with each step I could feel my horse searching for purchase on slippery rocks. One wrong move and we'd both be in the torrent. I reached the other side grateful to be alive. Last came the 4x4 vans. Charles's stalled mid-river, engine flooded. "We're taking on a lot of water," he called over the radio, while frantically moving equipment onto chairs. Moments later he too was pulled free with the winch.

The rain poured down and the river swelled behind us. There was no turning back now.

Then a mountain rose into view, so powerful it seemed like the other peaks bowed to it.

We made camp in a grassy opening at its base, and for once everyone exhaled. But I couldn't wait. We had come so far and were now so close to the mountain itself. While the crew assembled the gers, I beckoned to Kostas, Ishdorj, and a few others. We started riding up, the fog of the river creating a cold dew in the air. Somewhere on that mountain we passed a shrine covered in blue prayer flags, a large cauldron surrounded by a few fragments of cups, animal bones, and what I assumed were other shamanic offerings.

Later, on a plateau that remains unnamed, we came into a stand of pines the wind had brought down. I hopped off my horse and began looking around. Then Charles motioned me over from behind the exposed roots of a fallen pine.

"Albert, over here." His voice was low.

He slid his hand out from behind the root. Some kind of ceramic circular disk with a lion's face. I took it and flipped it over. On the back, the fingerprints of whoever had made it were pressed into the clay. I put my thumb against the impression and the sensation of feeling someone's fingerprint through hundreds of years of time hit the back of my neck like electricity.

Embedded in the roots around us: bricks, roof tiles, fragments of a world buried by time and torn back open by the mountain's storms.

Back in San Diego between expeditions, the problem of scale nagged at us. We had a whole mountain range to search and a single July window each year to do it in, constrained by both budget and weather. Even with the satellite imagery, how could we get through all that data? Every pixel could be an anomaly and we had no idea what we were looking for to begin with. Then one afternoon in a park with Luke and Derek, we watched a flock of birds wheel and ripple overhead, thousands of them moving as one mind, no leader, just each bird reacting to the ones beside it. At that same moment a cloud in the shape of a giant marshmallow man hung over the city, and we found ourselves wondering how many other people across town were looking up, and how many of them were perceiving the same thing as we were.

The two thoughts collided into an idea. What if thousands of human eyes could work the way that flock did? Each person scanning a small patch of our satellite imagery would see almost nothing alone. But where many of them independently flagged the same spot, a real pattern would rise out of the noise, one no single pair of eyes could ever find. A mind built of many minds. Luke started coding that night, a kind of video game where thousands of strangers could help steer us, their clicks from space nudging the search like hands on a giant Ouija board.

When the platform turned on, National Geographic promoted it and tens of thousands of volunteers began combing our satellite images pixel by pixel, tagging anything unusual. Their dots began appearing on the map. Then more. Then millions, accumulating in real time like a sky filling with stars. The agreements surfaced just as we'd imagined: clusters around rocks and yurts, lines tracing the nomads' dirt tracks, and then strange features. Large circular mounds, which Fred identified as Bronze age burials. Then single focal point where, one blue pixel in a vast stretch of wilderness. The summit.

That was the rhythm of those years. Each expedition sent us home with a new problem, and each winter we came back with a new way to see. The team largely stayed intact as everyone somehow found a way to let their other responsibilities know that every summer they needed to take leave for a quest to find Genghis Khan's tomb.

But that first summer, another summit was rising at home. Kara was pregnant.

We'd been together less than a year, barely knew each other, barely knew ourselves. But we were in love and ecstatic. My expedition planning began to overlap with birthing classes and hunts for used bassinets, the two summits growing side by side. The July window fell a month before her due date. Not ideal. But Kara encouraged me to go, as she always did, and her younger brother jumped in without hesitation to help while I was gone. I was grateful to both of them.

I left tormented anyway. The thought of missing my child's arrival sat in the back of my mind as I packed.

A week into the second year's expedition and one of the horsemen developed a red, swollen eye. When we looked under his eyelid, maggots. A fly had laid eggs there while he slept. Ishdorj, completely unfazed, stuck a slab of bloody goat meat against the man's face and waited. One by one, seven maggots crawled out of his eye and into the raw flesh. A bloody goat steak was our field medic.

Amaraa was with us this time, and everything felt better for it. He had shaved his head except for a patch of hair on his forehead as the ancients had, ready to enter the sacred landscape of his ancestors.

We would split into teams and hike the surrounding peaks with the map made from pixels that had been pored over by countless numbers of volunteer eyeballs of "armchair explorers," as *Wired* reported it. Ishdorj handed me the Kalashnikov. "If you're in trouble, shoot the gun in the air and we'll know where you are."

After three days of searching neighboring ridgelines, radioing across from parallel routes, we found nothing but wilderness and one abandoned Soviet geological marker. Whatever had happened on this particular mountain wasn't geological. It was unique. Someone had shaped it centuries ago, and that sort of transformation must have taken monumental effort.

It was time to climb to the top of the Sacred Mountain. The ascent was nothing like the Sierra Nevadas. This was older terrain, mountains worn by time, with rugged mesas rising from dense forest. As we climbed, the trees thinned until they disappeared, leaving us fully exposed. The rain clouds held their position in the distance and we pressed on to the summit.

The sun broke through as we reached the summit, golden and warm. Before us stood the largest shamanic shrine I had ever seen. Weathered blue prayer flags arranged around a massive soul banner whose horsehair tassels danced in the wind, similar to the one I had seen in the great hall of Mongolia's capital. The blue pixel had been this. In Genghis Khan's time, these Suldes were flown to catch the breath of the sky, the force they called *hiimori*, the vital energy source that gave its owner strength and wisdom. Hundreds of stone cairns were scattered across the top of the Eye like silent sentinels. I stood there and felt the wind.

Amaraa moved to the edge of the summit and quietly bowed his head to the four directions of the compass, then draped the waistbelt of his deel across his neck and raised his hands to Tengri, the Eternal Blue Sky. *The Secret History of the Mongols* describes this exact image on this very peak, Temüjin with his belt on his neck, kneeling nine times toward the sun, asking for guidance. Watching Amaraa in that gesture, eight hundred years compressed to nothing.

I stood behind him and felt not triumph, but awe. My presence in that moment felt almost absurd.

Genghis Khan had prayed to the same sky Amaraa was praying to now, and from that prayer he had changed the course of history.

Empires fell at his feet. The Silk Road opened, caravans stitched continents together, paper and printing moved west, gunpowder and compasses altered war and navigation. And in the unprecedented era of cultural exchange that came in the wake of his rule, ancient manuscripts that had been forgotten in libraries traversed Eurasia for the first time in thousands of years, a fact I sometimes argue could suggest Genghis Khan helped launch the Renaissance itself with the rediscovery of the Greek philosophies flowing along with the rest. From the Sea of Japan to Eastern Europe the world would change fundamentally, all of it originating from whatever happened on this peak. And here I was, a kid from California with a borrowed GPS and a knockoff jacket, standing in the shadow of this history with the memory of a Blue Spot that called me here, to witness this ancient scene unfold.

Back at camp, I lay awake for hours in the ger listening to the howl of distant wolves and the wind over the faint sound of the generator outside. In the Mongolian origin story, the world began when a blue wolf descended from the sky to this sacred mountain to meet a white deer born from the earth, a union of strength and purity, sky and land, the masculine and feminine, and from that union came the first clans of the steppe. With the sounds of the forest all around, the story was still alive in the air itself.

The next day, we set a camera on a timer. As I posed with my crew before the vast expanse of the Khentii range, I was filled with a gratitude I couldn't speak. Every friendship, every skill, all those years of hiking, climbing, and studying together, it had gotten me here. That night the satellite phone crackled with a broken message from Kara. The only words I could make out were "two centimeters" and "bedrest" and "it's close." Our baby was coming early.

We made our way out of the mountains as a group, then Ishdorj sent me with his best driver while the rest remained to complete the survey. The night before I left, we bought a cow from a nearby camp for a farewell feast. We led it away from the herd to be slaughtered, and when it was done, the remaining cows stampeded toward the blood-stained grass. They circled, stamped, and stood close, honoring the absence of one of their own.

I thought of those cows on the flight home, the way they stood close to what they could feel but no longer see.

Three days later, covered in dirt and grass from traveling by horse and truck and plane with no time for a shower, I opened the door to our apartment and there was Kara, smiling on the couch, feet up, smoothie in hand. "Welcome home honey, virgin daiquiri?" she said, pointing at it with a big smile. I had made it home just in time.

That night a drunken frat party raged next door. Four days later we moved to a quiet street surrounded by families, and the following morning Kara went into labor. I drove to the hospital like I was galloping across the plains.

One week before I had been on a horse on top of Mongolia's sacred mountain. Now I was in a beige room with a heart rate monitor and ice chips. Kara breathed the way she had been taught, then breathed in a way nobody teaches. The doctors came and went. 

Then a cry. His cry. Then he was in my hands. His own breath in his own chest.

He was the most beautiful thing I had ever seen. We named him Charles, in part inspired by the friend who had pulled the lion faced medallion out of the roots of a tree that had fallen on the sacred mountain.[

I looked down, there it was. The blueish birthmark on his back.

Kara had created a miracle, and she had done it with grace. He was impossibly small, his entire body fit between the palm of my hand and the bend of my elbow. I'd learned how to swaddle in all the childbirth classes, but holding him that first time everything felt so delicate I was terrified to do anything at all. I just sat there with my baby in my arms.

He looked up at me and opened his eyes, and in them I saw the entire universe. My life's purpose shrank to the scale of this tiny person. I understood in an instant something about fatherhood that had only been a cliché to me until that point. My world was growing in that room.

I knew I had to end the silence with my own father. Whatever had broken between us, the fight, the Chopin, the ten years of not speaking, I couldn't carry it into my son's life. Charlie would not grow up without a grandfather because I was trapped in something that happened when I was seventeen. Among the visitors, he came after my mother had gone. It was awkward and halting and insufficient, but it was a start.

My son had been born.

When we left for the final expedition, the tension was different. We'd found artifacts dating to the time of Genghis Khan's death. Our multi-antenna radar hinted at voids beneath rectangular structures, which we built into 3D virtual models. But again those were just pixels. We were close but the closer we got, the more I felt the weight of what that meant.

The ground on the plateau crunched underfoot in a way that didn't sound like earth. We debated lifting the top layer of moss. Not an excavation, just peel a little patch up like a carpet and roll it back when we were done. Underneath we could feel bricks laid in patterns beneath our feet. A newly fallen root had revealed celadon pottery, which Fred explained was a valuable ceramic not made anywhere nearby. Just one piece could reveal a pilgrimage from eight hundred years ago. These were offerings. We had agreed the first year that aside from a minimal set of dating samples, everything else would be photographed, cataloged, and returned precisely to where it had been found. But the temptations were mounting to blur the boundaries of non-invasiveness.

Then a low rumble emerged in the distance. In three years we had never seen another person. Now a flatbed Russian 66 came lurching into our valley on its huge wheels, a group of men standing in the bed around a large cylinder, guarding it. It wasn't until they began to unload that I realized the cylinder was several large octagonal drums stacked together. That was when it became clear: they were shamans, come to worship the mountain. Ishdorj greeted them and came back to me.

"Tonight, you and I sit with the head shaman. He asked who was the leader here, and he summoned you to judge your soul."

"Clean your face," Ishdorj said. "Clean your body. We will gather good wood to offer to their fire."

I remembered what Bibi had taught me about souls being seen in our smells. It was three weeks in, my soul wasn't smelling its best.

As the sky turned amber and mist began to rise over our camp, I walked the long path to the shaman's ger, which they had constructed over the course of the day. Ishdorj and I entered with the cautious reverence one would have entering the Vatican, carrying offerings of wood. The candles and open fire within filled the air with a smoke so dense it stung my eyes to tears and made me cough. Through the haze I could just make out the details of the shrines lining the perimeter: seven tables, each adorned with anthropomorphic figurines, headdresses, and ceremonial tools. Larger figures I could barely make out were sitting in various corners of the ger.

We were led outside to a clearing at the base of the mountain where another shrine of prayer flags had been constructed. Ishdorj and I were told to kneel before a fire. Between us and the shrine sat a pile of rocks holding a bow wrapped in blue silk, the blue of Tengri's eternal sky, pointed directly at the summit.

Nine cups of horse milk thrown into the sky, then nine tiny cups of vodka also sent to the sky. A chant in a tongue older than modern Mongolian, part ancient language and part something no one could fully translate. And then began the drumming, so deep I couldn't tell where it ended and my heartbeat began.

Boom. Boom. Boom.

Ishdorj and I were frozen, heads in the grass, not daring to brush the mosquitos from our foreheads. The drum beat faster. The shaman rose and spun, slamming his hammer, colored cords whipping through the air and striking bells, the energy building until something felt ready to rupture.

Then silence. He fell to the ground. Jerking forward like a crow, grunting in bursts of three.

Blackened cloths draped over his head and hid his face. Beside him sat a silver bowl and a long pipe. He sipped from the bowl. Drew on the pipe. Smoke curled up through the cloths and disappeared. Then he twisted to one side, body angled the way a crow tilts toward a sound, and began to talk.

I wrote in my journal that night. *"Unch, unch, unch." His head twitched again and he spoke. His voice was low and guttural and his tone changed between soft and aggressive spurts.*  

I tried to explain what our purpose was there. The satellites we had used, with many eyes helping search. The magnetometers and ground penetrating radars that we were using to look without digging. 

Ishdorj translated for me to the shaman's attendant, and the attendant translated into a proto-Mongolian tongue I would never know.  Nothing I had said had likely made any sense whatsoever.

Beneath a veil of black cloth tassels hanging over his face, and a feathered headband painted with two giant eyes, his head jerked back and forth. He sipped again from the silver bowl and drew a deep drag from the long pipe his attendant held. His body twisted the other direction, listening from beneath the cloths. He was speaking now not as himself but as something within him, in a voice only the attendant, after years at his side, could understand. The response began its return through the ridiculous chain of translations back to me.

He had asked me to bow my head. I pressed my forehead into the cold dewy grass. His hand, cold and heavy, on the back of my skull as I trembled. He didn't care about the technologies. He had wanted to know one thing. What were my intentions?

I answered in pieces, each one traveling down the chain and back before I could offer the next. "We came to find the Great Khan's tomb to show the world it's here in its sacred place." A pause. 

Then the more subtle truth. 

"My grandfather told me we might come from here, from the North." My voice cracked into the grass. "I think I am really here to find the story of Genghis Khan," 

I wanted to know what could exist within... me. The idea history has heroes from every culture and their stories had all not survived.  I had spent my childhood wondering who I was, trying to see myself in the hero's of the past, and hand never had anyone to celebrate that looked like me.  Who was my hero. 

Genghis Khan had only been described as a barbarian, when what I had felt all those years was the resonance of something so much more.  

An hour or more passed, as we spoke across a game of telephone that linked centuries and he began to tell me things of my lineage he saw in fragments, "Juchid, Northern Khentii Aimag." This was the names and location of a tribe of the steppe.

In the end he told me that he would tell my ancestors I was okay, and that I had chosen my way "which is a good path."

"Remind the world of the Great Khan and the power of the mountain, it's why you were drawn here. But respect the old ways," Ishdorj translated. And then we were dismissed.

The drums echoed through the camp all night, entering my dreams and weaving into my being.

The next morning I woke to the sound of the generator and the smell of boiling goat again. I walked into the clearing where the ashes of the shaman's fire still smoldered. Ishdorj was there, standing quietly with a cup of tea.

"You know, during the old time, we could not say his name," he said in a thick Mongolian accent. "They tried to kill us. They banned the script. They burned the books. Many died for it." He looked up at the mountain. "This is why I am here. For Mongolia. For Mongolians. To remember our origins." A look of purpose glowed from within him.

A few days later we placed every artifact back where we'd found it other than a few key horse bones and tiles for materials analysis and dating. Our survey lines were taken down, and we prepared to leave the mountain as we'd found it. When the last goat became "goat pockets," a Hot Pocket but made in the rain with week-old goat meat, it was time to go. 

Before we left the shaman came and blessed everyone on the team. We loaded our equipment onto the trucks, mounted our horses, and rode away from the sacred mountain for the last time. We crossed the final river, and looking back over my shoulder one last time it felt like I had passed through a threshold.

Hours later we emerged into grasslands. That night, in the midst of a massive hailstorm, we built our gers in six inches of standing water and huddled on cots, soaking and cold. By morning the steppe had drunk the flood clean. The plains shone like new. I walked out under what felt like an endless sky across the sea of green grass. With every step a thousand crickets fanned away under the shadow of my foot, like a rolling living carpet. The crew gathered for wrestling matches and a horse race. It was Naadam season, the annual nomadic time of celebration.

Mongolian horsemen and computer scientists all together, no longer separated. We had become family over three years. The horses were lined up. Ishdorj raised his hand and dropped it. A great cloud of dust bloomed as we bolted forward. The noise faded the way it had years before when I'd wrestled the nomad in Amaraa's uncle's camp. Everything narrowed to hooves and breath. Mongolians and the international team we had become, historians and engineers, the grizzled horsemen and PhDs who couldn't ride a few years earlier, all of us galloping together like we'd been doing it our whole lives.

A rider ahead hit a gopher hole. His horse rolled, launching him into the air. I pulled my reins and leapt, barely missing him as his horse slammed into mine. I teetered. Nearly fell. Righted myself. A glance back. A rider behind waved me on.

"Go! Go!"

We raced to the end of the line. The wind against our face, the dust blooming behind, the crew yelling, the hooves shaking the earth.

When it was over I didn't quite know how to feel. We were at the end of the plan that had been taped to the window of my hatchback three years before, and we would not be coming back. It was the end of an incredible journey that had started in my grandfather's words and was finishing in celebration of what had grown from nothing more than community and belief. We had found artifacts of a mysterious temple in tree roots that had presented themselves to us in a storm. They had come from afar to that mountain in the time of Genghis Khan's death. But we were not going to dig, so that was it. Yet we had also stood on the peak where the Mongol Empire was born and felt the hiimori fill the soul banner that flew there now, and we had just been judged by a shaman under a sky full of stars and passed.

Jack Weatherford, whose book had first sparked my imagination, once said to me in a phone call, "if you want to find Genghis Khan, you only need to look into the eyes of any nomad." After years of searching, I finally understood what he meant. I had been working so hard, almost belligerently solving problem after problem with technologies to look further, forgetting my original intention for coming to Mongolia in the first place years before. The answer had been in front of me, not hidden in some tomb but rather in the thunder of that race, in the hearts of my Mongolian family, in the soul banner still flying on the peak and the traditions that still served it. And it was in me.

The seed of this search had been a blue spot on my own back. It was on my son's now.

CHAPTER 3
Shifting Grounds

Why would a twenty-five-year-old me fly to the other side of the world on the word of my mother's father? To understand that, it's in the ground I grew up on.

Unfortunately, much of my own early childhood is gone. Memory gaps, probably from the surgeries and the anesthesia. And what came before me I know only secondhand, in pieces. But one thing seems clear. It was never as solid as it looked.

My mother was an actor and a musician who showed me how to feel what could not be measured. My father was an astrophysicist who looked at the cosmos and saw patterns that explained where everything came from.

I was raised in a house of art and science. I came from both. Neither could tell me where I belonged. Our lives happened in chapters, each one wherever my father's work took us next. When people ask me where I grew up, I tell them kind of everywhere.

Santa Cruz, California, 1989. I was eight years old in the city of my birth. We were coming home from Peace Choir rehearsal, a pack of tie-dye-shirted kids who sang anti-proliferation atomic countdown songs in front of grocery stores. That was the first time the earth literally changed under our feet.

My mother was driving the hand-me-down Caprice Classic we called "the Boat." Riding in it felt like bobbing at sea. My sister Jane sat up front in her sideways ponytail and neon pink Esprit sweater. I was in back staring at dry lawns and rows of stucco houses in the afternoon light. We passed Long's Drugstore with its posters of surfers riding huge waves, then turned toward our street on the edge of the canyon.

The car wobbled like we'd hit speed bumps. I saw people running out of their houses but didn't understand why. The Boat's suspension had swallowed most of the shaking.

We pulled up to find my older brother Ed frozen in the driveway. He was usually the one with all the answers, two years older, top-ranked in the state for math, the family genius. But right then he looked scared.

"You didn't feel it!?"

He'd been watching the World Series when the Loma Prieta earthquake erupted ten miles away, 6.9 on the Richter scale. Living in the Bay Area, we'd been warned for years about the pending "Big One." It gave me nightmares, scenes of being crushed or trapped in an elevator haunting my imagination. But now it had actually happened and I'd missed it. In a strange way, I felt robbed of the chance to finally face something I'd dreaded for so long.

Peering in, the house looked like a snow globe someone had shaken and set upside down. Books and debris scattered across the floor, cracks spidering along the walls. The only thing still resolute in its place was my mother's piano, firmly planted in the center of it all. She stopped us from entering.

Growing up in Hong Kong, my mother had been an actress in the same film studio as Bruce Lee, and maybe that's what was helping her keep her composure in this emergency. Or maybe it was that she was conditioned to be resilient. Her childhood came in the wake of a world war, famine, and the dawn of the Cold War. She was the oldest of four sisters and a brother, all sharing a tiny flat in a giant residential high-rise, where my uncle raised chickens in the window. And even amidst a challenging world she had thrived. Old black-and-white photos that I had found of her on set made me realize she had been the Chinese version of a Hollywood teen movie star. Yet somehow, an unlikely love grabbed hold, and she bravely left that world behind to follow my father wherever his work led, Canada, then Boston, then Cambridge, before it finally set them down in this small California town to start a family. A life where she didn't speak the language, sewed most of her own clothes out of habit, scrimped every penny with extraordinary commitment, reinvented herself as a piano teacher, and somehow held our family together between English and citizenship classes at night, cooking for us all, and helping me or my two siblings with homework, as I mostly squirmed in my chair like an inchworm. She was a tough cookie.

Now she held us all back from our own front door.

Jane stood beside her, the oldest and naturally the most composed of us siblings. "Is our house broken?" she asked softly, mostly to herself. If Ed was the genius, Jane was the rock. But now even she looked frightened as aftershocks kept bubbling up from below, what was solid was no longer solid.

I, unlike my brother and sister, was the problem child, ironically nicknamed "xiao poo" (little poop) at birth after a volcanic bowel movement ended my first few days of terrorizing the family in an undiagnosed bout of extreme constipation. I was the youngest, always getting in trouble, and scared of the dark. And most days we would be bickering about something. But in the immediate aftermath of the tectonic event our sibling rivalries melted away and we were just one small family humbled by cosmic forces.

We waited in the driveway for my father to get home from the university, the roads congested in the state of emergency.

My dad spent his days covered in chalk dust, using math to imagine the creation of planets and the properties of dark matter. Looking back, it's a wild thing to do with a life. He loved us deeply, but his work often came first. My mother carried most of the parenting weight for the two of them, while he would come home late with his head still in the stars. But this night was different. He pulled up and we ran to him and held on, thankful we were all okay and finally together.

My father ran into our darkened home just long enough to grab some food and water, our old A-frame tent, and the little green camping stove that had followed us everywhere we moved. He came out of the wreckage bewildered at the damage but also marveling that the row of National Geographic magazines on the shelf had shifted into a perfect sinusoidal pattern from the quake's horizontal long wave energy. "Physics!" he exclaimed with a finger in the air, followed by my brother offering to tell us how plate tectonics worked. We set up camp in the backyard just like we had in Yosemite or Joshua Tree, only this time under the shadow of disaster.

The canyon that butted against our yard was where we played almost every day. Now it was unstable, my father warned, telling us to stay clear of its edges.

It had been our wonderland of hideouts for years, sand caves we would sit inside, scraping pigment from the walls and mixing it with water to make paint, then covering the stone with our own version of ancient cave drawings. Only days before, we had cut a track through the fennel and turned it into an audacious homemade bobsled run for a scrap wood sled we called the Little Dipper. Ed designed it. Jane supervised with the calm authority of someone who knew how she would explain to our mother that her youngest had been launched off a cliff.

Those days felt far away now. Through the backyard fences we were learning how bad it had been. Over sixty people had lost their lives across the Bay Area, in fallen buildings and collapsed bridges. Our coastal mountain range had been the epicenter.

As night fell, more aftershocks came. My mother cooked whatever had been grabbed from the fridge during my dad's sprint through the wreckage, stirring a bowl of noodles with bok choy and eggs between the shakes. I lay sandwiched between my brother and sister as the earth beneath us continued to rumble and shift like a giant's belly.

Then, somewhere out of the darkness, a distant neighbor grabbed a megaphone. He had clearly decided to drink all his beer before it got warm, and he started calling out ratings for each tremor like an Olympic judge.

Five point four! Four point three!

Other voices joined in from yard to yard across the canyon while shooting stars streaked overhead. As the earth heaved beneath us we clapped and called out scores. With nothing more than a choice of perspective, the disaster had become something we were doing together.

---

That earthquake is one of the few things I remember clearly from Santa Cruz. Outside of that, we were constantly living in a state of learning and adopting the traditions around us. I joined a little league baseball team, having never played catch in my life. We celebrated Thanksgiving dinners with a blend of turkey and mashed potatoes with Chinese food as our side, watching football games we had no idea how to comment on. But I guess this is what our country was founded upon, an experiment in coming together. It was not new to my family, something we had been at for generations. We had bled for it.

My father's side of the story I knew well. It was an extraordinary adventure of humble roots impacting the highest levels of geopolitics. My great grandfather had immigrated alone at fifteen and worked the salmon canneries and railroads of British Columbia. He somehow raised his status to become one of the first Chinese clergy in North America. Picture a black-and-white photo of an immigrant amongst all white peers, in a place he was not supposed to be. His son, my grandfather Paul T.K. Lin, gave an award-winning speech he titled "the Pacific Charter" as a young student at the height of World War II. The recognition for Paul's thoughts and voice earned him his way to Harvard and a PhD program under the same advisor as Henry Kissinger, who himself would later become the US Secretary of State.

My grandmother told me once that she remembered Kissinger coming to their home for dinner. I learned later, from books about my grandfather, that he had played a critical role in establishing the first back channels of communication between China's premier Zhou Enlai and President Nixon. Ping-pong diplomacy, they called it. It must have been an adventure. He'd spent the rest of his life building bridges between East and West at a time when few existed, and would later receive the Order of Canada, his country's highest honor, for that work, and for the stoic nature with which he continued to pursue global diplomacy through McCarthyism, when fear of communism and espionage made the very idea of it suspect.

But it cost them dearly. This was during the Cold War, when the internal mistrust amongst departments and within governments could destroy people in its belligerence. My father's older brother, the elder of two sons, was killed under mysterious circumstances made top secret by the FBI. My grandmother always suspected it was a pressure tactic, unjust and misinformed, aimed at my grandfather. It became a weight that lived as deep invisible suffering across generations. It's probably why my dad dove so deep into the stars. And it was in the subtle ways my siblings and I experienced our parents' mindset. So much was left unspoken. The idea of belonging was always confusing.

My mother's side of the story, I actually knew little about until later in life. As a child, the few stories she would tell were just as heavy: the Japanese Invasion of China, migrations, a family scattered apart under air sirens, and miraculously regathered in the wake of destruction. Unfortunately, we had little chance to know her side of the family, as they all lived on the other side of the planet. But it was also that our lives followed my father's science, and it rarely pointed toward Hong Kong. Either way, a gap had opened to her world. She had chosen the adventure, and it must have been lonely all the same. Maybe the piano was her answer, a language she could speak freely anywhere.

As a boy I would fall asleep on the carpet under the piano bench while her sonatas wafted through the air.

When I asked, she would do her best to teach me. It was not something she was willing to force like homework, but something she seemed to want me to arrive at on my own. I would follow her hands along the keys and try to repeat what I had seen from memory, doing my best to hit them in the right order, rushing through as if afraid of losing a train of thought.

She stopped me.

"Feel the notes," she said. "And the gaps between them."

She wanted me to feel the expression in a sound, the way a vibration in the air becomes something inside you. To her, the wooden box of strings and hammers was a tool of infinite expression. I was not skilled enough yet to fully understand what she meant, but when she played I could feel what couldn't be captured in words or equations.

"Music is universal," she used to tell me. Then my dad would jump in and say he "only played the keyboard!" while holding his computer keyboard in the air like a big dork.

With all of the brutality of shifting empires in our family history, my parents had given me the middle name *Yu-Min*, meaning "citizen of the universe." I think they hoped my life would transcend geopolitics, beyond a choice of one tribe over another. *Albert*, I was told, was for physics. But the byproduct of all of it was that roots were obscured from us. It maybe wasn't a conscious choice, but it had been settled before I was born.

---

My fondest childhood memories came from the camping trips, where nature seemed to bring us all into presence with each other. My parents took us into the Sierra Nevadas with my dad's graduate students, driving through giant pines to clearings where they set up telescopes and pointed them at the sky. I remember the distinct smell of the green tent that my brother and sister and I piled into, the trees so tall they made us feel like ants. We hiked along streams that ran off mountains, with giant natural granite waterslides to slip down into ice-fed lakes with hoots of joy.

The first time I saw Yosemite Valley was on one of those trips. I sat in the back seat staring at the tiny climbers on the walls of El Capitan and Half Dome, three thousand feet of sheer granite. *Superhumans,* I thought.

One particular night, after a bear had broken into one of our campmates' cars, my father asked me if I wanted to run to the trading post with him for replacement supplies. Being the youngest, time with him to myself was rare, so I jumped at the chance. We drove down that long stretch of road as the pines rose up on all sides, and we talked the way we almost never did, just the two of us. He told me he was proud that I'd hiked for miles without complaining. We talked about the waterfall and the swimming hole. Then I asked him why he loved the stars. It took a moment for him to respond. Maybe he was deciding how much detail to bring.

Then he leaned in and told me his thoughts on everything.

"Moments after the Big Bang..." he began.

"There was a release of massive amounts of matter and antimatter into the universe, in nearly equal amounts that should have entirely canceled each other out. But by only the tiniest of margins, for unknown reasons, a hair more matter survived than antimatter. Just a sliver on the scales of cosmic creation was the difference between nothing and all of it, the stars, the mountains, the trees outside our window, the origins of life, us."

I didn't understand the depth of it at the time. I was eight years old. But he had let me into the thing he loved most, his curiosity and wonder. I remembered his voice on the road unwinding through the pines, and the feeling of awe that he carried in his words.


---

By the time I was ten, my father's career had progressed and he was granted a sabbatical in Cambridge.

"We're going to where the apple fell on Newton's head!" he'd say, eyes gleaming.

We were all proud of him. It is not easy to make a living by conceptualizing the universe but he was showing us it could be done. "Follow your passion and let the money figure itself out!" he would say with hubris and a hint of surprise that it was actually working. My mother, without hesitation, said "let's go!" My parents had the heart of explorers. They were demanding, frugal to a fault, and not heavy on emotional vulnerability, but they made sure we saw the world.

The journey took our family across the Atlantic and suddenly we were living in a world of knights and ancient stone. I remember the first morning, frosted grass, cobblestones, the smell of damp air, buildings standing since before my home country existed. As my father walked ahead of us through a courtyard, I saw him differently than I ever had. He belonged here, part of a tradition trying to understand the fundamental nature of things.

I met knights of England who were not in shining armor with a sword but rather well-worn sweaters and a chalkboard. An air of cheeky childlike wonder existed amongst my dad's community, communicating more in puns than full sentences. His world included people like Stephen Hawking, whose wife at the time, Jane, was my sister's namesake, and whose voice machine I once begged to press. My mother would drag us to my dad's talks for support. Some percentage of the audience was always rapt, but there was also a fair share of snoring heads, bobbing with convulsive whips as they tried to stay upright in a chair. Regardless, he was standing at the podium, and sitting in his lectures I felt both admiration and an unconscious fear that I would never live up to the bar he had set.

Then there were the excursions wherever an invitation to lecture would be extended. Astrophysics seemed to exist above politics. The Soviet Union had just collapsed and my parents took us across the upheaval. Freshly burned buildings in Moscow, the rubble of the Berlin Wall, cathedrals that had outlived empires. We boarded a train to Leningrad, and by the time it pulled in the city had been renamed St. Petersburg. My passport filled with stamps from nations that no longer existed. Everywhere the world was being torn apart and rebuilt, and beneath it all were layers of something far older that remained.

In Greece my brother and I counted the topless sunbathers like idiots while my parents drove along the coastline and Jane sat stuck in the back seat with us, shaking her head in shame. Later that day we sat in the back row of the ancient theater at Epidaurus, hearing a dropped coin on the stage and marveling at the acoustics of the space that rang true more than two thousand years after its construction.

My mother marched us through the great museums of every city we landed in, sometimes overdoing it, one more gallery after our legs had given out. I complained the whole way. But the wonder got in anyway. Amongst those curated walls of artifacts, I was getting my first exposure to versions of humanity that had existed before me. At night, all five of us crammed into whatever room we could afford, jumping from bed to bed until our parents yelled at us to stop. As long as we were together, the shifting ground didn't matter.

When we returned to Cambridge, my brother and sister would be off with friends again and my weekends were spent with my mother.

One day she pushed open the heavy door of a village church and pulled me inside. She wasn't religious then. Spirituality was not something our family ever spoke of. But the place felt like a portal into a fairy tale. Set into the cold stone floor were life-sized engravings of medieval knights in full armor, swords at their hips, hands pressed together in prayer, lions curled at their feet. Some were seven hundred years old.

We knelt on the stone, taped down a sheet of paper, and rubbed sticks of dark wax across it in long even strokes. The image came up slowly. First the outline of the shield. Then the chainmail. Then the face of someone who had lived and died centuries before I was born, emerging through the paper.

Sprawled across an ancient English tomb, I was a ten-year-old kid from California with blood rooted in Asia, running my hands over the traces of a collective past whose charisma had transcended either inheritance.

We were time travelers.

---

I tried desperately to fit in. I played conkers, a game where you smashed horse chestnuts on dangling strings. I learned soccer, wore Axe body spray like the other boys, and even developed a convoluted English accent and sang old British songs. That wasn't going to get me anywhere. I'd made a best friend in a guy named Guy, until he told me a girl from our school couldn't like me "because of the color of my hair."

He said it casually.

That ended the friendship, but the subconscious wound would follow me from school to school, country to country, eventually pointing me toward something older and deeper.

Seeing that I was struggling to fit in, my parents enrolled me in a Scout troop, and in those early days the draw to the wilderness became my closest companion. I started learning the basic skills of first aid, camping, backpacking.

---

Then we moved to Palo Alto a few years later, and suddenly I was a teenager. One day, cobblestones. The next, Silicon Valley, with the internet being born in the garages of my classmates. Everything felt equally full of possibility and at the same time untethered. Again, my brother and sister seemed to fit in fine. My sister became captain of the wrestling team. My brother was taking classes at Stanford by his sophomore year and even was taken by Steve Jobs's estranged daughter to the Sadie Hawkins school dance. I, on the other hand, was the smallest kid in my class by far. At lunch I'd hover with my tray, not knowing where to sit. But to be fair, the brutal social cage match of middle school is hard for everyone.

Friday nights I'd sit in my room building model airplanes, the oldies station crackling in the background or watching *Dances with Wolves* and wishing I could disappear into the ancient world the way Kevin Costner's character had. I devoured survival books like *Hatchet* and that's when I convinced a hunting-obsessed neighbor to bring me deer hides so I could tan them the way early humans had.

He eventually showed up with a bloodied garbage bag that weighed over fifty pounds, and stank of death. I couldn't have been happier, my mother not so much. Unfortunately, I skipped the crucial step of cleaning the remaining flesh before pouring the entirety of our kitchen's salt tin over it. For weeks our shed reeked like a coyote's den. There probably weren't too many other kids in Silicon Valley with a backyard full of rotting deer pinned up, but I was committed.

The world outside was inventing itself at an extraordinary pace and sometimes it felt too fast to hold on. Things were constantly appearing, like the first search engines, chatrooms, and a brief era of pagers in everyone's pocket, before cell phones. But I was always drawn to find things that felt more grounded than the technology bubbles rising around me.

I found another ragtag Scout troop of oddballs like me, overseen by two Harley-riding dads who were welders at the National Labs. One had "love" and "hate" tattooed across his knuckles. The other was simply named Gordo, although he was more jolly than plump. With them I returned to the Sierra Nevadas and discovered fly fishing, something that would become a sanctuary for me in years to come.

In the beginning, I caught nothing. Gordo would teach me knots and the names of flies, *the Royal Coachman, the olive-winged midge*. I'd cast my line and watch a tiny speck of intricately assembled fur and feathers drift downstream, reel it back in, cast again, nothing. The currents were a mystery. But each trip I brought my rod.

"You have to think like the fish," Gordo said. "Be in the water with it in your mind."

It went on like that for a year or more.

Then one day everything shifted. The river stopped being a surface. I could see the world under it. The shadows that had been rocks became fish, holding in the slack water behind them. The eddies became pockets where they slipped back and forth, waiting on whatever the current carried down. Nothing about the river had changed. How I saw it had.

I cast and waited, mending my line to let the drift run free. A stillness, a pause in the air like the space between my mother's notes. Then in a swirl the rod came alive with the feeling of thunder and the line snapped tight, the water electric in my hands.

I was as hooked as the fish.

My father had shown me how to look for patterns that could explain the rest. My mother had shown me how to feel the invisible. Standing in the cold water with a rod in my hands, I was doing both at once.

On long hikes, whenever we made camp, I'd find the nearest stream and catch trout to feed my friends. Scrambling over slippery rocks in icy water I found myself, learning how to be in nature, or maybe I should say re-learning.

By sixteen I was an Eagle Scout organizing hundred-mile backpacking trips with Garth, one of my closest friends and the "mule" of the troop. We traversed granite passes and epic valleys, navigating by topo maps and early satellite imagery, and carrying everything we needed on our backs. The stone spires rose up on all sides around us, cathedrals like the ones I had seen years before throughout Europe. Once we even resorted to eating a rattlesnake when a bear got into our rations in the night, despite having hung them in trees. We had only what we carried and left no trace. The wilderness was the one place where everything felt right, neither ancient nor modern.

---

As I approached young adulthood, everyone began going in their own directions.

Jane had left first. The week before she went, she took me on a long hike above the red clay roofs of Palo Alto, just the two of us. I remember sitting on a hill overlooking everything as she told me things were going to change, but it was going to be okay. I believed her, because she was Jane. Then Ed. Suddenly, their rooms went quiet one at a time, doors left open to hallways where nobody walked. The house felt like a ghost of itself. In their absence, the huge role they had played in my life came into painful clarity.

My parents' marriage also seemed to unravel in the quiet. Whatever had been holding them together, the moves, the adventures, the five of us crammed into motel rooms, had run out of road. The fights grew louder, the silences between them grew longer, all while I watched as the last kid in a home that was fading away.

Somewhere in my last year of high school, it became real. The fight reached a new amplitude and my father walked out. As he did, my mother, lost for words, sat down at her piano and played Chopin's Nocturne Op. 9 No. 2, like a musician on a sinking ship.

My father and I didn't speak again for nearly ten years.

That's how my childhood really ended, with fracture. The ground had shifted yet again, not by the rumbles of the earth's crust or by the geopolitics that seemed to constantly rewrite the maps. Just my mother's piano, my father walking out the door, and then silence.

Through all the uncertainty, the wandering in pursuit of the stars, and the literal shifting ground, we had been okay because we were together. Now what? I grew up in the West with a face that looked like the East. I grew up in modernity and yearned for the ancient. When my father walked out, the fracture exposed a hole that all our adventures had been covering. Where did I belong?
